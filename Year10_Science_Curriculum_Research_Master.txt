AC9S10U01 — HEREDITY, CELL DIVISION AND MENDELIAN INHERITANCE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain the role of meiosis and mitosis and the function of chromosomes, DNA and genes in heredity and predict patterns of Mendelian inheritance.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students need a connected chromosome-to-trait model, not isolated vocabulary. Mitosis explains genetically similar daughter cells used in growth and repair. Meiosis produces haploid gametes, introduces variation through chromosome assortment and crossing over, and halves chromosome number so fertilisation restores the diploid number. DNA is the hereditary molecule; genes are DNA sequences at loci on chromosomes; alleles are alternative forms of a gene. Year 10 prediction should use monohybrid Mendelian crosses, genotype/phenotype, dominance/recessiveness, probability and family/pedigree evidence. Do not imply that all traits are single-gene or perfectly dominant/recessive; acknowledge limits of simple Mendelian models.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: descriptor and elaborations establish the required links among mitosis, meiosis, chromosomes, DNA, genes, heredity and Mendelian prediction.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: F.1 Introduction to cell division; F.2 The cell cycle; F.3 Describe phases of mitosis and the cell cycle; F.4 Identify phases of mitosis in plant cells; G.1 Structure and function of nucleic acids; G.2 The genetic code; I.1–I.2 Sexual reproduction and genetic variation; J.1 genotype and phenotype; J.2 dominant and recessive; J.3 Punnett squares: Mendelian inheritance.
- IXL live practice, J.3: a chicken leg-colour prompt states yellow allele l is recessive to white allele L and asks the learner to complete all four genotype cells in a Punnett square. This confirms progression from allele notation and dominance to systematic gamete combination, then genotype/phenotype probability.
- Khan Academy authenticated: Comparing mitosis and meiosis, plus linked meiosis I/II, gamete/haploid/diploid and fertilisation instruction. Useful sequence: compare purpose, chromosome number, number/genetic similarity of products, then connect meiosis and fertilisation to heredity.
- NHGRI genetics resources: genes are units of heredity carried in DNA on chromosomes; meiosis creates reproductive cells with one chromosome set. https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary
- OpenStax Biology 2e, cell reproduction and Mendelian genetics: chromosome behaviour explains segregation and independent assortment; Punnett squares express expected probabilities rather than guaranteed family outcomes. https://openstax.org/details/books/biology-2e
- ABC Education: no stable, tightly matched Year 10 interactive sequence was required for this code; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED. Public-source coverage remains sufficient.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Chromosomes are organised DNA-protein structures; a gene is a DNA segment at a locus; alleles are gene variants.
2. DNA replication occurs before division. Mitosis separates duplicated chromosomes once and usually produces two genetically similar diploid cells.
3. Meiosis has two divisions after one DNA replication and produces four genetically varied haploid cells.
4. Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I; sister chromatids separate in meiosis II. Crossing over and independent assortment add variation.
5. Fertilisation combines haploid gametes and restores diploid chromosome number.
6. A genotype records alleles; phenotype is an observable outcome influenced by genotype and often environment.
7. In a complete-dominance monohybrid cross, each parent contributes one allele. A Punnett square enumerates equally likely fertilisation combinations.
8. Ratios are probabilistic expectations over many offspring, not a required pattern in every small family.
9. A test cross or pedigree can constrain possible genotypes, but evidence may not always determine one genotype.
10. Simple Mendelian models have limits: incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, polygenic traits and environmental effects require other models.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Sort mitosis versus meiosis by purpose, cell type, division count, products, ploidy and genetic similarity.
- Sequence cell-cycle/meiosis events and identify which chromosome structures separate.
- Translate DNA–gene–chromosome statements and reject part/whole reversals.
- Infer parent gametes, fill Punnett squares, calculate genotype and phenotype probabilities, and reverse-reason from offspring.
- Common traps: meiosis makes identical cells; DNA replication happens between meiosis I and II; dominant means common/stronger/better; recessive alleles disappear; a 3:1 ratio guarantees four offspring; phenotype always reveals genotype.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–8 chromosome/DNA/gene/allele relationships
- 9–16 mitosis purpose, stages and outcomes
- 17–24 meiosis, ploidy, variation and fertilisation
- 25–32 genotype, phenotype, dominance and gametes
- 33–42 monohybrid Punnett-square prediction and probability
- 43–48 pedigree/test-cross interpretation and limits of Mendelian models
Test questions — exactly 16, independently worded and not recycled:
- 1–3 molecular/chromosomal heredity

Feedback must name the reasoning error, show allele contributions explicitly, and distinguish expected probability from observed frequency.

AC9S10U02 — EVOLUTION BY NATURAL SELECTION AND SUPPORTING EVIDENCE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Use the theory of evolution by natural selection to explain past and present diversity and analyse the scientific evidence supporting the theory.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must use the causal mechanism: heritable variation exists; environmental conditions create differential survival or reproduction; allele/trait frequencies change across generations. Individuals do not evolve during their lifetimes and organisms do not acquire needed traits because they try. Evidence analysis should combine fossil succession/transitions, comparative anatomy and embryology, biogeography, molecular sequence similarity and directly observed change. Evidence supports common ancestry and evolutionary mechanisms through converging, testable patterns; one isolated example is not the whole case.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: requires both explanation by natural selection and analysis of evidence for past and present diversity.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: K.1 Introduction to natural selection; K.2 effects of gene mutations on organisms; K.3 evaluate fossil evidence of evolution; K.4 evaluate anatomical and molecular evidence; K.5 construct explanations of natural selection.
- IXL live practice, K.5: data show lactose-specific fermentation in a bacterial population rising from 26% to 82% after environmental change. Dropdown reasoning links increased frequency to higher survival/reproduction and resource availability. This is a strong authoring model because learners must connect evidence, fitness and population change rather than merely name natural selection.
- Khan Academy authenticated Evidence for evolution and Introduction to evolution/natural selection: fossil, anatomical, molecular and direct-observation evidence are presented as mutually reinforcing lines; shared derived features and sequence similarities support common ancestry.
- UC Berkeley Understanding Evolution: natural selection acts on existing heritable variation and changes populations over generations; evidence comes from multiple independent fields. https://evolution.berkeley.edu/
- Smithsonian Human Origins evidence resources: fossils, dating, genetics and comparative anatomy illustrate how claims about evolutionary history are tested. https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence
- OpenStax Biology 2e, evolution chapters: selection changes allele frequencies; mutation supplies new variation while selection is non-random with respect to reproductive success. https://openstax.org/details/books/biology-2e
- ABC Education: suitable evolution materials may supplement examples, but no single stable code-complete sequence was used; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Populations contain variation, some of which is heritable.
2. More offspring may be produced than can survive and reproduce; environments create selection pressures.
3. Individuals with traits that increase reproductive success contribute proportionally more alleles to later generations.
4. Over generations, heritable trait/allele frequencies can change: the population evolves.
5. Fitness is context-dependent reproductive success, not strength, health or moral superiority.
6. Mutation is a source of new alleles; recombination reshuffles alleles. Mutations arise without regard to what an organism needs.
7. Selection, genetic drift and gene flow can all change populations, but the descriptor’s explanatory centre is natural selection.
8. Fossil position, age and morphology show succession, extinction and transitional combinations.
9. Homologous structures and developmental patterns support common ancestry; analogous structures mainly show convergent adaptation.
10. DNA/protein sequence similarities permit quantitative comparison and phylogenetic inference.
11. Biogeographic distributions match descent, isolation and geological history.
12. Direct observations such as resistance demonstrate evolution on measurable timescales.
13. Strong analysis asks whether a predicted pattern occurs, considers alternative explanations, and synthesises independent evidence.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Read before/after graphs and complete a causal natural-selection explanation.
- Decide whether a mutation changes protein/function and whether the change is heritable.
- Evaluate which fossil layer/dated specimen supports a proposed sequence.
- Distinguish homologous from analogous structures and compare DNA sequence differences.
- Common traps: individuals evolve; organisms mutate because they need to; selection gives organisms traits; all mutations are harmful; “fittest” means strongest; gaps in fossils mean no evidence; similarity alone proves direct descent from a living species.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–8 variation, heredity, mutation and selection pressure
- 9–18 causal natural-selection explanations from scenarios/data
- 19–26 fossil evidence, dating and transitional patterns
- 27–34 comparative anatomy, embryology and biogeography
- 35–42 molecular evidence and common ancestry
- 43–48 synthesis, claim-evidence-reasoning and limitations
Test questions — exactly 16, independent:
- 1–4 mechanism of natural selection in novel populations
- 5–7 mutation/variation and data interpretation
- 8–10 fossil evidence analysis
- 11–13 anatomical, biogeographic and molecular evidence
- 14–16 multi-source evaluation and misconception diagnosis
Feedback must explicitly identify variation, selection pressure, differential reproductive success and generational population change.

AC9S10U03 — BIG BANG MODEL AND EVIDENCE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Describe how the big bang theory models the origin and evolution of the universe and analyse the supporting evidence for the theory.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Teach the Big Bang as the scientific model that the observable universe evolved from an extremely hot, dense early state as space expanded and cooled. It is not an explosion of matter from one point into pre-existing empty space. The evidence set must include galaxy redshift/expansion, cosmic microwave background radiation and primordial light-element abundances; large-scale structure and cosmic age consistency can extend analysis. Students should connect each observation to a model prediction and distinguish observation, inference, model and unresolved question.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: the code requires description of origin/evolution modelling plus analysis—not recall—of supporting evidence.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: JJ.1 Structure of the universe and JJ.2 Properties and classifications of stars. No dedicated IXL Year 10 Big Bang/evidence skill was visible: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for the descriptor’s central evidence analysis.
- IXL live practice, JJ.1: students select true statements about the universe as all space, its contents and relative age. It establishes scale/age vocabulary but is below the full curriculum demand; authoring must add expansion, cooling and evidence reasoning.
- Khan Academy authenticated Cosmology and astronomy, Big bang and expansion lesson: Big bang introduction, expansion and Cosmic background radiation provide a coherent conceptual sequence. The CMB resource supports treating relic radiation as evidence of a formerly hot universe.
- NASA Science universe resources: observations of expansion and the cosmic microwave background underpin the modern hot Big Bang model; WMAP/COBE/Planck measurements constrain age, composition and early fluctuations. https://science.nasa.gov/universe/
- ESA Planck: precision maps of the cosmic microwave background show early density variations and provide tests of cosmological parameters. https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Planck
- OpenStax Astronomy 2e, cosmology: Hubble–Lemaître expansion, background radiation and predicted light-element abundances are independent, converging evidence. https://openstax.org/details/books/astronomy-2e
- ABC Education: public Big Bang explainers can supply Australian classroom context, but no stable complete evidence-analysis mapping was relied on; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. The model describes an expanding universe that was hotter and denser in the past.
2. Expansion means distances between sufficiently separated galaxies increase as space expands; galaxies are not all flying from a central point inside space.
3. As expansion proceeds, average temperature and density decrease.
4. Early high temperatures allowed particles and nuclei; later cooling allowed neutral atoms, releasing photons that now form the CMB.
5. Gravity amplified early density differences into stars, galaxies and large-scale structure over billions of years.
6. Distant-galaxy spectra are generally redshifted; redshift increasing with distance supports systematic cosmic expansion.
7. A hot early universe predicted relic thermal radiation before the CMB was detected. Its near-uniform microwave spectrum and small anisotropies strongly support the model.
8. Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts approximate primordial abundances of hydrogen, helium and some lithium; observations broadly match.
9. Independent evidence is stronger because expansion, CMB and abundances measure different consequences of the same model.
10. Evidence can refine model parameters; it does not establish what caused the initial state or describe “before” the model’s domain.
11. Scientific theory means a well-supported explanatory framework, not a guess.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Order universe stages using temperature, density and time.
- Interpret simplified redshift/distance data and infer expansion.
- Match evidence to prediction: redshift→expansion; CMB→hot dense early state; light elements→early nucleosynthesis.
- Compare competing claims and identify which evidence discriminates between them.
- Common traps: one galaxy’s Doppler shift alone proves the Big Bang; Big Bang had a centre in space; the solar system formed at the Big Bang; CMB comes from stars; theory means speculation; the model explains an absolute beginning or cause.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–8 universe scale, age and model language
- 9–16 expansion, redshift and distance data
- 17–24 hot dense state, cooling and chronological sequence
- 25–32 cosmic microwave background prediction and observations
- 33–38 primordial light-element abundance
- 39–44 large-scale structure/age consistency and converging evidence
- 45–48 model scope, uncertainty and claim evaluation
Test questions — exactly 16, independent:
- 1–3 describe model and sequence
- 4–7 analyse unfamiliar redshift data
- 8–10 analyse CMB evidence
- 11–12 analyse abundance evidence
- 13–16 synthesise evidence, compare claims and define model limits
Feedback must link every observation to the relevant prediction and must correct explosion/centre misconceptions.

AC9S10U04 — EARTH-SYSTEM ENERGY FLOW AND GLOBAL CLIMATE CHANGE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Use models of energy flow between the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere to explain patterns of global climate change.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
The core is an interacting Earth-system energy model. Students trace incoming short-wave solar radiation, reflection, absorption, transfers among land/ocean/living systems/air and outgoing long-wave radiation. They use this model to explain climate patterns and change through greenhouse forcing, feedbacks, ocean heat uptake and circulation, albedo, carbon exchange and aerosols. Climate is a long-term statistical pattern; a local weather event alone neither proves nor disproves global change. Matter cycles may alter energy flow, but energy flows and is eventually radiated to space.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: explicitly requires models across all four spheres and explanation of patterns of global climate change.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: L.1–L.2 carbon cycle through biosphere/geosphere; HH water cycle; II.1 use data to describe climates; II.2 greenhouse effect; II.3–II.6 factors affecting climate. Photosynthesis and cellular-respiration skills support biosphere–atmosphere carbon/energy links.
- IXL live practice, II.2: an atmosphere prompt asks learners to select gases/resources and stable-temperature functions organisms rely on. The skill links atmospheric gases, living organisms and insulation; later authoring must extend this to radiative balance, enhanced greenhouse forcing and quantitative climate patterns.
- Khan Academy authenticated The Sun’s energy and Earth’s weather, redirected to Global winds and currents: uneven solar heating drives atmospheric and ocean circulation and supplies a precursor model for hydrosphere–atmosphere energy redistribution.
- NASA Earth energy budget: climate depends on balance between absorbed solar energy and outgoing infrared energy; greenhouse gases reduce the rate of energy loss and create an energy imbalance until the system warms. https://science.nasa.gov/earth/climate-change/
- IPCC AR6 Working Group I: human influence has warmed atmosphere, ocean and land; assessed evidence integrates observations, physical understanding and climate models. https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/
- Bureau of Meteorology and CSIRO, State of the Climate: Australian and global observations document warming, ocean heat, sea-level and extreme-event patterns. https://www.csiro.au/en/research/environmental-impacts/climate-change/state-of-the-climate
- OpenStax Environmental Science: carbon cycle, greenhouse effect, albedo and ocean/atmosphere interactions support a systems treatment. https://openstax.org/details/books/environmental-science
- ABC Education: climate resources can provide cases, but no single stable resource was treated as code-complete; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. The atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere exchange energy and matter continuously.
2. Solar short-wave energy is partly reflected by clouds, aerosols, ice and bright surfaces and partly absorbed by atmosphere, land and ocean.
3. Warm surfaces emit long-wave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some infrared, slowing energy loss to space.
4. Increasing greenhouse-gas concentrations create positive radiative forcing/energy imbalance; warming continues until outgoing energy again balances incoming energy at a higher temperature.
5. Oceans store and transport most excess heat because of their large heat capacity; currents redistribute energy and delay some surface response.
6. Land/ocean warming affects evaporation, atmospheric water vapour, precipitation and circulation.
7. Ice/snow loss reduces albedo and increases absorption, a positive feedback.
8. Water vapour is largely a feedback to initial warming; carbon dioxide is a major long-lived forcing under current human-driven change.
9. Biosphere and soils exchange carbon with the atmosphere through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, fire and land-use change.
10. Geosphere processes include long-term carbon storage, weathering and volcanic emissions/aerosols; volcanic aerosols can cause short-term cooling.
11. Climate models conserve energy, represent transfers and test how forcings/feedbacks generate spatial and temporal patterns.
12. Evidence should use multi-decade trends, multiple variables and global/regional datasets; weather variability remains superimposed on climate trends.
13. Correlation alone is insufficient, but attribution is strengthened by physical mechanism, characteristic patterns and model/observation agreement.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Label energy-budget diagrams and classify reflection, absorption, convection, latent heat and infrared emission.
- Trace a perturbation through multiple spheres and predict reinforcing or balancing effects.
- Interpret anomaly graphs, maps, ocean-heat or ice/albedo data.
- Compare model runs with natural-only versus natural-plus-anthropogenic forcings.
- Common traps: greenhouse effect is ozone depletion; gases trap heat permanently; energy cycles like carbon; the ocean is not warming if air fluctuates; one cold day disproves warming; all feedbacks cause warming; CO2 is the only climate influence; volcanoes explain the sustained modern trend.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–8 four-sphere components, reservoirs and interactions
- 9–18 planetary energy budget and transfer processes
- 19–26 greenhouse effect, forcing and radiative balance
- 27–34 ocean circulation/heat uptake and water-cycle links
- 35–40 albedo, water-vapour, carbon-cycle and aerosol feedbacks
- 41–46 interpret climate trends/maps/model comparisons
- 47–48 evaluate system-model assumptions and weather/climate claims
Test questions — exactly 16, independent:
- 1–3 trace energy across spheres
- 4–6 energy-budget/greenhouse reasoning
- 7–9 ocean, ice and biosphere feedbacks
- 10–13 analyse unfamiliar climate datasets/models
- 14–16 evaluate causal explanations, limitations and misconceptions
Feedback must trace the causal chain through named spheres, distinguish forcing from feedback, and separate short-term variability from long-term trend.

AC9S10U05 — NEWTON’S LAWS, FORCE, MASS AND ACCELERATION
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Investigate Newton’s laws of motion and quantitatively analyse the relationship between force, mass and acceleration of objects.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must connect all three laws to evidence from motion investigations and use vector net force in quantitative analysis. Newton’s first law concerns zero resultant force and constant velocity, not merely stationary objects. For constant mass, Newton’s second law is ΣF = ma; acceleration has the direction of net force and depends directly on net force and inversely on mass. Newton’s third-law forces are equal and opposite, act simultaneously on different objects and therefore do not cancel on one object’s free-body diagram. Authoring should include force diagrams, units, signs/directions, friction, normal force, weight and experimental graphs, while avoiding advanced momentum calculus.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: the descriptor combines investigation of the three laws with quantitative analysis of force, mass and acceleration.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: Y.1 Distance and displacement in one dimension; Y.2 Solve problems using kinematic equations; Z.1 Newton’s laws of motion; Z.2 Newton’s first law; Z.3 Newton’s second law; and AA.1 Conservation of mechanical energy as a related extension.
- IXL live practice, Z.3: a tug-of-war scenario gives acceleration 2.7 m s⁻² and mass 35 kg and asks for net-force magnitude in newtons, rounded to the nearest tenth. This confirms direct F = ma calculation, unit interpretation and identifying net—not merely applied—force.
- Khan Academy checked sequentially in the authenticated Khan tab: Newton’s second law of motion states F = ma, with acceleration increasing with net force and decreasing with mass. The broader forces course supports free-body diagrams and all three laws.
- OpenStax Physics, dynamics chapters: inertia, vector force addition, free-body diagrams and ΣF = ma provide the standard quantitative model; third-law pairs act on different systems. https://openstax.org/details/books/physics
- The Physics Classroom, Newton’s Laws tutorials: worked force diagrams and misconception-focused explanations provide accessible public scaffolding. https://www.physicsclassroom.com/class/newtlaws
- ABC Education: relevant motion demonstrations may supplement contexts, but no stable resource covered the complete quantitative descriptor; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Motion changes only when the vector sum of external forces is non-zero.
2. Newton’s first law: an object remains at rest or at constant velocity unless acted on by a net external force; inertia increases with mass.
3. Net force is the vector sum of forces on the chosen object. Balanced forces mean ΣF = 0, so acceleration is zero, not necessarily velocity.
4. For constant mass, Newton’s second law is ΣF = ma. SI units are N, kg and m s⁻²; 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻².
5. At fixed mass, doubling net force doubles acceleration. At fixed net force, doubling mass halves acceleration.
6. Choose a positive direction, assign signs to force components, calculate net force, then solve and report direction.
7. Weight is gravitational force W = mg; mass is amount of matter/inertia and is not measured in newtons.
8. Normal force is a contact force perpendicular to a surface and is not always equal to weight.
9. Friction/drag oppose relative motion or attempted motion; they affect net force.
10. Newton’s third law: if A exerts a force on B, B simultaneously exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force of the same interaction type on A.
11. Third-law pairs do not cancel because they act on different objects. Forces that balance on one object are not third-law pairs.
12. In a force–acceleration investigation at constant mass, graphing a against F_net gives a straight line through the origin with gradient 1/m ideally.
13. At constant net force, acceleration varies inversely with mass; plotting a against 1/m should be linear.
14. Experimental limitations include friction variation, track tilt, timing/sensor resolution and treating pulling force as net force without subtracting resistance.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Identify laws from unfamiliar situations rather than keyword matching.
- Calculate F, m or a, including opposing forces and signed directions.
- Select or construct free-body diagrams before calculating net force.
- Interpret force–acceleration or acceleration–mass data and evaluate whether results support a law.
- Common traps: a moving object must have a forward force; balanced forces mean the object stops; action–reaction forces act on one object; normal force always equals weight; use applied force instead of net force; confuse speed with acceleration; ignore unit conversion or direction.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–6 force vocabulary, vectors, units and system choice
- 7–13 Newton’s first law and inertia
- 14–22 free-body diagrams, balanced/unbalanced forces and net force
- 23–34 F = ma calculations with one- and two-direction forces
- 35–40 Newton’s third-law interaction pairs
- 41–46 experimental graphs, proportional reasoning and sources of error
- 47–48 integrated unfamiliar applications
Test questions — exactly 16, independently worded:
- 1–3 first-law and net-force reasoning
- 4–8 multi-step second-law calculations
- 9–11 free-body diagram and direction analysis
- 12–13 third-law pairs
- 14–16 investigation/data evaluation
Feedback must state the selected system, show signed net force, use correct units and identify why a distractor violates a law.

AC9S10U06 — ATOMIC STRUCTURE AND PERIODIC TABLE ORGANISATION
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain how the structure and properties of atoms relate to the organisation of the elements in the periodic table.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students should explain rather than merely locate. Atomic number/proton count defines an element and orders the table. Electron-shell/valence structure produces repeating chemical properties, so periods and groups reflect atomic structure. The scope includes protons, neutrons, electrons, isotopes, ions, electron arrangements for main-group elements and broad periodic patterns in metallic character/reactivity. Detailed quantum numbers, orbital hybridisation and exceptions among heavy transition elements are beyond the core Year 10 demand.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: requires a causal connection between atomic structure/properties and periodic organisation.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: N Introduction to the periodic table; O.1 Count subatomic particles in atoms and ions; O.2 Nuclear notation for atoms and ions; Q.1 Identify electron configuration of an atom or ion; R.1 Periodic trend—ionisation energy; S.1–S.2 chemical bonding as a property consequence.
- IXL live practice, Introduction to the periodic table: learners use the displayed table to match calcium, copper, carbon, chlorine and cobalt to Ca, Cu, C, Cl and Co. This establishes symbol/table fluency but does not by itself satisfy the explanatory code; authoring must extend to proton number, shells, groups and property prediction.
- Khan Academy checked sequentially: Introduction to the atom explains protons, neutrons and electrons and that atomic number—the proton count—defines the element; linked atomic-structure/periodic-table lessons support shell and group reasoning.
- Royal Society of Chemistry atomic-structure resources connect nucleus/electrons with periodic organisation and chemical behaviour. https://edu.rsc.org/resources/atomic-structure-video-clips-and-resources/1119.article
- IUPAC Periodic Table: authoritative element names, symbols, atomic numbers and table organisation. https://iupac.org/what-we-do/periodic-table-of-elements/
- OpenStax Chemistry 2e, electronic structure and periodic properties: recurring valence configurations explain group similarities and broad trends. https://openstax.org/details/books/chemistry-2e
- ABC Education: no single stable resource was used as a complete mapping; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Protons are positive and neutrons neutral in the nucleus; electrons are negative and occupy energy levels around the nucleus.
2. Atomic number Z equals proton number and uniquely identifies an element. The modern periodic table is ordered by increasing Z.
3. Mass number A = protons + neutrons; neutrons = A − Z.
4. Neutral atoms have electrons = protons. Ions form by electron loss/gain; nuclear identity normally remains unchanged.
5. Isotopes of one element have the same proton number but different neutron numbers; their chemistry is similar because electron structure is similar.
6. Periods are rows. For the first main-group periods, the period number indicates the highest occupied electron shell.
7. Groups are columns. Main-group elements in a group have similar outer-electron patterns and therefore similar bonding and reaction behaviour.
8. Group 1 atoms tend to lose one valence electron and form +1 ions; group 17 tend to gain one and form −1 ions; group 18 have stable full outer shells and low reactivity.
9. Across a period, proton number increases and valence electrons generally increase; properties change from metallic to non-metallic in broad patterns.
10. Down a group, additional electron shells increase atomic size and shielding; these structural changes help explain reactivity trends.
11. Metals generally lose electrons and conduct; non-metals more often gain/share electrons. These are trends, not definitions without exceptions.
12. Electron configuration is the bridge from atomic structure to periodic position and chemical property prediction.
13. Relative atomic mass is an isotope-weighted average and is not the same as an individual atom’s mass number.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Read atomic number, symbol and relative atomic mass from a table cell.
- Calculate proton, neutron and electron counts for atoms, isotopes and ions.
- Infer period/group or likely ion from electron arrangement and reverse the mapping.
- Compare elements in the same group and explain similarity using valence electrons.
- Predict broad reactivity/property patterns using shells, shielding and outer electrons.
- Common traps: atomic number equals neutrons or mass number; ions form by changing protons; isotopes are different elements; group and period reversed; same period means same properties; all values in table cells are whole-number mass numbers; noble gases have no electrons.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Practice questions — exactly 48:
- 1–7 subatomic particles and element identity
- 8–15 atomic/nuclear notation, isotopes and ions

AC9S10U07 — REACTION PATTERNS AND FACTORS AFFECTING RATE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Identify patterns in synthesis, decomposition and displacement reactions and investigate the factors that affect reaction rates.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Two linked outcomes are required. Students classify and generalise synthesis, decomposition and single/double displacement patterns using word, formula and particle representations while conserving atoms. They then investigate rate effects of temperature, concentration/pressure, surface area, catalysts and reactant identity using collision theory. Rate means change in reactant/product quantity per time. A catalyst provides an alternative lower-activation-energy pathway and is regenerated; it does not change the reaction’s overall products or equilibrium yield in the simple model.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: explicitly names synthesis, decomposition and displacement patterns and requires investigation of rate factors.
- IXL Australia Year 10 authenticated mapping: U.1–U.3 atom counting and balancing equations; V.1 Classify chemical reactions; V.2 Single replacement reactions; W.1 Factors that affect reaction rate. Formula/nomenclature and bonding skills provide prerequisites.
- IXL live practice, W.1: silver nitrate reacts with equal volumes of 3.40 and 7.90 mol L⁻¹ sodium chloride. Learners complete a causal chain: higher concentration → more frequent reactant-particle collisions → increased reaction rate. This confirms that questions require mechanism, not just memorised direction of change.
- Khan Academy authenticated High school chemistry Reaction rates lesson: Collision theory, Factors affecting reaction rates and linked understand/apply exercises. Productive progression is collision requirement → activation energy/orientation → explain each manipulated factor.
- Royal Society of Chemistry education resources provide practical rate investigations and reaction-pattern demonstrations. https://edu.rsc.org/resources
- OpenStax Chemistry 2e: stoichiometric equations conserve atoms; kinetic molecular reasoning explains concentration, temperature, surface area and catalysts. https://openstax.org/details/books/chemistry-2e
- Chemguide reaction-rate explanations supply accessible collision-theory examples and catalyst-energy profiles. https://www.chemguide.co.uk/physical/basicratesmenu.html
- ABC Education: possible reaction demonstrations are supplementary; no stable code-complete resource was required; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Chemical equations represent rearrangement of atoms; coefficients change amounts, while subscripts must not be altered when balancing.
2. Synthesis pattern: two or more reactants form one principal product, A + B → AB.
3. Decomposition pattern: one reactant forms two or more products, AB → A + B; energy input is often required.
4. Single displacement: a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element in a compound, A + BC → AC + B. Reactivity determines whether reaction occurs.
5. Double displacement: ions exchange partners, AB + CD → AD + CB, often driven by precipitate, gas or water formation; although the descriptor says displacement broadly, this is useful controlled extension.
6. Classification should rely on reactant/product structure, not surface clues such as bubbles alone.
7. Reaction rate can be measured from loss of mass, gas volume, colour/opacity, concentration or product amount per unit time.
8. Collision theory: reactant particles must collide with sufficient energy and suitable orientation for reaction.
9. Higher concentration or gas pressure increases collision frequency per volume.
10. Greater surface area of a solid exposes more particles, increasing collision frequency.
11. Higher temperature increases collision frequency and, more importantly, the fraction of collisions above activation energy.
12. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated.
13. Rate graphs: initial gradient gives early rate; plateau indicates no further measured change, not necessarily “particles stopped moving”.
14. Fair tests vary one factor, control others, repeat measurements and use suitable time/rate data.
15. Hazards increase with temperature, concentration, pressure and fine powders; risk controls must be proportionate.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Classify equations from formula patterns and complete missing reactant/product categories.
- Use atom counts and coefficients to verify conservation.
- Predict single-displacement outcomes from a reactivity series.
- Interpret rate graphs or experimental comparisons and explain using collision frequency/energy.
- Design or critique fair investigations of temperature, concentration, surface area or catalyst.
- Common traps: coefficients change formulas; all two-reactant reactions are synthesis; any element displaces any other; a catalyst is consumed or increases product amount; more collisions always means more successful collisions; crushing changes concentration; temperature only increases collision number, not collision energy.

AC9S10H01 — VALIDATION, PUBLICATION, PEER REVIEW AND REFINEMENT
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain how scientific knowledge is validated and refined, including the role of publication and peer review.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students should treat validation as an ongoing, distributed process rather than a single approval stamp. Publication makes claims, evidence and methods open to scrutiny. Peer review checks relevance, reasoning, methods and communication before publication but cannot guarantee truth or detect every problem. Replication/reproducibility, new instruments, larger datasets, alternative analyses and converging evidence can confirm, qualify or overturn claims. Refinement is a strength of science. Questions should distinguish peer review, editorial decisions, replication and consensus, and include conflicts of interest, uncertainty and correction/retraction.

SOURCE EVIDENCE

- National Academies, Reproducibility and Replicability in Science: transparent methods/data and independent checks are central to assessing robustness; reproducibility and replication are related but distinct. https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25303/reproducibility-and-replicability-in-science

- ABC Education: no stable, comprehensive peer-review resource was used; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Scientific claims gain confidence through transparent evidence, logical reasoning, methodological quality and repeated critical testing.
2. Publication communicates methods, results, limitations and references so a wider community can inspect and build on the work.
3. Pre-publication peer reviewers evaluate whether methods and analyses support conclusions, identify weaknesses and recommend revision, acceptance or rejection.
4. Editors make publication decisions; peer reviewers do not certify a claim as permanently true.
5. Peer review can miss errors, bias, fraud or weak design and varies across journals and disciplines.
6. Replication collects new data using comparable methods to test whether a finding recurs. Reproducibility often means obtaining consistent results from the same data/method or clearly specified procedures; terminology varies.
7. Independent confirmation is stronger than repeated work by one team using the same assumptions.
8. Converging evidence from different methods reduces dependence on one measurement or model.
9. Larger/more representative samples and improved instruments may narrow uncertainty or reveal earlier limitations.
10. Scientific knowledge is refined when new evidence changes estimates, boundary conditions, mechanisms or explanations.
11. A correction repairs part of the record; a retraction warns that a paper’s findings are unreliable. Retraction does not mean science as a whole failed.
12. Consensus is a judgement based on the weight of evidence, not a vote that replaces evidence.
13. Conflicts of interest, publication bias and selective reporting can affect the record; disclosure, preregistration, data sharing and registered reports are safeguards.
14. Appropriate confidence language should match evidence quality and acknowledge remaining uncertainty.

- Compare two studies for transparency, sample quality, controls, replication and converging evidence.
- Decide what peer review can and cannot establish.
- Interpret a correction, retraction or conflicting replication without overgeneralising.
- Common traps: published means proven; peer reviewers repeat the whole experiment; one failed replication automatically proves fraud; consensus is majority opinion without evidence; changing explanations show unreliability; preprints have completed peer review; more citations alone validate a claim.

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Develop investigable questions, reasoned predictions and hypotheses to test relationships and develop explanatory models.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must move from an observation or broad topic to a feasible, measurable question about a relationship; propose a hypothesis that offers a tentative evidence-based explanation; derive a directional prediction that should occur if the hypothesis/model is useful; and use outcomes to develop or revise an explanatory model. AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. An investigable question can be answered using observable/measurable evidence with available time, equipment, participants and ethical permissions.
2. Experimental questions often use “How does [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] when [key controls] are held constant?”
3. Observational questions can test relationships when variables cannot ethically or practically be manipulated.
4. A hypothesis is a tentative explanatory statement grounded in prior knowledge, not a guess or a restated prediction.
5. A reasoned prediction states the expected observable outcome and why the hypothesis/model leads to it.
6. Variables require operational definitions: exactly how each is changed, measured or classified.
7. Feasibility includes suitable range, resolution, sample size, safety, ethics and available materials.
8. Testability/falsifiability means conceivable evidence could count against the claim.
9. A prediction can be unsupported while the broader hypothesis remains plausible if the method was invalid or auxiliary assumptions failed; evaluation is required.
10. Models represent selected components and relationships and deliberately simplify reality.
11. Evidence can support, contradict or reveal limits of a model; models are revised by changing mechanisms, variables, parameters or scope.
12. Alternative hypotheses may make the same prediction, so discriminating tests are more informative.
13. Correlation questions do not automatically establish cause.
14. Strong inquiry connects: observation → question → hypothesis/model → prediction → evidence → evaluation/revision.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Select feasible questions from a fixed materials list.
- Rewrite broad or value-laden questions into measurable relationship questions.
- Identify IV, DV and controls and write operational definitions.
- Distinguish question, hypothesis, prediction and model statement.
- Derive different predictions from competing models and choose a discriminating test.
- Common traps: any answerable question is experimentally investigable; hypothesis is an “if–then” prediction without explanation; prediction is certain; unmeasurable terms such as “better”; available equipment ignored; supportive evidence proves a model; models are exact copies.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Validity: the design isolates and measures the intended relationship well enough to answer the question.
2. Reproducibility: another investigator can follow the reported method and obtain compatible findings within uncertainty.
3. The IV is deliberately changed; the DV is measured; controlled variables are held constant or monitored.
4. A control condition provides a comparison baseline; repeated trials are not the same as a control.
5. Confounding variables change systematically with the IV and offer alternative explanations.
6. Random error creates unpredictable scatter; systematic error shifts results consistently; both require different responses.
7. Replication, randomisation, adequate sample size and standardised technique reduce vulnerability to chance and bias.
8. Methods require exact quantities, equipment, calibration, range, intervals, timing, sequence and data-recording plan.
9. A pilot study can reveal unsuitable ranges, ceiling/floor effects, hazards and procedural ambiguity.
10. Risk = likelihood × consequence conceptually. Controls should eliminate/substitute/isolate hazards before relying only on PPE.
11. Ethical planning includes consent, privacy, minimising harm, animal welfare, environmental impact and honest data handling.
12. Work on Country/Place must consider permissions, Traditional Custodians, cultural authority, access restrictions and handling/citation of heritage and artefacts.
13. “Do not touch” is not the only protocol; consultation may change the question, method, data ownership and communication.
14. A reproducible method must still be valid; repeating a biased design reproduces the bias.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Identify IV, DV, controls, control group and confounders in prose methods.
- Repair a flawed method by specifying quantities, repeats, randomisation and measurements.
- Match hazards to controls and judge residual risk.
- Evaluate ethical and Country/Place scenarios before data collection.
- Common traps: more detail guarantees validity; repeats remove systematic error; PPE is always the best control; control variable and control group are identical; permission from landowner alone covers cultural authority; reproducible means identical numerical results.

AC9S10I03 — PRECISE MEASUREMENT, USEFUL SAMPLE SIZE AND REPLICABLE DATA
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Select and use equipment to generate and record data with precision to obtain useful sample sizes and replicable data, using digital tools as appropriate.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must choose equipment and procedures by range, resolution, sensitivity and fitness for purpose; record units and appropriate precision; collect enough independent and repeated data to reveal variation; and use digital tools when they improve logging, consistency or scale. Precision is not accuracy. A large number of repeated readings from one biased or non-representative source does not create a useful sample.

SOURCE EVIDENCE

- Khan Academy: measurement/significant-figure material offers arithmetic support, but no one resource maps equipment choice, sample size and digital logging completely; PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods: measurement processes require calibration, repeatability/reproducibility checks and explicit treatment of variation. https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/
- Bureau International des Poids et Mesures, measurement uncertainty guidance: a result is interpreted with unit, uncertainty and traceability rather than an unsupported string of digits. https://www.bipm.org/en/committees/jc/jcgm/publications
- Australian Bureau of Statistics sample-design guidance: sample usefulness depends on representation, independence and design, not size alone. https://www.abs.gov.au/websitedbs/d3310114.nsf/home/basic+survey+design

- ABC Education: no stable code-complete measurement resource was used; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Range is the span an instrument can measure; resolution is the smallest displayed/marked increment; sensitivity is response to change.
2. Precision describes closeness of repeated values; accuracy/trueness describes closeness to an accepted value.
3. Calibration compares an instrument with a reference and can reveal systematic offset.
4. Record every measurement with a value, unit and precision justified by instrument resolution.
5. Significant figures prevent false precision but are not a complete uncertainty analysis.
6. Analogue scales require consistent eye position and estimation; digital displays can still be biased or poorly calibrated.
7. Select an instrument whose range includes expected values and whose resolution can distinguish meaningful changes.
8. Repeated measurements quantify short-term variation; independent replication across operators/equipment/occasions tests robustness.
9. Sample size must be sufficient for variation and the intended inference; biological samples must represent the target population.
10. Pseudoreplication occurs when repeated readings from one experimental unit are incorrectly treated as independent samples.
11. Digital sensors enable frequent automatic logging and reduce transcription/timing variation, but require calibration, sampling-rate choice and metadata.
12. Sampling too slowly can miss rapid change; sampling too fast can create redundant data without improving validity.
13. Missing values, saturated sensors and data-processing settings must be documented.
14. Raw data should be preserved; transformations/cleaning should be traceable.
15. Replicable data are compatible within expected uncertainty, not necessarily numerically identical.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Match instruments to expected range/resolution and justify the choice.
- Read analogue/digital scales and record correct units/significant figures.
- Diagnose calibration offset, random scatter, saturation and parallax.
- Distinguish repeats, independent samples and pseudoreplication.
- Choose digital sampling interval and metadata for a phenomenon.
- Common traps: more decimal places mean more accuracy; zeroing guarantees calibration; repeated readings increase biological sample size; average removes systematic error; digital sensors have no uncertainty; discard inconvenient data without a rule.

AC9S10I06 — VALIDITY, REPRODUCIBILITY, CLAIMS AND UNCERTAINTY
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
======================================================================

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Assess the validity and reproducibility of methods and evaluate the validity of conclusions and claims, including by identifying assumptions, conflicting evidence and areas of uncertainty.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must distinguish method validity from reproducibility and then judge whether conclusions are warranted by the evidence. Evaluation must identify assumptions, incompatible evidence and uncertainty—not merely list “errors”. Strong responses connect a limitation to its likely effect on data and claim, compare alternative explanations, and calibrate confidence. A reproducible method may still measure the wrong thing; a valid result may require replication before broad generalisation.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
- Australian Curriculum v9: the descriptor explicitly joins method assessment with evaluation of conclusions/claims, assumptions, conflicting evidence and uncertainty.
- IXL Australia Year 10 relevant mapping: GG.1 Evaluate claims about natural resource use—fossil fuels; GG.2 groundwater claims; K.3–K.4 evaluation of fossil, anatomical and molecular evidence; A inquiry variables/control skills. No single IXL skill spans method validity, reproducibility and uncertainty: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- IXL live practice, GG.1: learners inspect a sourced 1965–2015 global energy-use graph and choose the statement actually supported (“increased most years”) rather than a contradictory constant-use claim. This confirms evidence-bounded claim selection, source attribution and trend reading.
- Khan Academy: argument/evidence resources provide claim–evidence reasoning prerequisites, but no direct science sequence covers validity, reproducibility, conflicting evidence and uncertainty together; PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- National Academies, Reproducibility and Replicability in Science: method transparency, computational reproducibility and independent replication are distinct contributors to confidence. https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25303/reproducibility-and-replicability-in-science
- NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook: assumptions, residuals, uncertainty and sensitivity checks are essential when evaluating model/data conclusions. https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/
- Australian Bureau of Statistics quality framework: relevance, accuracy, timeliness, accessibility, interpretability and coherence affect whether data support use. https://www.abs.gov.au/websitedbs/D3310114.nsf/home/Quality:+The+ABS+Data+Quality+Framework
- Committee on Publication Ethics guidance provides cases where conflicting evidence, corrections and undisclosed assumptions alter confidence. https://publicationethics.org/
- ABC Education: no stable code-complete evaluation sequence was relied on; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
1. Method validity asks whether the design measures/tests the intended question and controls plausible alternatives.
2. Reproducibility asks whether sufficiently documented analysis/procedures can produce compatible results; replication uses new data/conditions.
3. Reliability/precision alone does not establish validity: a miscalibrated instrument can give repeatable but biased values.
4. A conclusion must match the direction, magnitude, uncertainty, population, conditions and time span of the data.
5. Generalisation beyond the sampled population or tested range needs justification.
6. Assumptions may concern measurement calibration, sample independence, model form, constant conditions, representativeness or absence of confounding.
7. An unstated assumption is not automatically false; evaluation asks whether it is reasonable and how sensitive the conclusion is to it.
8. Conflicting evidence should be compared for method quality, independence, sample, context, uncertainty and compatibility—not counted as votes.
9. Apparent conflict may arise from different definitions, scales, populations or conditions.
10. Random uncertainty limits precision; systematic bias threatens accuracy; model uncertainty concerns whether important processes are omitted/misrepresented.
11. Error bars/intervals do not mean every value inside is equally likely and do not remove other uncertainty sources.
12. Correlation alone does not validate a causal conclusion; mechanism, temporal order and confounder controls matter.
13. Sensitivity analysis tests whether reasonable choices/assumptions change the conclusion.
14. A qualified claim can remain valid when limitations are explicit; “more research needed” should specify what evidence is missing.
15. Confidence language should range from unsupported/limited to well-supported, not simply true/false.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Decide whether data support, contradict or fail to address a claim.
- Compare two methods for validity and reproducibility and explain consequences.
- Identify the hidden assumption required for an inference.
- Reconcile conflicting datasets or identify a discriminating next study.
- Common traps: reproducible equals valid; any anomaly invalidates the study; largest sample always wins; peer-reviewed means certain; overlapping error bars prove no difference; correlation proves mechanism; uncertainty means nothing is known; limitations require total rejection.

4. A laboratory report prioritises reproducible method, results, analysis and limitations; a public explainer prioritises context and clear meaning but still links evidence.
5. Technical terms should be defined when needed, not simply removed; analogies require stated limits.
6. Headings, captions, tables, graphs, diagrams and callouts should serve comprehension and evidence.
7. Captions should state the main takeaway and include units/source; decorative graphics must not imply unsupported relationships.
8. Visual scales, axis choices and colour must not distort data; colour alone should not carry meaning.
9. Accessible digital texts require alt text, captions/transcripts, logical heading order, readable contrast and meaningful link text.
10. Source citation enables verification and distinguishes evidence from author interpretation.
11. Uncertainty should be communicated in understandable terms without false certainty or false balance.
12. Digital tools support editing, visualisation, collaboration and multimodal delivery but require checking templates, auto-generated summaries and data transformations.
13. Ethical communication avoids identifying participants, exaggerating risk, stigmatising groups or exposing restricted knowledge.
14. Cultural protocols can affect permission, attribution, images, terminology and distribution.
15. Evaluation asks whether the intended audience can accurately understand and use the message.

IXL/KHAN QUESTION PATTERNS AND TRAPS
- Choose the best format/features for a specified audience and purpose.
- Rewrite one scientific finding for specialist, student and public audiences without changing meaning.
- Repair misleading captions, graph scales, jargon and unsupported certainty.
- Audit a digital product for accessibility, source attribution and cultural permissions.
- Common traps: simplify by deleting uncertainty; add decorative complexity; use unexplained jargon; cite only at the end without linking claims; rely on colour alone; treat digital polish as accuracy; include restricted data because it is interesting.
