# AC9EFLA03 — Batch 1 question bank

## Practice questions

## Tier 1 — Knowledge and terms

### P01 — A text in the playground

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read the prompt and options aloud.

**Question:** Which item is a sign that gives readers a message in a playground?

A. A “Close the gate” notice

B. A skipping rope

C. A drinking bottle

D. A patch of grass

**Correct answer:** A — The notice uses words to communicate a message.

**Coverage:** Recognising an environmental sign as a text.

### P02 — A printed book

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read all options aloud.

**Question:** Which description best matches a printed storybook?

A. A bell that rings at lunchtime

B. Bound pages with words or pictures that tell a story

C. A box used to store pencils

D. A song heard without any words or pictures

**Correct answer:** B — A printed storybook is a text made from organised pages.

**Coverage:** Identifying a book as one form of text.

### P03 — A digital text

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read the examples aloud.

**Question:** Which is a digital text?

A. An empty lunchbox

B. A plain wooden block

C. A message displayed on a tablet

D. A ball rolling down a ramp

**Correct answer:** C — The tablet displays a message that can be read or viewed.

**Coverage:** Recognising a screen-based text.

### P04 — Sorting forms of text

**Type:** Matching

**Delivery:** Read each example and the labels **sign**, **book** and **digital text**.

**Question:** Match each example to its form: (a) an “Exit” label above a door, (b) a picture story with paper pages, (c) a weather page on a classroom screen.

**Marking key:** (a) sign, (b) book, (c) digital text. Award 1 mark per correct match; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Classifying three familiar text forms.

### P05 — Text or object?

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Show or name a cereal box that has a product name and picture.

**Question:** Is the writing and picture on the cereal box a text? Tell why.

**Marking key:** Yes (1 mark); explains that the words/picture communicate information, such as the cereal name or what is inside (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Recognising text embedded on an everyday object.

### P06 — One message, different forms

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “The message ‘Wash your hands’ could be on a paper poster or on a screen.”

**Question:** Are both versions texts? Explain your answer.

**Marking key:** Yes (1 mark), because both communicate the same message even though one is printed and one is digital (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Understanding that text form can change while a message remains.

## Tier 2 — Core skill practice

### P07 — Choosing a text for a quick warning

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read the situation and options aloud.

**Question:** People need to notice that a floor is wet as they walk past. Which text form is most useful?

A. A blank sheet of paper

B. A long storybook kept in a cupboard

C. A private message on a tablet in another room

D. A clear warning sign beside the wet area

**Correct answer:** D — A nearby sign can give the warning quickly.

**Coverage:** Connecting a sign with an immediate public purpose.

### P08 — Finding information in a book

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Present a simple animal information book or describe one.

**Question:** Name one kind of information a reader might learn from this book.

**Marking key:** A plausible detail such as what an animal eats, where it lives or what it looks like. Award 1 mark. The answer must be information that words or pictures could communicate.

**Coverage:** Identifying information carried by a book text.

### P09 — Features of a digital story

**Type:** Matching

**Delivery:** Read the features and labels **paper storybook** or **digital story**.

**Question:** Match the feature to the form where it is most likely: (a) swipe to the next screen, (b) turn a paper page, (c) tap a speaker button to hear narration, (d) use a ribbon bookmark.

**Marking key:** Digital story: (a), (c). Paper storybook: (b), (d). Award 1 mark each; 4 marks total.

**Coverage:** Distinguishing familiar affordances of print and digital texts.

### P10 — Reading a symbol and words together

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Describe a sign showing a bicycle picture crossed by a red line with the words “No bicycles”.

**Question:** What message does the sign give? Which parts help communicate it?

**Marking key:** Bicycles are not allowed (1 mark); identifies the picture/crossed line and/or the words as meaning-making parts (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Interpreting words and symbols in a sign.

### P11 — Naming a useful form

**Type:** Oral response

**Delivery:** Say, “Your class wants families to know the time of a concert.”

**Question:** Name one text form the class could use and tell where people would see it.

**Marking key:** A suitable pairing, such as a printed invitation sent home, a poster near the classroom or a digital notice on the school app. Award 1 mark for the form and 1 mark for a workable location; 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Selecting and locating a text for a communication need.

### P12 — Comparing two forms

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Show or describe a printed class timetable and the same timetable on a screen.

**Question:** Tell one way these texts are the same and one way they are different.

**Marking key:** Same: both give the schedule or contain the same information (1 mark). Different: paper versus screen, fixed versus scrollable/tappable, or another observable form difference (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Comparing message and medium across print and digital forms.

## Tier 3 — Guided application

### P13 — Selecting a bedtime text

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read all options aloud.

**Question:** A family wants to share a made-up adventure before sleep. Which item is most clearly suited to that purpose?

A. A picture storybook about a flying boat

B. A “Staff only” sign

C. A bus timetable

D. A label that says “Flour”

**Correct answer:** A — The storybook contains an imaginative text for shared reading.

**Coverage:** Matching a literary book form to a reading purpose.

### P14 — Turning a spoken reminder into text

**Type:** Drawing response

**Delivery:** Say, “The teacher says, ‘Remember your hat.’ Make a small sign that gives this message.” Provide paper and drawing tools.

**Question:** Create a sign that Foundation students could understand.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for communicating the hat reminder through words, a recognisable image or both; 1 mark for making the message easy to notice; 2 marks total. Conventional spelling is not required if the child explains the sign.

**Coverage:** Representing a spoken message as a visual sign.

### P15 — A screen message for one person

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read the scenario and options aloud.

**Question:** Aria’s uncle sends her family a photo and the words “I arrived safely.” What kind of text is this?

A. A road sign

B. A digital message

C. A printed recipe book

D. A wordless toy

**Correct answer:** B — The photo and words are sent and viewed digitally.

**Coverage:** Identifying a multimodal digital message.

### P16 — Information for many visitors

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “A garden closes at 5 o’clock. Many visitors need to know before entering.”

**Question:** Would a sign at the gate or one small book at the office work better? Choose and explain.

**Marking key:** Chooses the gate sign (1 mark) because it is visible at the relevant place and quickly informs many visitors (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Evaluating a text form for audience, place and speed.

### P17 — Retelling across forms

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Say, “You read a paper book about a lost duck. You want to share the story using a tablet.”

**Question:** Name two things you could put in the digital retelling and tell how they would help the audience.

**Marking key:** Any two suitable digital text elements with purpose, such as drawings/photos to show events, recorded voice to tell the story, typed labels, or ordered screens. Award 1 mark per suitable element and 1 mark for explaining how at least one supports meaning; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Transforming a story from print into a simple digital text.

### P18 — Reading an unfamiliar label

**Type:** Oral response

**Delivery:** Describe a storage tub with a picture of paintbrushes and the word “Brushes”.

**Question:** What is likely kept in the tub? How does the text help you know?

**Marking key:** Paintbrushes (1 mark); refers to the picture, word label or both (1 mark); 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Using image and word evidence in a functional label.

## Tier 4 — Problem solving and reasoning

### P19 — When one form is not enough

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Read the scenario aloud.

**Question:** A class puts a concert notice only inside a book that stays on the library shelf. Few families see it. Suggest a better text form and explain why it would reach the audience.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for a suitable form such as an invitation, poster, email/app notice or webpage; 1 mark for a suitable distribution/place; 1 mark for explaining that families can access or notice it; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Solving an audience-access problem by changing text form.

### P20 — Inferring purpose from form

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Describe “a large card beside a path with a short message, an arrow and a toilet symbol”. Then read the options.

**Question:** What is this text most likely designed to do?

A. Tell a long imaginary story

B. Record a private diary entry

C. Direct people to a facility

D. Teach a song verse by verse

**Correct answer:** C — Its short message, arrow and public location support directions.

**Coverage:** Inferring purpose from a sign’s design and placement.

### P21 — Same information, two audiences

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Say, “Foundation children and their families both need to know what to bring on an excursion.”

**Question:** Suggest one text for the children and one for the families. Explain one difference between the two.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for a child-accessible form such as a picture checklist; 1 mark for a family form such as a detailed printed/digital notice; 1 mark for a meaningful difference in detail, language, delivery or design based on audience; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Designing complementary text forms for different audiences.

### P22 — Is a picture always a text?

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “A tablet shows a red circle by itself. Later it shows the same red circle beside the words ‘Tap to stop’.”

**Question:** Which version gives the clearer text message? Explain.

**Marking key:** The second version (1 mark), because the words and/or button context explain what the red circle means (1 mark); 2 marks total. Accept a well-explained alternative if the child establishes a familiar stop-symbol context.

**Coverage:** Reasoning about how context and combined modes make meaning clear.

### P23 — Checking whether something communicates

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Present three examples orally: an empty sheet, a sheet with a drawn map and arrows, and a screen showing a written message.

**Question:** Which examples act as texts? Give a reason for each choice.

**Marking key:** Identifies the map and screen message as texts (1 mark each) because they communicate directions/information (1 mark). Recognises that the empty sheet does not yet carry a message (1 mark); 4 marks total.

**Coverage:** Applying the idea of text as purposeful communication across media.

### P24 — Recovering a missing message

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Say, “Rain has washed every word from a park sign, but its arrow still points left. Visitors are confused.”

**Question:** What information would you add to make the sign useful again? Explain how your addition works with the arrow.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for naming a destination or adding a clear symbol, such as “Playground”; 1 mark for stating that the arrow shows the direction; 1 mark for explaining that destination plus direction completes the message; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Reconstructing missing information in a multimodal sign.

## Exam questions

## Section A — Core knowledge

### E01 — Recognising a book text

**Type:** Multiple choice

**Delivery:** Read the options aloud.

**Question:** Which item is most clearly a book used to give factual information?

A. A clock showing nine o’clock

B. A stop sign beside a road

C. A blank drawing board

D. A paper book titled “How Frogs Grow”

**Correct answer:** D — The title signals an informational book.

**Coverage:** Identifying an informative book from title and form.

### E02 — Naming the form

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “A child taps a screen to read a class announcement.”

**Question:** What form of text is the announcement?

**Marking key:** A digital text, screen text or digital announcement. Award 1 mark.

**Coverage:** Naming a digital text from its mode of access.

### E03 — Classifying examples

**Type:** Matching

**Delivery:** Read the labels **sign**, **book**, **digital text** and the examples.

**Question:** Match: (a) recipe pages bound together, (b) “Keep off the grass” beside a lawn, (c) an interactive map on a phone.

**Marking key:** (a) book, (b) sign, (c) digital text. Award 1 mark each; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Independently classifying familiar text forms.

## Section B — Application and problem solving

### E04 — Choosing a form for directions

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “Visitors keep walking past the school office.”

**Question:** Choose a text form that could help them find it and describe what the text should include.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for an appropriate sign or map; 1 mark for useful content such as “Office”, an arrow or location image; 1 mark for placing it where visitors make the choice; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Designing a functional directional text.

### E05 — Comparing print and digital versions

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Describe a poem printed on a card and the same poem shown on a tablet with a play-audio button.

**Question:** Give one similarity and two differences between the versions.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for the shared poem/message; 1 mark for paper versus screen; 1 mark for a second valid difference such as audio, tapping, swiping, portability or page handling; 3 marks total.

**Coverage:** Comparing equivalent text content across two forms.

### E06 — Fixing an inaccessible notice

**Type:** Short response

**Delivery:** Say, “The only notice about sports day is on a classroom computer that families cannot use.”

**Question:** Suggest one change that would help families receive the message and explain why it works.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for a suitable alternative or additional form, such as a note sent home, email/app message or public webpage; 1 mark for explaining how families can access it; 2 marks total.

**Coverage:** Solving a distribution problem by changing text form.

## Section C — Extended response and analysis

### E07 — Planning three connected texts

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Say, “The class is holding a book swap. Children, families and visitors need different information.”

**Question:** Plan two different text forms that could advertise or explain the event. State what each would communicate, where it would appear and why that form is useful.

**Marking key:** For each of two forms, award 1 mark for an appropriate form and content and 1 mark for a logical location/audience link (4 marks). Award 1 further mark for explaining a useful contrast between the forms, such as a quick public poster versus a detailed digital notice; 5 marks total.

**Coverage:** Coordinating text form, content, placement and audience.

### E08 — Analysing a multimodal safety text

**Type:** Extended response

**Delivery:** Describe a pool sign with the words “Walk”, a picture of walking feet and a red crossed-out picture of a running child.

**Question:** Explain the message, how each part contributes, and why a sign is more suitable here than a storybook kept at reception.

**Marking key:** 1 mark for “walk/do not run”; 1 mark for explaining the word “Walk”; 1 mark for explaining one or both pictures; 1 mark for noting that the sign is visible and quick to understand at the place of risk; 1 mark for contrasting this with a less immediate storybook; 5 marks total.

**Coverage:** Analysing modes and evaluating form for a real-time safety purpose.
