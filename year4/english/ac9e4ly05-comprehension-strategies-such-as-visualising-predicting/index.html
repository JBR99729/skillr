AC9E4LY05 • Year 4 English

Comprehension strategies: build, link and check meaning

Use comprehension strategies when listening, viewing and reading to build literal and inferred meaning, expand content knowledge, integrate and link ideas, and understand texts.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
Learning goals

1. What students learn

Strong comprehension is active. Students notice what a text says, connect information across words, images and sound, infer what is implied, identify main and supporting ideas, judge whether evidence actually supports a claim, and repair understanding when something does not fit.

  • Connect modes: explain how print, images and sound support, extend or conflict with one another.
  • Find information: use purposeful key-word scanning and listening.
  • Build meaning: distinguish literal details, supported inferences, predictions, main ideas and summaries.
  • Evaluate support: identify a claim, its evidence and the reasoning that connects them.
  • Monitor: pause, reread, self-question, cross-check and revise an interpretation when new evidence disagrees.
Core reasoning routines

2. Five routines to teach explicitly

Clue → meaning → inference

Ask: What exact clue do I have? What does it suggest? What conclusion fits all the clues? A supported inference is more than a possible guess.

Details → common idea → main idea

A main idea must cover all important details. Reject an answer that is true but too narrow, too broad, off-topic or introduces new information.

Evidence → reasoning → claim

Evidence is not automatically strong. Ask whether it is relevant, sufficient and representative enough for the size of the claim.

Notice → pause → check → correct

When understanding breaks down, stop. Reread, ask a targeted question, cross-check another clue or source, then update the meaning.

Mode → contribution → combined meaning

Do not list “words, image, sound” separately. Explain what each contributes and how they work together.

Worked examples

3. Worked examples

Inference

Text: A bottle lid is loose, a book is damp and there is a puddle in a backpack.

Reasoning: Loose lid → water could escape. Damp book + puddle → water leaked inside. Inference: the bottle probably leaked. Do not add an unsupported cause such as “the bottle cracked on a bicycle”.

Prediction

Text: Two students compare an old garden photograph with a tree that still exists, then walk towards the tree.

Prediction: they will probably investigate the area near the tree. The prediction follows the characters’ current goal.

Main idea

Details: sunflower seeds can be eaten, made into a spread and pressed for oil. Main idea: sunflower seeds can be used in several foods and products. “Sunflower oil is used in baking” is too narrow.

Self-monitoring

Text: “Although the creek looked shallow near the bank, the centre channel was deeper than it appeared.” If a reader writes “the creek is shallow”, the word although and the second clause should trigger rereading and correction.

Curriculum coverage

4. ACARA elaborations taught here

  • E1: make connections between information in print, images and sound.
  • E2: read or listen for key words to identify specific information.
  • E3: interpret the main idea and supporting ideas.
  • E4: identify evidence and reasoning authors use to support points or arguments.
  • E5: apply self-monitoring and self-correction, including rereading, self-questioning, pausing, confirming and cross-checking.

The Practice bank also uses inference, prediction, summarising, connecting and visualising as comprehension processes within the content description.

Common misconceptions

5. Common mistakes

  • Possible = supported: a conclusion can be possible without being justified by the text.
  • One detail = main idea: a main idea must cover the important details together.
  • Opinion = evidence: a reason needs relevant support, not just confidence.
  • First interpretation is fixed: readers should revise when later information conflicts.
  • Modes are separate: multimodal comprehension requires linking what each mode contributes.
  • Scanning without a purpose: key words depend on what information the reader needs.
Teach and assess

6. Instructional sequence

  1. Model: think aloud using one exact clue at a time.
  2. Guide: ask students to point to evidence before answering.
  3. Contrast: compare the best answer with a plausible but unsupported distractor.
  4. Transfer: use a fresh print, listening or multimodal text.
  5. Reflect: ask which strategy repaired or deepened understanding.

Formative check: Give an unseen short text. Ask for one literal detail, one inference with evidence, the main idea, and one self-question the reader could use if confused.
