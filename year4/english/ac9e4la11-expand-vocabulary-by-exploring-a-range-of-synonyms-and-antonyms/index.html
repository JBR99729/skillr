Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to expand vocabulary by exploring a range of synonyms and antonyms, and using words encountered in a range of sources. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include context, language, vocabulary.

Learning target: expand vocabulary by exploring a range of synonyms and antonyms, and using words encountered in a range of sources

4-Step Instructional Sequence

4. 4-Step Instructional Sequence

Step 1: Explicit Teaching & Modelling

Define the outcome-specific metalanguage (context, language, vocabulary) and model how to identify the feature, select evidence and explain or use it.

Step 2: Guided Analysis

Work through a second example together: What is the choice or feature? Where is the evidence? What does it do here?

Step 3: Collaborative / Independent Practice

Students annotate, compare, read, edit, speak or compose with a fresh example, then apply the concept independently.

Step 4: Formative Assessment

Use an unseen micro-text, oral task or creation task. Require precise evidence plus a justified explanation or deliberate crafting choice.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Curriculum example 1

Use this context directly: building vocabulary from research about technical and learning area-specific topics; for example, gravity and magnetic forces. Ask students to identify the relevant English feature, point to evidence and explain what it contributes to meaning or communication.

Curriculum example 2

Use this context directly: determining or clarifying the shades of meaning of synonyms and antonyms. Ask students to identify the relevant English feature, point to evidence and explain what it contributes to meaning or communication.

Curriculum example 3

Use this context directly: using words encountered in texts that are formed from a First Nations Australian language; for example, Woomba Woomba or Toowoom was the place referred to by First Nations Australians on the Darling Downs and then the name was referred to as Toowoomba by the drovers (teaching context). Ask students to identify the relevant English feature, point to evidence and explain what it contributes to meaning or communication.

Use source-based cultural evidence

When a text, language name or cultural protocol is discussed, identify the source and describe what it supports without treating one example as representing all First Nations Peoples or Countries/Places.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

Worked example and reasoning checks

Choose a synonym that fits the context

Use synonyms and antonyms precisely rather than treating related words as interchangeable.

Worked example

Replace ‘small’ in ‘The scientist examined a small insect’ with ‘tiny’, ‘brief’ or ‘narrow’.

  1. Identify the meaning needed: the insect's overall size.
  2. ‘Tiny’ means very small. ‘Brief’ describes a short duration; ‘narrow’ describes a small width.
  3. Choose ‘tiny’ if the insect is very small. Read the revised sentence to check that its meaning still fits.

Watch for this mistake: Words with related meanings may differ in strength or fit different contexts. Check the whole sentence before substituting a synonym.

Try it, then explain

  1. Choose a suitable antonym for ‘generous’ when describing a person who will not share: noisy, selfish or cheerful.

    Hint

    Look for an opposite meaning about willingness to give.

    Answer and explanation

    Selfish. It contrasts with generous in this context.

  2. In ‘a brief visit’, can ‘brief’ be replaced with ‘tiny’ without changing how natural the sentence sounds? Explain.

    Hint

    What does brief describe here?

    Answer and explanation

    No. ‘Brief’ describes a short time; ‘tiny’ normally describes size.
