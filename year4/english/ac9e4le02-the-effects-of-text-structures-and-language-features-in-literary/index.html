Learning goals

Learning goals

Learning intention: Explain how a writer’s structure and language affect your reading, then share an opinion supported by the text.

Success criteria: I can identify a particular choice, point to its words or place in the story, describe its effect and explain my response. I can listen to another reader’s supported opinion even when it differs from mine.

Official content description: describe the effects of text structures and language features in literary texts when responding to and sharing opinions

Key concept: choice, evidence, effect, opinion

Key concept: choice, evidence, effect, opinion

A writer’s text structure is how events or parts are arranged. A flashback returns to an earlier event; tension is a feeling of uncertainty or worry about what may happen; a resolution settles a story’s main problem. An unresolved chapter ending may keep you curious.

Language features include descriptive noun groups, sensory details, repetition and figurative expressions. A noun group names someone or something and can add describing details. A simile makes a comparison, often using like or as; a metaphor describes one thing as another. Personification gives human qualities to something nonhuman. Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration.

Move from “I liked the story” to an opinion with a reason: “I found the ending reassuring because the rescue resolves the danger.” Naming a device alone does not explain its effect. Start with what the words do here; readers may respond differently.

E1: Explain an effect and share an opinion

E1: Explain an effect and share an opinion

Original poem: “Still we wait beside her door. / Still we wait and hope once more. / Soon our friend may feel well.”

Model: “I find the repeated ‘Still we wait’ caring because the friends keep hoping for their sick friend.” Another reader might focus on worry; ask which words support that response.

Checkpoint: Does repetition always mean impatience? Answer: No. Here friend, hope and feel well support concern and hope. In a late-bus poem with foot-stamping, the same phrase could suggest impatience.

E2: Use precise language about an image

E2: Use precise language about an image

Original extract: “A tiny, mud-spattered puppy shivered on the step. Its eyes were two pleading buttons.”

Model: “The noun group ‘a tiny, mud-spattered puppy’ helps me picture a small, muddy animal. The metaphor about its eyes makes its appeal feel hard to ignore, so I feel sympathetic.” Tiny suggests size; mud-spattered suggests appearance. The puppy’s feelings still need to be inferred from the whole extract.

Try together: Compare “the puppy” with the expanded noun group. Answer: The shorter version names the animal but loses the size and muddy detail.

E3: A flashback changes how we read the present

E3: A flashback changes how we read the present

Original extract: “Now Ada held the prize. A month before, her first clay bowl had collapsed. She tried again each afternoon.”

Model: “The flashback shows Ada’s earlier difficulty and practice. I find the prize satisfying because I understand the effort behind it.” The phrase a month before signals an earlier time. A flashback does not always create suspense; this one adds background and appreciation.

E3: Build tension, then resolve it

E3: Build tension, then resolve it

Original extract: “The ladder shook. Ella gripped its rail. Then the gardener held the ladder steady, and Ella stepped safely down.”

Model: “The shaking ladder and Ella’s grip create worry. The gardener’s help and Ella’s safe landing resolve the immediate problem, so I found the ending reassuring.” The short action sentences move quickly here. Short sentences can also be calm: “The lake rested. A bird called. I breathed slowly.”

More language choices and their effects

More language choices and their effects

  • Simile: “The little boat bobbed like a cork.” The comparison helps you picture the light boat rising and falling.
  • Personification: “Leaves whispered above the sleeping cat.” Whispered gives the movement a gentle sound that suits the quiet scene.
  • Hyperbole: “I waited a thousand years for the swing,” Sam groaned. The impossible wait expresses impatience.
  • Sensory language: “The sour lemon stung my tongue.” Taste and touch details help a reader imagine the experience.
  • Withheld information: “Inside the box was something nobody expected.” Leaving the object unnamed encourages curiosity.

You do not need to use every term in every response. Choose the one that accurately explains the passage.

4-Step Instructional Sequence

4-Step Instructional Sequence

  1. Explicit Teaching & Modelling: Read the puppy extract aloud. Underline the noun group, describe the picture it creates and model an opinion with because.
  2. Guided Analysis (We do): Read the Ada extract. Locate the time shift; compare what the reader knows before and after the flashback. Ask why the earlier failure affects appreciation of the prize.
  3. Collaborative / Independent Practice: Pairs read the waiting poem, each shares a response and asks for one supporting phrase. Independently write a response to the ladder extract.
  4. Formative Assessment: Use the exit ticket below. Look for a named or accurately described feature, specific evidence, an effect and an opinion. Reteach the missing link rather than require identical feelings.
Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

E1: sharing and discussing understanding of the effects of literary techniques on their appreciation of texts

Taught through the repeated waiting poem and partner exchange, including respectful differences in response.

E2: sharing responses to texts using appropriate language to talk specifically about grammar and literature; for example, “The use of the noun groups to describe the character really helps to create images for the reader.”

Taught through the expanded puppy noun group and precise feature–image–opinion model.

E3: using language appropriate to a text such as “flashback”, “tension” and “resolution” when sharing opinions about plot structure

Taught through Ada’s flashback and the ladder problem’s build-up and resolution.

Common Student Misconceptions

Common Student Misconceptions

  • “Naming the feature is enough.” Add the evidence and what it changes in your reading.
  • “All readers must have the same opinion.” Different responses can be valid when each fits the text.
  • “Short sentences always cause tension.” Consider the actions, words and setting too; calm breathing can use short sentences.
  • “An uneasy mood proves danger.” Fog or an unexplained sound can create uncertainty without proving a threat.
  • “A flashback is any past-tense story.” It is a shift from the story’s current moment to an earlier event.
Support, Core and Extend

Support, Core and Extend

Support: Read aloud, explain unfamiliar words and offer “I felt ___ because the words ___ made me imagine ___.” Let the learner dictate their response.

Core: Explain one structural and one language choice, then compare your response with a partner’s.

Extend: Replace one description or change the event order and explain how your response changes. Stay with clear Year 4 examples; no formal literary essay is required.

Important questions and answers

Important questions and answers

Question: “The door handle turned. Nila held her breath. Who was outside?” Why might a reader want to continue?

Answer: The visitor remains unknown. The question and Nila’s reaction create uncertainty, encouraging the reader to find out.

Question: Can I appreciate a sad ending without enjoying what happens?

Answer: Yes. You could say that a repeated empty chair makes a loss feel real while explaining that you feel sad about it.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Original extract: “The gate squealed. Ivy froze. Then her brother stepped out holding the lost scarf.”

Task: Explain how your feelings might change across the passage and give your opinion of the ending.

Review hint: Notice what is uncertain first, then what the final event explains.

Possible answer: The noise and Ivy freezing made me uneasy. Her brother appearing with the scarf explains the moment and brings relief, so I found the ending reassuring. Accept another opinion that uses the actual events.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Original extract: “Now Mia smiled beside the flowering pot. Two weeks earlier, she had almost thrown away the bare stem. Her sister had said, ‘Give it time.’”

Task: Name the time shift, explain what it adds and share an opinion about the ending with an adult or partner.

Possible answer: The flashback shows earlier doubt and the sister’s encouragement. I find Mia’s smile satisfying because she waited and the plant flowered.

Adult check: Look for the earlier-time evidence, its effect and the learner’s own supported opinion. If the response only retells events, ask “How does knowing that earlier doubt change the ending for you?” The multiple-choice score supports learning; the shared response is separate adult-checked evidence.
