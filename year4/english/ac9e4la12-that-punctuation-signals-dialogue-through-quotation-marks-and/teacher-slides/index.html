Year 4 English · AC9E4LA12

Understand that punctuation signals dialogue through quotation marks and that dialogue follows conventions for the use of capital letters, commas and boundary punctuation

Use quotation marks, capitals, commas and clear boundaries in your own dialogue.

Ready to project and teach

Learning goalsRead and discuss

Show who speaks and where their words begin and end

Learning intention: I can read and punctuate a character’s dialogue, then write my own short exchange with clear speakers.

Dialogue is spoken language represented in writing. Quotation marks enclose the exact spoken words. A speech tag, such as Mina said or she asked, identifies the speaker. Boundary punctuation includes a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. Capitals, commas and these marks show how the speech and surrounding narration fit together.

Success criteria: enclose only the spoken words; start a complete spoken sentence with a capital; use the appropriate comma or boundary mark; keep a tag outside the quotation; start a new paragraph when the speaker changes; explain a punctuation choice in my own dialogue.

Quotation convention: these exercises use double quotation marks. Consistent single quotation marks are also used in books and are not automatically incorrect. Curly or straight marks are acceptable when clearly paired. Do not change styles simply because a new character speaks.

Use paper or your usual writing support. If listening to a question, also inspect the written options or ask an adult to name the punctuation marks exactly: a spoken reading alone may not reveal which marks are printed. A student can dictate punctuation and paragraph breaks; the adult checks the actual recorded writing.

Read quotation marks and speech tagsRead and discuss

Find the actual spoken words

Preserved Mina model: Mina said, ‘I found it.’ The single quotation marks show Mina’s exact spoken words; the comma after said introduces them. In the double-mark style used for the following models: Mina said, “I found it.” The meaning is unchanged.

Ava said, “We should leave now.” The speech tag comes first, followed by a comma. With the tag last, write: “We should leave now,” Ava said. The comma goes inside the closing quotation mark; the full stop ends the sentence after said.

PatternModelWhat each mark does
Tag before speechAva said, “We should leave now.”The comma follows the tag. The complete spoken statement starts with W and ends with a full stop inside its quote pair.
Speech before tag“We should leave now,” Ava said.The comma is inside the closing quotation mark. The full stop comes after the following tag.
Question before tag“Should we leave now?” Ava asked.The question mark belongs inside the quotation. Do not add a comma after it. Ava keeps its capital because it is a name.
Exclamation before tag“Wait for me!” she called.The exclamation mark belongs inside the quotation; she stays lower case because the tag continues the sentence.

Quick check: Read: “The bridge is open,” Tom said.

Name the spoken words and the tag. Expected: The bridge is open is the speech; Tom said is the tag. If the learner includes Tom said inside the speech, bracket the spoken words separately before adding punctuation.

A comma can also separate someone being addressed within the speech: “Hello, Tessa,” Ali said. The first comma separates Tessa from the greeting; the second joins the spoken statement to the following tag. Do not confuse these two jobs.

Split dialogue and new sentencesRead and discuss

Check whether the speech continues or begins again

One interrupted spoken sentence: “We should,” Ava said, “leave before dark.” Remove the tag and the speech is “We should leave before dark.” The word leave continues the same sentence and is lower case. The tag stays outside two matched quotation pairs, with commas separating it from the spoken parts.

Two spoken sentences: “We should leave now,” Ava said. “It will be dark soon.” The full stop after said ends the first written sentence. It starts a new spoken sentence, so it needs a capital. A continuation beginning with I or a person’s name still retains its normal capital; do not judge only from the first letter.

Guided check: Compare these two forms.

“The path,” Bo said, “is dry.”

“The path is dry,” Bo said. “We can cross.”

Which has a continuation and which has a new spoken sentence? Expected: the words is dry continue The path in the first form; We can cross is a new sentence after the tag’s full stop in the second.

Speech tags, action and speaker paragraphsRead and discuss

Keep the character’s speech and action clear

Speech tag: “Did you hear that?” Kai whispered, stepping back. Whispered reports how Kai said the words. The added action does not turn whispered into a separate action sentence.

Separate action beat: “Did you hear that?” Kai stepped back. Stepped back tells an action, not a way of speaking. It starts its own sentence. For a statement, compare these two forms:

“I am ready,” she said.

“I am ready.” She lifted her bag.

New speaker, new paragraph:

“Are you coming?” Maya asked.

“I will be there soon,” Leo replied.

Maya’s turn and Leo’s turn belong in separate paragraphs. Keep each tag with its speaker’s words. A long quotation may wrap across several screen lines without a speaker change; automatic line wrapping does not require extra quotation marks or paragraph breaks.

Dialogue should help the scene move forward. Choose clear words and a useful tag; “Kai shouted loudly” may repeat an idea already carried by shouted. Precise wording can help, but punctuation and speaker clarity are this lesson’s focus.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: identifying the use of quotation marks, capital letters, commas and boundary punctuation to signal dialogue in texts
  • E2: using punctuated dialogue in own writing

AC9E4LA12: understand that punctuation signals dialogue through quotation marks and that dialogue follows conventions for the use of capital letters, commas and boundary punctuation

E1 in this lesson: locate each quotation pair and explain capitals, commas, questions and exclamations in complete tag-first, tag-last and split models.

E2 in this lesson: create actual original dialogue in guided practice, adult-reviewed bank tasks, all eight homework activities and the exit ticket. Recognising a correct printed answer alone does not establish independent writing.

Four steps: I do, We do, You do, CheckRead and discuss

Model, analyse, compose and check

  1. Model: Say Mina’s sentence aloud, then point to the written speech. Mark only I found it with quotation marks. Add the introducing tag/comma and explain the capital and final stop.
  2. Guide: Move Ava’s tag from first to last. Ask which punctuation changes and which spoken words stay the same. Compare a question with a statement, then compare she said with She lifted her bag.
  3. Compose: Partners each invent a line about choosing a class activity. Write both turns in new paragraphs. Each student then creates a fresh exchange independently, using at least one question.
  4. Check: Read the actual written exchange. Ask the learner to name the quote pairs, one tag connection and a speaker change. If marks are missing, bracket the exact speech first. If a comma joins an action incorrectly, identify the action’s complete sentence and repair its boundary.
Misconceptions and practice choicesRead and discuss

Correct the exact punctuation problem

  • “The speech tag belongs inside the quotes.” Usually the character does not speak their own tag. Enclose the actual spoken words, leaving Mina said outside.
  • “Every quotation starts a new sentence after it.” A following tag can continue the sentence: “Are you ready?” she asked. A name still keeps its capital.
  • “A question mark also needs a comma.” Keep the question mark inside the quotation and do not add a redundant comma before the tag.
  • “An action is a speech tag.” Read: “I found it.” She opened her hand. The action begins a new sentence. Opened describes an action; said would be a speech tag.
  • “Every new screen line needs new quotes.” Only the writing’s actual quotation boundaries and speaker paragraphs matter; screen wrapping may change between devices.

Support: use cards for tag, spoken words and punctuation. Begin with Mina’s tag-first model and dictate marks if needed.

Core: write tag-first and tag-last statements/questions, then a two-speaker exchange.

Extend within the code: interrupt one spoken sentence with a tag, distinguish it from two spoken sentences and add a separate action. Explain the actual punctuation rather than making the scene longer.

Important questions and answersRead and discuss

Explain your editing decisions

Why does a statement use a comma before a following tag?
The speech and tag form one written sentence. In this convention the comma goes inside the closing quotation mark, then the whole sentence ends after the tag.
Why is she lower case after a spoken question?
Read: “Are you coming?” she asked. The tag continues the sentence; a separate action sentence would begin with a capital.
Can two spoken sentences share one quotation pair?
Yes: “I like this place. We should return,” Jo said. Both sentences are spoken; the final comma joins the second statement to the following tag.
Are single quotation marks wrong?
No. Books use different consistent styles. Follow the stated style for a focused editing task and use one style consistently in your own story.
Assessment-style tasks and review modelsRead and discuss

Try the punctuation yourself

  1. Rewrite these words as dialogue with the tag first: nico asked can we use the hall. Hint: mark the exact question.
  2. Move the tag to the end of Vera said, “The kite is ready.” Keep the spoken words unchanged. Hint: check the statement’s comma and the final stop.
  3. Correct “I need a rest,” she sat on the bench. Hint: sitting is an action.

Model 1: Nico asked, “Can we use the hall?”

Model 2: “The kite is ready,” Vera said.

Model 3: “I need a rest.” She sat on the bench.

Adult evidence: require quotation pairs around only the speech, appropriate capitals and each correct boundary. Accept consistent single quotation marks. Reteach the tag/action distinction if the final edit still joins sitting with a comma.

Exit ticketRead and discuss

Write an original dialogue scene

Create three turns between two invented characters preparing a class display. Include one question, one statement and one separate action sentence. Start a new paragraph for each speaker change, then explain two punctuation choices.

One possible model:

“Where shall we put the poster?” Noor asked.

“The wall near the door is clear,” Zac replied. He picked up the tape.

“I will hold the poster,” Noor said.

Explanation: the question mark belongs inside Noor’s quotation. He picked up the tape is a separate action sentence, so it starts with a capital after the preceding sentence’s full stop.

Adult check: inspect all three actual written or dictated turns. Require clear alternating speakers and paragraphs, correctly paired quotations, suitable capitals, a question, a statement, a separate complete action and two accurate explanations. Accept original details and any grammatical tag placement; do not require the model’s wording.

Next step: if the quote boundaries are unclear, identify exactly what each character said. If the action/tag distinction is secure and all marks support the intended reading, continue to the eight-task worksheet, Practice and Test.
