Learning goals

Create a story, then make purposeful changes

Learning intention: I can develop a storyline, character and setting, then use feedback to edit my writing.

A storyline is the connected sequence of events. A character acts, feels and makes choices. A setting tells us where and when events happen; its conditions can change what a character can do. Editing includes improving these choices, as well as checking sentences and punctuation.

Success: write connected events, show a reason for a character’s decision, use a setting detail that matters, and keep a first draft, a specific peer comment and a purposeful revision.

From goal to outcome

Build events that connect

Original story seed: Mina has a map and wants to find her brother before the parade. A crowd blocks her view. She asks a steward where families can meet. At the meeting point, her brother waves.

The goal gives Mina a reason to act. The crowd creates a difficulty. Asking the steward is an attempt, and the reunion resolves this search. A story may have other shapes; this is a useful planning route, not a rule that every story must follow.

Model: goal → difficulty → choice → result. Ask “Why does the next event happen?” If Mina suddenly finds her brother with no clue, add the missing action.

Quick check: What does the crowd change? It stops Mina seeing her brother, so she seeks help. If the answer only describes the crowd’s colour, return to the link between setting and action.

Develop characters and settings

Choose details that do a job

Before: Leo was nervous backstage. After: Leo rubbed his damp palms, then stepped towards the curtain. The action suggests nerves and his decision to perform.

Before: The bush was noisy. After: Cicadas rattled so loudly that Amira stopped to listen for the faint call. The sound affects her action rather than merely decorating the setting.

It is fine to tell readers a feeling or summarise unimportant time: “For an hour, they followed the empty road.” Develop important moments through selected actions, thoughts, senses and dialogue. More adjectives do not automatically improve a story.

E1: Sentence choices, dialogue and devices

Create with a range of sentence choices

Official elaboration: creating texts using a range of sentence types, including dialogue and literary devices.

Original scene: Amira heard a scratching sound behind the shed door. “Can you hear that?” she asked. “Wait here while I ask Dad to open it with us.” A loose branch tapped the roof like a tiny drum. Amira waited.

The question invites a response; the request makes a safe plan; the comparison helps us imagine the tapping. The short final sentence holds the moment. Short sentences can have different effects in other scenes.

Choose a precise verb for your purpose: “Eden went towards the sleeping puppy” could become “Eden tiptoed towards the sleeping puppy” to show careful, quiet movement. “Stomped” would suggest heavy steps and change the scene. Compare meanings, then explain your choice; the longest word is not automatically the best.

Join related ideas when it helps: “When the creek covered the stepping stones, they turned back.” Keep sentences complete and speakers clear. A literary device should suit the intended image or feeling, not be added to every line.

Try: Write three sentences about a model boat rocking gently. Include one comparison and explain why it suits gentle movement. Example comparison: “It rocked like a leaf in a puddle.”

E2: Exchange feedback and revise

Give a reader useful help

Official elaboration: collaborating with a peer to edit literary texts by sharing feedback about choices made to develop storylines, characters and settings.

Draft: Noor looked for her dog. She went to the gate. Everything was fine.

Peer comment: “The gate gives me a place to picture. I still need to know how the search ends. What does Noor find there?” Writer’s reply: “The dog is behind the gate; I will show her finding him.”

Revision: Noor heard a familiar whine behind the gate. Her dog wriggled out, and she knelt to hug him. Writer’s explanation: “I added the sound, discovery and reaction so the search has a clear ending.”

  1. Read a peer’s actual scene and name one useful choice.
  2. Point to one unclear event, motivation or setting detail. Ask a specific question.
  3. Listen to the writer’s reply; suggest a possible change without taking over their story.
  4. Exchange roles. Keep the comment, revision and reason.

An adult can help rehearse this process at home. Record peer collaboration only when a real two-way peer exchange happens.

4-Step Instructional Sequence

Plan, draft, discuss, revise

  1. Model: read Mina’s story seed; identify the goal, obstacle, choice and result.
  2. We do — guided analysis: compare Leo’s before/after sentences and explain the detail’s effect.
  3. Practise: write a 5–7 sentence story about a missing part in a group model. Include setting, a character decision and an outcome. Exchange feedback with a peer.
  4. Check: revise two sentences and explain how a specific change helps a reader. Inspect actual writing and peer evidence, not only quiz choices.

Materials/time: paper or a writing device; a peer for the exchange; about 15 minutes modelling and 20–30 minutes drafting and editing, adjusted to the class.

Common Student Misconceptions

Make each edit matter

  • “A story is a list of events.” Show why a character’s action follows the problem.
  • “More description is always better.” Select details that help readers picture, understand or feel this scene; move distractions.
  • “Never tell a feeling.” Telling can summarise; developed actions and dialogue are useful at important moments.
  • “Editing means only fixing spelling.” Also check missing events, unclear motivation, inconsistent setting and the ending.
  • “I must accept every suggestion.” Consider the reader’s difficulty and choose a revision that serves your story; explain that choice.
Support, Core and Extend

Write at the right level of support

Support: orally plan a goal, problem, action and result; dictate the scene if needed; revise one sentence with a partner’s question.

Core: draft 5–7 connected sentences with dialogue and a setting detail that affects action; revise two sentences after peer feedback.

Extend: write two possible endings, select the one best prepared by earlier events and explain the choice. Stay with clear Year 4 storytelling; no formal literary theory is needed.

Boundary: use familiar sentence punctuation and reading experience as prerequisites. Story creation and purposeful revision are the target, rather than a fixed plot formula or advanced grammatical labels.

Important questions and answers

Ask about the reader’s experience

Why does Mina ask for help? The crowd blocks her search; the help is a response to a setting obstacle.

Must every emotion be shown through an action? No. Choose action or direct telling to suit the moment and pace.

What makes the comment on Noor’s draft useful? It names a strength, locates an unanswered question and asks for the missing discovery.

What should I keep after editing? A first version, the actual comment, a changed passage and an explanation linking the change to a reader’s need.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Apply it to a new scene

Draft: “A gust blew the picnic map away. Bo arrived at the meeting point. Everyone smiled.”

Task 1: Write the missing event that explains how Bo reaches the right place. Expected evidence: a plausible link, such as asking the organiser for directions. Hint: connect the lost-map problem to the arrival.

Task 2: Add one action or line of dialogue showing Bo’s relief. Example: Bo let out a long breath and waved to the group.

Task 3: Ask a peer whether your sequence is clear; record their comment and revise if needed. Evidence: actual feedback and a reasoned writing decision.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Create and explain

Write 4–6 sentences about a child returning a borrowed object. Develop a place, a small difficulty and a purposeful choice. Include dialogue or an apt literary device. Exchange one specific comment with a peer and revise one sentence.

Adult looks for: an actual connected literary scene; character and setting developed through relevant details; a visible purposeful edit; genuine peer exchange where claimed. Accept varied stories and outcomes.

If the sequence is unclear: ask the writer to explain aloud why each event happens, then add the missing link. If feedback is vague: ask the reader to name the exact sentence that confused them. Selecting good edits in Practice/Test supports learning but does not alone demonstrate story writing.
