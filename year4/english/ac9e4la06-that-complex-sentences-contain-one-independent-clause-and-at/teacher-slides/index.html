Year 4 English · AC9E4LA06

Complex sentences: independent and dependent clauses

Combine a complete main idea with a clause that adds description, time, reason, condition or contrast.

Ready to project and teach

Learning goalsRead and discuss

Build complete sentences with useful detail

Learning intention: I can combine a complete main idea with a dependent clause, then explain the relationship or description I have added.

A clause contains a verb and expresses an event, action or state. In the examples here, it has a subject as well. An independent (main) clause can stand as a complete sentence. A dependent clause needs a main clause to complete the meaning. A complex sentence has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

An adverbial clause can give time, reason, condition or contrast, often introduced by a subordinating conjunction such as before, because, if or although. An adjectival (relative) clause describes a noun, often beginning with who, which or that. These are two ways to add precise information.

Success criteria: locate the whole main and dependent clauses; keep the intended meaning when combining ideas; write a complete sentence; explain what the dependent clause adds.

Use paper or your usual writing or communication support. Practise orally before writing if helpful. Focus on clear Year 4 sentences; longer sentence length and advanced clause labels are not the goal.

Independent and dependent clause modelsRead and discuss

Find the complete main idea

“We sheltered because the hail began.” Main clause: “We sheltered.” Dependent clause: “because the hail began.” The dependent clause gives the reason. In “The shelter that has a green roof is open”, “that has a green roof” describes shelter; the main clause is “The shelter is open”.

Sentence or groupWhat to notice
Small shorebirds fly quickly across the inlet.One clause: extra describing words and phrases do not automatically make a sentence complex.
We stayed inside because it rained.Main: We stayed inside. Dependent: because it rained. The reason is stated.
It rained, so we stayed inside.Two independent clauses joined with so. It communicates the same cause and result using a compound structure.
Because it rained.A dependent clause used alone is a fragment here. Add a main idea: Because it rained, we stayed inside.

Clause or phrase? “After lunch” has no verb and is a phrase. “After lunch ended” contains the verb ended and is a dependent clause. A capital letter and full stop do not turn a dependent clause into a main clause.

Quick check: In “When the music stopped, the dancers bowed”, which words can stand alone? Answer: The dancers bowed. If you chose the when-clause, say it aloud and ask what happened when the music stopped.

Describe a noun with an adjectival clauseRead and discuss

Add a description that belongs to the right noun

Start with “The canoe reached the bank.” Add the detail “The canoe has a yellow stripe.” Combine them: The canoe that has a yellow stripe reached the bank. The clause “that has a yellow stripe” describes canoe. Removing it leaves the main clause “The canoe reached the bank”.

A person: “The guide who knows the wetland led our walk.” The who-clause describes guide. “The guide led our walk” is the main clause. A clause can sit inside the main-clause wording; the main clause need not be one uninterrupted group on the page.

Attach the description clearly: “The cat watched the bird that had a long tail” describes the bird. If you mean that the cat has a long tail, write “The cat that had a long tail watched the bird”. Moving the clause changes the noun described.

Check completeness: “The canoe that has a yellow stripe” names and describes a canoe but leaves its main action unfinished. Add “reached the bank”. More adjectives would not fix the missing main action.

For these identifying descriptions, who or that can describe people, and which or that can describe things when the sentence is grammatical. Do not reject a valid alternative just because it differs from the model. These identifying clauses do not automatically need commas. Extra-information descriptions can use commas, but do not put commas around every relative clause.

Try it: Combine “The painter smiled” with “The painter made the mural”. Model: The painter who made the mural smiled. The clause describes painter. Check the actual sentence and the named noun; accept a grammatical that-version.

Add time, reason, condition or contrastRead and discuss

Choose a clause for the meaning you need

RelationshipOriginal model and explanation
Time and orderBefore the race began, we checked our laces. Checking happened first. We checked our laces before the race began keeps that order.
Simultaneous timeLeila read while Ben drew. Both actions happened at the same time here.
DurationWe waited until the doors opened. Opening marks the end of the wait.
Reason or causeThe grass grew because rain soaked the ground. Rain is the reason; reversing the clauses' roles would change the claim.
ConditionIf the path is clear, we can take the short route. If does not prove that the path is clear. We must take another route unless the path is clear means that a clear path is the exception to needing another route.
ContrastAlthough the wind was strong, the banner stayed in place. It stayed despite a difficulty. Although does not mean because.

The conjunction's meaning depends on the whole sentence. In “Since the water was cold, we wore wetsuits”, since gives a reason. Other uses of since refer to time. Read the actual connection instead of assigning every word one fixed meaning.

Reorder and punctuate: “We packed up after the game ended” can become “After the game ended, we packed up”. Keep the event order and place a comma after the introductory dependent clause. A final because, if or after clause usually does not need that introductory comma.

Quick check: Write a complete sentence beginning “Although the path was steep”. One model: Although the path was steep, we reached the lookout. Check a main clause and a plausible contrast; reteach if the response is only a fragment.

Curriculum Coverage & ElaborationsRead and discuss

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: creating richer, more specific descriptions by using adjectival clauses; for example, “Crossing the mountain range was difficult.” becomes “The mountain pass which had received days of heavy rain was dangerous.”
  • E2: creating more precise and detailed sentences by adding adverbial clauses; for example, “They crossed the mountain range.” becomes “Although the path was overgrown, they crossed the mountain range.”

Supporting teaching example — not official elaboration wording: “Denise found a cocoon.” can be developed into “Denise decided to keep an eye on the cocoon until the butterfly emerged.” The dependent adverbial clause “until the butterfly emerged” adds a time relationship.

E1 in this lesson: the canoe, guide and painter models show how a relative clause adds a specific noun description. In the official example, “which had received days of heavy rain” describes mountain pass.

E2 in this lesson: the race, rain, path and banner models add time, cause, condition and contrast. In the official example, “Although the path was overgrown” adds a difficulty despite which the crossing happened.

Four steps: I do, We do, You do, CheckRead and discuss

Teach, analyse, create and check

  1. Explicit teaching and modelling: Write “We sheltered” and “because the hail began” on two cards. Ask which can stand alone. Join them and explain that the hail gives the reason. Then model the green-roof shelter description and identify its noun.
  2. Guided analysis: Together underline “who made the mural” in the painter example and read the remaining main clause. Compare a because sentence with an although sentence; ask how the relationship changes. If learners include the noun in the relative clause, show which words remain in the main clause.
  3. Collaborative and independent practice: Partners describe an imaginary object using a relative clause, then write a separate sentence about it using a time or reason clause. A partner points to the described noun or explains the relationship. Each student then writes their own fresh example.
  4. Formative assessment: Use the exit ticket below. Check actual sentences and explanations, not a claim of completion. Reteach clause completeness with two cards if a fragment remains; move a misplaced relative clause beside its noun if the description becomes unclear.
Common Student MisconceptionsRead and discuss

Small checks that prevent common errors

  • “Long means complex.” Count clauses, not words. A long noun description or phrase can still belong to one simple sentence.
  • “Every clause can stand alone.” “Because the hail began” has a verb but depends on a main clause. Read it aloud to hear the unfinished relationship.
  • “All dependent clauses start with because or when.” Who, which and that can introduce clauses describing nouns. They need substantial practice too.
  • “Because and although mean the same thing.” Because supplies a reason; although marks a contrast. Check the event's actual meaning.
  • “A relative clause describes the nearest idea anywhere.” Attach it beside the intended noun. Check who or what has the described feature.
  • “Every conjunction needs a comma before it.” In the models here, put a comma after an introductory dependent clause; do not insert a comma inside its subject and verb.
Support, Core and ExtendRead and discuss

Practise at the right level

Support: Start with the two shelter cards. Identify the main idea orally, then add a provided because-clause. Use “The toy that ___ is ___” to compose a complete description with an adult.

Core: Combine supplied ideas with a relative clause and write time, cause, condition and contrast sentences. Identify the noun described or the relationship expressed.

Extend within this code: Rewrite the same event in two clause orders and explain why its meaning stays the same. Revise an unclear noun description and explain the improved attachment. Accuracy and meaningful detail matter more than accumulating clauses.

Important questions and answersRead and discuss

Explain how your sentence works

Can a complex sentence begin with Because?
Yes: “Because the hail began, we sheltered.” It must include a main clause; Because at the start does not make the whole sentence wrong.
What is the main clause in “The guide who knows the wetland led our walk”?
The guide led our walk. The relative clause describes guide and sits within that main-clause wording.
Does changing clause order always change event order?
No. “Before the race began, we checked our laces” and “We checked our laces before the race began” both put checking first.
Is an adjectival phrase the same as an adjectival clause?
No. “With a yellow stripe” is a phrase. “That has a yellow stripe” is a clause, including the verb has. Both describe a noun, but their structures differ.
Assessment-style questions and review hintsRead and discuss

Try a fresh sentence

  1. Find the main and dependent clauses in “We lowered the shade because the sunlight was bright”. Review hint: read the complete main idea without the reason.
  2. Combine “The artist lives nearby” and “The artist paints birds” using a relative clause. Name the noun it describes. Review hint: keep both facts and check that your main action is complete.
  3. Write two versions of a sentence about finishing lunch before tidying a table. Start one with After. Review hint: preserve which event happened first.

Models and adult check: 1. Main: We lowered the shade; dependent: because the sunlight was bright. 2. The artist who paints birds lives nearby; the clause describes artist. Accept a valid that-version or the alternative “The artist who lives nearby paints birds”. 3. After we finished lunch, we tidied the table. We tidied the table after we finished lunch. Check both complete sentences, their event order and the first introductory comma.

Exit ticket and mastery evidenceRead and discuss

Create and explain

Write two complete sentences about an imaginary library corner. In one, use a clause to describe a bookcase. In the other, use a reason or time clause. Identify both dependent clauses and explain what each adds.

One model: The bookcase that has curved shelves stands by the window. We switch on a lamp when the light fades. “That has curved shelves” describes bookcase; “when the light fades” gives the time for switching on the lamp.

Adult check: Require two actual complete sentences, a relative clause clearly describing bookcase, a meaningful reason or time clause and correct identification/explanation of both. Accept original details and grammatical alternatives; use writing, dictation or the student's usual communication support. A label without a created sentence is not enough.

Next step: If a main clause is missing, remove the relative clause temporarily and complete the main idea. If the time or cause is reversed, order the two events before recombining. If all evidence is clear, continue to the eight-task worksheet, Practice and Test.
