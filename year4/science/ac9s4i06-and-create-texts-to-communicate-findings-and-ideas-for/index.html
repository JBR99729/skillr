Make your scientific meaning clear

Learning intention: Create a scientific text for a chosen audience and purpose, using accurate evidence, vocabulary and helpful digital tools where appropriate.

Learn the skill

1. Choose audience, purpose and format

Name who the text is for and what it should help them understand. Choose a poster, report, labelled model, oral explanation or other useful format.

A clear purpose

Explain to a younger class how the tested surfaces changed the block’s sliding distance.

Try it · Does “make a slideshow” state the audience and purpose?

No. It names a format. Also state who will use it and what they should learn.

2. Connect the finding to evidence and vocabulary

Use scientific terms that make the explanation precise, and explain unfamiliar words. Include units and relevant results rather than replacing evidence with impressive language.

Short report model

With the same block and release setup, board runs were 58–62 cm and felt runs were 28–32 cm. Friction between the tested surfaces helps explain the shorter travel on felt. These results apply to this model test.

Try it · Is “felt makes the block heavier” supported by these records?

No. The records compare sliding distances, not mass. Describe the relevant surface interaction instead.

3. Choose and check representations and tools

A graph can compare distances, a photograph can show the setup, and a digital table can organise records. Check labels and numbers against the source records before sharing.

Tool or representationWhat to check

Try it · Must every scientific explanation use a digital tool?

No. Use one where it helps. Accurate drawings, writing or speaking can also communicate the science.

Common mistakes

“Simpler language allows different results.” Simplify the wording while keeping the evidence and meaning accurate.

“A decorated picture proves the conclusion.” Link the conclusion to relevant observations or measurements.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain it two ways

2 minutes: choose two audiences. 4 minutes: write a short explanation for each using the same records. 2 minutes: add a useful labelled representation. 2 minutes: ask a partner to check the numbers, units and meaning.

Adjust the challenge Support: dictate one accurate sentence and label a supplied graph. Extend: justify which details each audience needs and one limitation to keep.

Use supplied records or authorised images. Do not publish personal details or imply that invented model results were collected by your class.

Three tricky questions

1. Does a shorter felt column in a distance graph mean less friction?

No. It means a shorter recorded distance. Under the controlled model setup, greater friction can help explain the shorter slide; the graph does not directly measure force.

2. Can an audience-friendly explanation omit the fact that the test used a particular block and setup?

Keep enough context to avoid making a universal claim. Simplification should preserve the finding’s limits.

3. Is a visually attractive report automatically scientifically reliable?

No. Check evidence, method, labels and reasoning as well as presentation.

FAQ

Which scientific vocabulary is useful here?

Words such as friction, surface, distance, evidence and fair comparison, with clear explanations when needed.

What should a peer check?

Whether the text answers its purpose, fits its audience, preserves the records and uses accurate labels and vocabulary.

What students learn in AC9S4I06

Learning intention: Write and create scientific texts for a clear purpose and audience. Use accurate evidence, scientific vocabulary and useful visuals; choose digital tools when they help the audience understand.

A scientific text may be a report, labelled model, poster, spoken explanation, song or slideshow. The format can change, but the underlying evidence and scientific meaning must remain accurate.

  • Name who the text is for and what it should explain or encourage.
  • Write an informative explanation or recommendation with relevant evidence.
  • Create and label a model that communicates a relationship.
  • Acknowledge the source of information and respect the knowledge holders.
  • Use feedback to improve accuracy and readability.

Year-level boundary: Use familiar water, materials and feeding relationships. Explain terms in everyday language. Formal scientific publishing, advanced food-web calculations and professional media production are not required; paper, voice or simple digital tools are all suitable.

Key vocabulary
Purpose
Why a text is being made: for example, to inform, explain or encourage a useful action.
Audience
The intended readers, viewers or listeners.
Scientific vocabulary
Precise terms that help explain the topic, such as evaporation, consumer, predator, flexibility or insulation.
Caption
A short explanation of what a visual shows.
Attribution
Acknowledging whose work or knowledge is used and where it was learned.
Introduced predator
An animal brought outside its natural range that hunts and eats other animals.
Prey
An animal that a predator eats.
Concept models and worked thinking

Write a material recommendation

Audience: Classmates choosing a model tent cover. Purpose: Explain which tested sample meets two needs.

Model report: “Our model tent needs a cover that keeps the inside dry and folds into shape. Coated nylon met both requirements in our short test. Cotton folded but let water through. Thin card stayed dry but did not fold as required. We recommend the tested coated nylon; this does not show that every nylon fabric or tent works in every storm.”

Teacher asks: Where does each sentence’s evidence appear? Expected: Pupils point to both requirement columns and each named sample. The coating and short test belong in the report.

Explain a feeding model

Model explanation for classmates: “Grass is a producer. The grasshopper eats grass, and the small bird eats the grasshopper. In this scenario, the introduced cat is a predator of the bird. Hunting can reduce bird survival. Fewer birds could leave more grasshoppers uneaten, but other factors can also affect the numbers.”

The arrow key explains food and energy moving to the eater. The model shows selected links, not measured local population counts or every possible food source.

Build a clear water-saving message

Audience: The school community. Purpose: Share the example findings and encourage sensible water use.

Model message: “In this invented example, tap running time was sixteen minutes before the reminder and eleven after it in matching lunch breaks. Turn taps off while soaping, then rinse. Tell an adult about leaks.” The chart measures minutes, not litres; it does not prove the reminder alone caused the difference.

A large labelled visual and a short spoken explanation suit assembly. A printed poster can serve the same purpose. Keep necessary drinking and hygiene practices.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Official content description: write and create texts to communicate findings and ideas for identified purposes and audiences, using scientific vocabulary and digital tools as appropriate

  1. E1: discussing the purpose of a text and identifying vocabulary appropriate to the topic and audience. Compare a teacher report with a younger-class explanation. Keep data exact while defining terms, changing sentence length and selecting relevant detail.
  2. E2: acknowledging and learning about First Nations Australians’ ways of representing and sharing information about water sources. Read the attributed Ngurrara I example below. Write a source acknowledgement and explain how the named work shares knowledge about Country, including water sources. Learn from its public account without inventing or copying cultural meanings.
  3. E3: sharing ideas about ways to represent feeding relationships including using drawings, labels, images or models. Create the feeding model as a drawing or labelled cards. Explain arrow direction, organism identities and one simplification to a partner.
  4. E4: producing an informative text using scientific vocabulary to explain the impact of introduced predators on food chains. Write a short paragraph using predator, prey and consumer. Explain a direct effect and a possible indirect effect in the cat–bird–grasshopper scenario; do not claim exact number changes.
  5. E5: constructing a report using scientific vocabulary to explain which materials are best suited to be used for making particular products, such as nylon for tents, rubber for shoes or wool for warm clothing. Use the tent model to write a recommendation. Explain rubber grip and flexibility for a sole, or wool fibres trapping air to help slow heat loss in clothing. Material properties must connect to the particular product’s needs.
  6. E6: creating posters, a song, slideshow or performance to encourage the school community to save water. Create a real message with a specific audience, achievable action and accurate reason or evidence. A digital tool is useful when it makes labels or spoken explanations clearer, not when it covers the evidence.
Learning from a water-knowledge representation

Ngurrara I: a publicly explained example

The National Museum of Australia describes Ngurrara I as a collaborative painting made by nineteen Traditional Owners. Its view of the Great Sandy Desert includes waterholes. The museum connects the work with Walmajarri and Wangkajunga peoples and their enduring relationships with Country. This is a specific example of artwork communicating place-based knowledge, including water sources, rather than just a list of current water measurements.

Source: National Museum of Australia, Australian Journey: Travelling Country, opening transcript account of Ngurrara I. The short account above is enough for this task; the full historical video is not required.

Teacher does: Read the note, name the work and knowledge holders, and ask how information can be communicated visually. Follow local guidance when seeking local examples; communities and works can share knowledge differently.

Student creates: Write an acknowledgement and a two-sentence explanation based on the supplied public account. Do not copy the artwork, guess unshared meanings or claim one symbol system applies everywhere.

Expected: An attributed explanation recognising that the named artwork conveys knowledge of Country and waterholes. Reteach if the response treats First Nations knowledge as one generic tradition or presents guessed meanings as facts.

Guided learning activities

We do: report reconstruction

Teacher and class work together to order question and purpose → method → results table or model → finding → conclusion and limit. Explain how each part lets the reader follow and check the evidence. Then write a new short report using the tent records rather than merely naming sections.

Audience rewrite

Evidence: water warmed from 21°C to 29°C in ten minutes. For a poster: “Water warmed from 21°C to 29°C in ten minutes.” For a spoken explanation: “The temperature increased by eight degrees Celsius during our ten-minute test.” Both preserve the same evidence. Do not add “largest rise” without comparison data.

Visual audit and peer feedback

Purpose → choose a useful visual → label names and units → write a caption → explain the finding → check readability. Ask a partner to tell you the message in their own words, then revise one unclear part without changing the data.

Quick checkpoint: Does a colourful chart become a measured field survey? No. Label invented data or a model honestly; format does not change evidence type.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Adapt the audience by changing the facts: Change vocabulary support and layout while preserving values and meaning.
  • A model shows everything: Name the selected relationship and one limitation.
  • Scientific language means the longest word: Choose a precise relevant term and explain it when needed.
  • Insulation generates heat: Wool can trap air and help slow heat loss; it is not a heat source.
  • Fewer birds guarantees exactly twice as many insects: Explain a possible relationship and acknowledge other influences.
  • Public cultural knowledge needs no acknowledgement: Credit the knowledge holders and source, and stay within what is publicly explained.
  • A water campaign should stop every use: Target avoidable waste while preserving necessary drinking and hygiene.
Support, Core and Extend

Support: Offer a word bank, labelled evidence table and sentence frames: “This text is for…”, “The evidence shows…”, “We recommend…because…”. An adult can read the public source note aloud.

Core: Create a labelled model, write an informative explanation and a material report, and develop a school water-saving message.

Extend: Present the same findings in a report and an oral or digital format, explain why each audience needs different support, and revise after feedback while keeping every claim accurate.

Important questions and answers
What changes when a report becomes a poster?
Layout, sentence length and amount of detail may change; evidence and scientific meaning do not.
How do feeding arrows help an audience?
A key explains food and energy moving from source to eater.
Why does a recommendation need properties and evidence?
The reader needs to see how the tested material meets the product’s particular requirements.
How can a text acknowledge the water-knowledge example?
Name Ngurrara I, its Traditional Owner creators and the museum account, then explain only the publicly supported information.
When is a digital tool appropriate?
When it helps the intended audience understand, for example by enlarging labels or combining a readable diagram with spoken explanation.
Assessment-style questions and review hints

Prompt: Explain evaporation to a younger pupil. Expected: “Liquid water changes into water vapour in the air; this is evaporation.” Review: Do not say the water is destroyed.

Prompt: Write a sentence recommending a flexible material for a shoe sole that also needs grip. Expected: Link both flexibility and grip to the use and refer to tested evidence where provided. Review: One useful property does not establish every requirement.

Prompt: Create a school water-saving slogan with a safe action. Expected: A clear action such as reporting a leak or turning taps off while soaping, with necessary water use preserved. Review: A rhyme or digital effect does not justify a false savings claim.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Create a mini-report for classmates choosing a model container. The container must bend without breaking and stay dry inside. Sample A bends and stays dry; B bends but leaks in the same short test. Write three sentences and add a labelled evidence sketch or table.

Expected: Identify the audience and two needs; compare both samples accurately; recommend A for these tested conditions and avoid claims about every future use. The created visual must show both labels and both results.

Continue when: the pupil actually creates the text and visual, uses relevant vocabulary and links the choice to evidence. Reteach if: they only describe what they would create, omit a requirement, change a value or confuse the model with measured real-world population data.
