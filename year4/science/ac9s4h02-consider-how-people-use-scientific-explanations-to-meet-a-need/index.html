YEAR 4 SCIENCE · AC9S4H02

Use science to solve a practical problem

Learning intention: Connect a practical need to a scientific explanation, then test whether a proposed solution works within its limits.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Recover steel clips from a mixed tray

The need is to recover ordinary steel clips from a tray containing steel clips, plastic counters and aluminium pieces. A large classroom magnet is a possible tool because it attracts the tested steel across a small gap. It will not automatically collect every metal. The example test record below is invented.

Object type in model testNumber presentNumber lifted by the magnet
Ordinary steel clips55
Plastic counters50
Aluminium pieces50

Illustrations provide context. Use the supplied labelled records and models for exact values; do not measure the artwork.

Learn the skill

1. State the need and success criteria

Say exactly what the solution should do. Collecting the steel clips while leaving the other materials is a clearer goal than simply saying “make sorting better”.

A testable requirement

Recover the supplied ordinary steel clips without picking up the plastic and aluminium samples.

Try it · Would “looks impressive” alone show that the sorting need is met?

No. Check whether the intended objects are recovered and the unwanted ones remain.

2. Explain why the idea might work

Link a scientific property or interaction to the job. Magnetic attraction can move the suitable steel objects towards the tool, even before contact.

Explanation → action

Magnet attracts the tested steel → use the magnet to collect those clips from the mixture.

Try it · Would the same magnet necessarily collect the aluminium?

No. An ordinary classroom magnet does not strongly attract ordinary aluminium. A different sorting step is needed.

3. Test, compare and improve

Record what the tool collects, what it misses and what else is picked up. Change one feature at a time when comparing improvements, such as the working gap.

CheckUseful evidence
Does it meet the need?Correct objects recovered
What is its limit?Materials it does not collect
What might improve it?A fair comparison at a different gap
Try it · Does success with five clips prove that a factory sorter is ready?

No. A larger setting introduces other materials, quantities, safety needs and operating conditions.

Common mistakes

“Science gives one perfect solution.” Several solutions may meet different needs; test them against clear requirements.

“A magnet sorts all metal from non-metal.” Magnetic response varies. Do not use that claim for all metals.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Design a sorting explanation

2 minutes: state the need and criteria. 4 minutes: draw a labelled model of the sorting idea. 2 minutes: explain the magnetic interaction. 2 minutes: name a limitation and a fair improvement test.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the supplied model record. Extend: compare this idea with a second safe sorting method using the same success criteria.

Use the model table or adult-supervised equipment. Adults select large, intact magnets and age-safe samples; avoid small loose magnets and sharp metal.

Three tricky questions

1. If one clip stays behind, must the explanation of magnetism be wrong?

No. Gap, position, material or the tool’s strength may affect the outcome. Investigate the specific cause.

2. Is the strongest magnet always the best solution?

Not automatically. Handling, the intended objects, unwanted pickup and the task’s conditions also matter.

3. Can knowledge of friction solve a different need?

Yes. For example, evidence about grip can inform shoe or braking designs. The solution must be tested for its own purpose.

FAQ

What does “meeting a need” mean?

Using an idea to address a stated practical problem and checking the result against what is required.

Must the solution be a new invention?

No. Choosing or improving an existing method can also apply scientific knowledge.

What students learn in AC9S4H02

Concept models and worked thinking

Turn heat-transfer science into an insulated-container design

need: keep drink cool→science: reduce heat transfer→criteria: temperature after 2 h→choose materials→test→improve

The explanation guides a design feature, but the solution must still meet constraints such as mass, cost, safety and reuse. Testing checks whether it works under stated conditions.

  1. Identify the need to keep the drink cool and the direction of heat transfer from warmer surroundings.
  2. Explain how an insulating layer slows that heat transfer.
  3. Check the temperature evidence under the same starting conditions.

Six ways science meets a need

E1 · Decomposers solve a waste problem

Food businesses and councils need ways to manage suitable plant and animal waste. Decomposers break down organic material. Industrial systems manage air, moisture and temperature to support the process and check the output. Their accepted materials and controlled conditions may differ from a home heap.

Worked example: A facility finds ordinary plastic fragments in its output. Improve sorting rather than assuming extra time will compost all plastic. Check the facility’s accepted list; do not copy industrial processing of animal waste in a classroom.

Check: Why add ventilation? Answer: Many useful decomposers need air. A scientific explanation identifies how the action helps, not merely that a machine is “better”.

E2 · Community-led water solutions

ACARA’s account describes Arrernte Elders in Central Australia investigating solar-powered water treatment to address water-quality needs. This is a documented example, not a claim about the project’s current operating status.

Science-to-need link: Test the source water, choose treatment for the identified problem and monitor the treated water. Dissolved substances can remain when water looks clear; removing visible dirt alone does not establish drinking safety. Community choices, local training and maintenance help the solution continue meeting actual needs.

Check: Does a tank create more rain during a dry season? Answer: No. Storage planning must consider supply and use. Study the account; do not drink classroom filtered water.

E3 · Plant knowledge and useful materials

ACARA describes Yolŋu Peoples at Yirrkala using knowledge of stringybark and seasonal conditions to obtain a painting surface while protecting the tree’s survival. This named example shows how plant knowledge supports a useful material and continuing access to it.

Across the varied examples in the curriculum, plants provide materials for tools, weapons, sound instruments, clothing, cosmetics and artworks. Knowledge of growth, suitable plant parts and recovery matters alongside properties such as strength and flexibility. These are diverse, place-connected knowledges; one example does not stand for every community.

Classroom response: Read a reliable attributed example and draw a need–plant knowledge–use–future supply map. Do not remove bark, collect protected material or copy cultural designs. Check: Why is “natural means unlimited” wrong? Answer: Plants can be damaged or harvested faster than they recover.

E4 · Plastic properties change decisions

Plastic’s durability can be helpful in a reusable box and problematic in litter that persists. Flexibility, strength and resistance to water vary between products. These properties help explain choices about purchasing fewer unnecessary items, caring for reusable products and disposing of them through suitable local systems.

Worked example: A sturdy box already owned can remain useful for repeated storage. Its durability is a reason to care for it, not to discard it after one use. A “compostable” product may need particular industrial conditions; check collection instructions. Breaking into tiny pieces is not proof of harmless decomposition.

Check: Does every recycling symbol guarantee local acceptance? Answer: No; the material, product form and available process matter.

E5 · Friction helps people stop

Bicycle brakes use friction between contacting surfaces to slow a wheel. Tyre-road grip also depends on friction. Wet or icy conditions can reduce grip, affecting braking and steering, so safety decisions must consider the surface and conditions.

Safe model: An adult compares a small sliding block on two tabletop surfaces with identical starting conditions. A longer stopping distance can suggest less resistance in this setup. This illustrates a scientific link, not a complete test of road or helmet safety. Do not test by riding on slippery surfaces.

Check: Is one dry-surface result proof of the safest tyre everywhere? Answer: No. Relevant conditions and repeated evidence are needed.

E6 · Magnetic sorting meets different needs

In recycling, a magnet can help lift suitable iron or steel from mixed materials. In mining, different magnetic responses may help separate minerals. In food processing, magnetic separators can remove suitable unwanted iron or steel fragments. Each application uses the same force for a different need.

Worked example: A separator lifts steel but leaves aluminium. That does not make aluminium a non-metal; common magnets do not strongly attract it. Another process may be needed. In a food line, other contamination still requires other checks.

Check: What evidence should a separator claim include? Answer: Tests with the actual mixture and an account of what remains.

Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use reliable, community-approved sources and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Do not present diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before reproducing cultural material or inviting community participation.

Important questions and answers

How does composting use science?
It provides suitable conditions for decomposers to process accepted organic material.
Why test source water?
The identified problem guides suitable treatment and later checks.
How can plant knowledge protect future resources?
Understanding survival and recovery helps guide sustainable harvesting.
Why does plastic durability affect disposal?
A durable product can last through reuse but may persist if littered.
How do friction and magnetism meet different needs?
Friction supports braking and grip; magnetism helps separate suitable magnetic materials.
Why test a proposed solution?
Evidence shows whether it meets the need and what limitations remain.
Apply, check and explain

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Question: What does the sorting evidence show? Answer: Fewer plastic pieces after the change, with some still present. Review hint: Do not turn improvement into a claim of perfection.

Question: Explain why a magnet leaving aluminium behind is not proof that it failed to collect steel. Answer: The separator’s intended job and different materials’ magnetic responses matter.

Support, Core and Extend

Support: Use “People need ___. Because ___, they use ___.” Core: Explain a solution with a correct causal science link and relevant evidence. Extend: Identify a limitation and an additional check without assuming success everywhere.

Exit ticket

  1. Name one practical need from the lesson.
  2. Explain the science behind a response to it.
  3. State evidence that would show success and one limit.

Expected evidence: For example, organic waste management; decomposers under suitable conditions; reduced suitable scraps with contamination checks. Accept other accurate lesson examples. Reteach if the answer merely names a product or uses “scientific” without explaining how it works.

Year-level boundary

Use qualitative explanations about decomposition, plant survival, material properties, friction and magnetic attraction. Engineering vocabulary can help planning but is not the learning goal. Do not require force equations, microbiology, treatment chemistry or professional safety certification. Use paper models and clean household materials; adults supervise magnets and water, and no test water is consumed.

Common misconceptions
  • The first idea is called the solution — Define criteria and compare alternatives before deciding.
  • Scientific explanation guarantees success — A design must be tested under relevant conditions.
  • Best means best for everyone — Users and constraints can change the preferred trade-off.
  • Aesthetic preference used as criterion — Criteria should be measurable or clearly evaluable.
