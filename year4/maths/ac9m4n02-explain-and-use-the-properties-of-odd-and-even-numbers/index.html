Odd and even number patterns (AC9M4N02)

Learn the skill

Six learning focuses

Learning goal: An even number can be arranged into pairs with no remainder; an odd number leaves one unpaired. Whether a whole number is odd or even is called its parity. This follows predictable patterns under addition, subtraction and multiplication.

1. Make pairs and equal shares

An even whole number can be arranged entirely in pairs. An odd whole number leaves one object unpaired. Zero is even: there are no unpaired objects, and two equal shares of zero are possible.

Use pairs to explain odd and even

odd: one unpaired

Nine is odd because four pairs can be made and one object remains unpaired. A final digit of 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8 identifies an even whole number.

  1. Make pairs until fewer than two objects remain.
  2. Four pairs use eight objects; the ninth object has no partner, so nine is odd.
  3. For larger numbers, each ten already makes five pairs. Only the ones can leave a single leftover, which explains the final-digit rule.

E2: explain why some collections can be shared evenly between 2 people and some leave a remainder.

Try it: Classify 17 counters by making pairs.

Check the answer

Odd: eight pairs use sixteen counters and one remains.

2. Use the ones digit

Every ten is made of pairs, so the higher places cannot create an unpaired one. Only the ones digit decides. Endings 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 mean even; 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 mean odd.

E1: explain why numbers ending in 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 are even and those ending in 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 are odd.

Try it: Is 8 043 odd or even? Which digit decides?

Check the answer

Odd. Its ones digit is 3, even though its thousands digit is even.

3. Explain addition patterns

Paired groups joined together remain paired. Two odd groups each bring a spare; the two spares make a pair. Therefore even + even and odd + odd are even, while one odd and one even give an odd total.

Two odd groups combine to make an even total

This explanation works for any two odd whole numbers: their pairs stay paired and their two leftovers pair with each other.

Try it: Without calculating the total, classify 37 + 25.

Check the answer

Even. Both addends are odd and their two spare objects can pair.

4. Explain subtraction patterns

For non-negative whole-number differences, removing even from even or odd from odd gives even. The mixed cases give odd. Check the roles in the actual subtraction; subtraction cannot simply be reversed.

Keep the results as whole numbers here. Removing an even amount removes complete pairs; removing an odd amount also removes one unpaired item or breaks a pair.

Try it: Can 82 − 35 equal 48? Use odd/even reasoning.

Check the answer

No. Even minus odd must be odd, but 48 is even. This rejects 48 without finding the exact difference.

5. Explain multiplication patterns

An even factor lets the objects form pairs, so any whole-number product with an even factor is even. Odd × odd is odd: an odd number of odd groups leaves one spare overall.

Explain patterns using pairs, not only examples. Two unpaired objects from odd + odd join to form another pair.

For addition, combine paired collections and inspect the leftovers. For multiplication, an even-sized group consists only of complete pairs; repeating it preserves those pairs. For odd × odd, the odd number of single leftovers has one left after pairing.

Try it: Predict whether 13 × 7 is odd or even.

Check the answer

Odd, because both factors are odd. Multiplying by an even factor instead would give an even product.

6. Follow a decision procedure

An algorithm is an ordered set of steps. Read the whole number, inspect its ones digit, ask whether it belongs to 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and follow yes → even or no → odd. Then stop.

Follow an odd/even decision

Worked trace: 354 ends in 4 → Yes → Even. 731 ends in 1 → No → Odd. Applied to 0, 18, 25 and 42, the Even branch keeps 0, 18 and 42. Zero belongs here: it makes two groups of zero with none left over.

Use odd/even reasoning to check an answer

If someone writes 62 + 28 = 89, reject it: even + even must be even, while 89 is odd. But an even proposed answer is not automatically correct: 62 + 28 = 88 also has the required parity but is wrong. Calculate the exact sum, 90, to finish the check.

E3: explain and use the patterns in adding, subtracting and multiplying odd and even numbers to check calculations.

E4: follow a flow-chart algorithm to decide whether numbers are even or odd and identify numbers divisible by 2.

Try it: Trace the procedure for 0 and 105. Does matching odd/even prove every digit of an answer is right?

Check the answer

0 is even; 105 is odd. Matching odd/even is only a check: a wrong answer can have the expected parity too.

Guided learning activities

1. Pairing proof

Use counters to model 13, 14 and 15, then record the number of pairs and whether one remains.

2. Operation investigation

Test several odd and even additions, then write a general rule and explain it with pairing.

odd + oddunpaired + unpaired makes a pair
odd + evenone unpaired remains
even + evenall pairs remain

3. Parity prediction

Predict whether each result is odd or even, then use a calculation to check. 4 382 + 7 915 is odd (even + odd); 326 × 47 is even (an even factor); 9 999 − 624 is odd (odd − even). Their exact values are 12 297, 15 322 and 9 375.

Common misconceptions

  • The first digit decides odd or even — Use the ones digit: 3 482 is even despite starting with 3. Every ten, hundred and thousand can be fully paired.
  • Every multiplication result is even — Odd × odd is odd.
  • A pattern from one example is proof — Test several cases and explain using pairs or factors of 2.
  • Subtraction pattern ignored — Parity rules for subtraction match addition when whole-number results are allowed.
