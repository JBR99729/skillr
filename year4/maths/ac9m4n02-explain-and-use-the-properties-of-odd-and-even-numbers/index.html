AC9M4N02: Odd and even numbers

Explain and use the properties of odd and even numbers.

Topic guideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
What students learn in AC9M4N02

An even number can be arranged into pairs with no remainder; an odd number leaves one unpaired. Parity follows predictable patterns under addition, subtraction and multiplication.

Learning routine: Pair the quantity → Identify parity → Test an operation → Generalise → Justify

Success looks like

  • Classify odd/even
  • Use pairing explanations
  • Apply operation patterns
  • Predict parity
  • Justify generalisations
Concept models and worked thinking

Use pairs to explain odd and even

odd: one unpaired

Nine is odd because four pairs can be made and one object remains unpaired. A final digit of 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8 identifies an even whole number.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: explain and use the properties of odd and even numbers.

  • E1: explain why numbers ending in 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 are even and those ending in 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 are odd.
  • E2: explain why some collections can be shared evenly between 2 people and some leave a remainder.
  • E3: use patterns such as even + even = even, odd + even = odd and odd + odd = even to check calculations.
  • E4: follow a flow-chart algorithm to decide whether numbers are even or odd and identify numbers divisible by 2.
Quick check and feedback
  1. Is 347 odd or even? Odd, because the ones digit is 7.
  2. Will odd + odd be odd or even? Even; for example 5 + 7 = 12. Two unpaired ones combine to make a pair.
  3. A student says 3 x 6 must be odd because it starts with 3. What is the fix? Check the product: 18 is even, and any number of equal groups of an even number is even.

Mastery evidence: Students justify parity using ones digits, pairs, arrays or a clear counterexample, not by guessing from the first digit.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Odd means prime — Many odd numbers are composite, such as 9 and 15.
  • Every multiplication result is even — Odd × odd is odd.
  • A pattern from one example is proof — Test several cases and explain using pairs or factors of 2.
  • Subtraction pattern ignored — Parity rules for subtraction match addition when whole-number results are allowed.
