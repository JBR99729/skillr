Statistical investigations

conduct statistical investigations, collecting data through survey responses and other methods; record and display data using digital tools; interpret the data and communicate the results

Practice on app ↗Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Worked example

A class preference question expects varying responses that can be collected and compared.

Learn the skill

1. Ask a question you can investigate

A clear survey question tells people what to answer.

Try it · How could you ask about preferred fruit so each person gives one response?

Ask each person to choose one fruit from the same list.

2. Record and display

Enter categories and counts in a table. Use a labelled graph to show the totals.

Try it · What should the height of each column represent?

The number of responses for that category.

3. Interpret and report

Use the displayed counts and name the group you investigated.

Try it · In your group, six chose apples and four chose pears. What can you report?

Two more people in the group chose apples than pears.

Common mistakes

Drawing a conclusion about everyone from a small group. Name the group you surveyed when reporting your result.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Run a quick survey

Ask everyone the same question about a preferred fruit. Count each response once, enter the totals in a spreadsheet and create a column graph. Explain one comparison.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two response options. Extend by asking whether another group might answer differently.

FAQ

How can you check that every response is included?

Add the category counts and compare the total with the number of responses.

What students learn in AC9M4ST03

A statistical investigation is a connected process. The question determines the data needed, the method affects data quality and conclusions must be supported by the collected evidence and limited to the population studied.

Learning routine: Pose question → Plan population/method → Collect ethically → Clean and organise → Display → Interpret → Communicate → Reflect

Success looks like

  • Pose statistical questions
  • Plan ethical collection
  • Use digital recording
  • Interpret evidence
  • Communicate and reflect

Worked elaborations and complete investigation evidence

Learning intention: Carry out a question-to-report investigation using genuine records and digital displays.

Year 4 boundary: Use counts, categories, clear scales, informal shape and variation. Formal averages, median calculations and causal claims are not required.

Elaboration 1: A complete class movie survey

Ask “Which of these movie genres would you choose?” with one response each and clear categories. Record anonymous responses digitally and check the total. Invented example Comedy 8, Adventure 6, Animation 6 totals 20. Make a column graph and report: Comedy has the largest count,2 more than either other genre. Do not say most of the class chose Comedy:8 is less than half of 20. Communicate the graph to the class, explain the method and acknowledge that this is one group at one time. Students must conduct their own survey and present actual results.

Elaboration 2: Retrieve and compare real online records

With an adult, choose a question such as comparing rainfall in two places for the same month. Retrieve official records using the place/date selection, retaining source, period, variable and units. Use another suitable online source and check whether definitions match. Enter comparable records digitally, graph and report a numerical comparison. Missing rainfall is not zero. Invented rehearsal values PlaceA30 mm and PlaceB45 mm differ by 15 mm; they are not retrieved weather observations. Actual investigation work must include the real records and sources, or remain pending if unavailable.

Elaboration 3: Investigate a cereal shelf-position claim

To explore whether higher-sugar cereals occur at children’s eye level, define a height band and what counts as higher sugar before collection. Record each inspected product or position, shelf band and sugar per 100 g, using a consistent counting rule for duplicate positions. Use adult-provided photographs or permission-based observation with readable labels. Invented rehearsal: of 6 inspected positions,3 of 4 in the height band and 1 of 2 outside meet an agreed sugar threshold. This describes those positions only; it does not establish intent or a result for every shop. Create a digital table/display and report the definitions, observed counts and limitations.

Support, core and extend

Support: Organise a small dataset together and check each entry once. Core: Create and interpret the required digital display. Extend: Compare representations and explain a limitation using the same Year 4 ideas.

Important questions and answers

Does the largest category always mean more than half? No: a largest count of 8 in 20 is less than half. Does a graph establish why something happened? No: it shows recorded patterns unless additional evidence supports a cause.

Assessment-style check and review hints

A one-choice survey records Adventure 7, Comedy 5, Animation 3; one of the five Comedy entries is an accidental duplicate. Correct the digital table, state the total and report one finding with a limitation. Answer: 7, 4, 3 total 14 genuine responses; Adventure has 3 more than Comedy. Describe only these respondents and explain the corrected duplicate. Check the raw record before deleting an entry.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Show your investigation question, method, actual records and digital display. Communicate two numerical findings and a limitation to another person, then record their question and your answer. An adult checks all stages against the source records; a completion tick alone is insufficient.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: conduct statistical investigations, collecting data through survey responses and other methods; record and display data using digital tools; interpret the data and communicate the results
  • E1: creating a survey to collect class responses to a preferred movie choice, and recording data responses using spreadsheets; graphing data using a column graph or other appropriate representations and interpreting the results of the survey reporting findings back to the class
  • E2: conducting a statistical investigation and acquiring data from different online sources; for example, using online query interfaces to select and retrieve data from an online database such as weather records, Google Trends or the World Health Organization
  • E3: investigating different contexts in which statistical investigations can take place and the types of questions to ask to collect data relevant to the context; for example, investigating supermarket customer complaints that breakfast cereals with the most sugar are positioned at children’s eye level, discussing what questions they would need to ask and answer

Common misconceptions
  • Leading survey question — Wording should not push respondents toward an answer.
  • Duplicate or inconsistent entries — Clean data before calculating frequencies.
  • Conclusion exceeds sample — State who was surveyed and limit the claim.
  • Display chosen after writing conclusion — Let the question and data determine the display.
