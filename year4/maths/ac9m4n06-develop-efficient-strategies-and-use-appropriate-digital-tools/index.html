Learn the skill

Six learning focuses

Learning goal: Efficiency means choosing a method that is accurate, understandable and well suited to the numbers and context. Mental strategies, written methods and digital tools each have appropriate uses.

1. Add by partitioning or compensation

Choose a method that fits the numbers. For 398 + 247, add 400 + 247 = 647, then remove the extra 2: 645. The compensation undoes a deliberate change; simply adding two changes the problem.

Choose a friendly addition or subtraction

CalculationCompensationWhy the adjustment works
2 998 + 4673 000 + 467 − 2 = 3 465Increasing the first addend by 2 makes the sum 2 too large.
4 872 − 1 9964 872 − 2 000 + 4 = 2 876Subtracting 2 000 removes 4 too many, so return 4.
  1. Notice how close the number is to a round thousand.
  2. Record the changed calculation and the compensating adjustment.
  3. Check by another method: 2 998 + 400 + 60 + 7 = 3 465; 2 876 + 1 996 = 4 872.

Compensation is especially useful here, but another correct strategy may be clearer for a different learner. A method must preserve the original calculation.

Try it: Calculate 299 + 156 with compensation.

Check the answer

455. Use 300 + 156 = 456, then subtract the extra one.

2. Subtract by taking away or finding the gap

For 503 − 278, count up from 278: 22 reaches 300, 200 reaches 500, then 3 reaches 503. The total gap is 225. Check by joining the removed amount and remainder.

Partition, bridge and count the distance

Bridge a hundred: for 597 + 86, use 3 to reach 600, leaving 83 to add. The result is 683.

Compatible numbers: 1 250 + 375 + 750 = (1 250 + 750) + 375 = 2 375. Reordering the addends does not change their total.

Keep the place-value columns in the usual order and start working at the right, in the ones column.

In 4 786 + 2 357, 13 ones become 1 ten and 3 ones. The 14 tens become 1 hundred and 4 tens. The 11 hundreds become 1 thousand and 1 hundred. Record 7 143.

Subtract across zeros: 4 002 − 1 758 = 2 244. One place-value regrouping is 3 thousands, 9 hundreds, 9 tens and 12 ones. Remove 1 thousand, 7 hundreds, 5 tens and 8 ones, leaving 2 thousands, 2 hundreds, 4 tens and 4 ones. Check 2 244 + 1 758 = 4 002.

Try it: Find 402 − 198 using a useful nearby number.

Check the answer

204. Subtract 200 to get 202, then return the extra 2 removed. Check 198 + 204 = 402.

3. Partition a multiplication

For 6 × 24, split 24 into 20 + 4. Six groups of each part give 120 + 24 = 144. An array or region should show both partial products and retain every cell.

Partition a multiplication region

Every part must be multiplied by the same other factor. For the larger retained example, 36 × 24 = 36 × 20 + 36 × 4 = 720 + 144 = 864. The 24 equal groups are split into twenty groups and four groups.

Check: 32 × 3 is 90 + 6 = 96, not 90 + 2. The two ones also occur three times.

E3: connect array rows and columns with groups and multiplication sentences. The labelled-groups example labels four rows, seven per row and the total.

E6: use place-value partitions, basic facts and regions for products, including double-double. The doubling and multiplication-region examples connect the parts to the complete calculation.

Try it: Work out 7 × 23 by partitioning.

Check the answer

161. Seven twenties give 140 and seven threes give 21; 140 + 21 = 161.

4. Double and halve with care

For 5 × 28, double the number of groups and halve each group: 10 × 14 = 140. The total stays fixed. Doubling just one factor would instead double the product.

Double one factor and halve the other

Teacher asks: “What happens to the number of groups? What happens to each group?” Students explain that twice as many groups, each half the size, keep the same total.

For 25 × 16, double 25 and halve 16: 50 × 8 = 400. Another useful method is double, double: 38 × 4 gives 76, then 152. Doubling both factors would make a product four times as large; it would not preserve it.

E2: demonstrate doubling/halving and regrouping factors with materials. The doubling-and-halving example rearranges 5 × 28 as 10 × 14 and explains the unchanged total.

Try it: Use doubling and halving to calculate 25 × 16.

Check the answer

400. For example, 25 × 16 = 50 × 8 = 100 × 4. Each step preserves the total.

5. Divide using facts and divisible parts

For 156 ÷ 6, split into 120 and 36. Share both parts among six: 20 + 6 = 26. Both partitions must be divided. Multiplication checks that 26 groups of six make 156.

Use equal shares and inverse facts for exact division

For 936 ÷ 8, use 800 + 136: 800 ÷ 8 = 100 and 136 ÷ 8 = 17. Together, each share is 117. Check 117 × 8 = 936, so there is no remainder.

Repeated halving is useful for 96 ÷ 8: 96 → 48 → 24 → 12, because 8 = 2 × 2 × 2. In contrast, dividing by 3 and then by 2 solves division by 6. The divisor must be nonzero.

E5: use arrays or bars when the total, number of groups or amount in each is missing. The labelled-groups example teaches all three roles and an unknown equal length.

E7: explain division with materials, equal thirds and inverse multiplication. The exact-division example splits 96 into shareable parts and checks 936 ÷ 8.

Try it: Calculate 168 ÷ 7 with useful parts.

Check the answer

24. Split 168 into 140 + 28; 140 ÷ 7 = 20 and 28 ÷ 7 = 4. Check 24 × 7 = 168.

6. Choose and explain a method or tool

Mental methods, written working and digital tools each help in different situations. State the quantities and operation first. A calculator checks a calculation you enter; it cannot decide whether your number sentence fits the story.

Connect the model to every number in a sentence

Missing quantitySituationSentence and meaning
Total9 trays with 8 buns on each9 × 8 = 72 buns altogether
Number of groups36 beads; 6 beads per bracelet36 ÷ 6 = 6 bracelets
Amount in each group48 cards shared equally into 4 piles48 ÷ 4 = 12 cards per pile

Say the unit with each number. A quotient may count groups or the amount in one group, depending on what the divisor represents.

Use a digital tool with a clear purpose

  1. Identify the needed operation: combine the weekly counts by addition.
  2. Enter each amount once in B2:B5, put the sum formula in B6, and inspect the range B2:B5. Keeping the formula outside the input range avoids replacing an entry or including the total in its own sum. A spreadsheet is useful when entries will change or the list is long.
  3. Check independently: (1 250 + 1 130) + (1 485 + 1 635) = 2 380 + 3 120 = 5 500.
  4. If B5 increases by 100, the displayed total should increase to 5 600. A formula is useful only when the entries and range are correct.

A nursery shares 3 276 seedlings equally among 7 garden beds. A calculator gives 3 276 ÷ 7 = 468 seedlings per bed. Verify 468 × 7 = 3 276 and keep the unit with the answer. For 49 + 51, mental pairing gives 100 without needing a tool.

E1: choose efficient larger-number addition and subtraction methods: partitioning, inverse relationships, compatible numbers, jumps, bridging, written algorithms and digital tools. The addition, subtraction and digital-tool examples show and check these choices.

E4: use a model to solve a product or exact division and explain each number. The labelled-groups example distinguishes trays, buns, beads, bracelets, cards and piles.

Try it: Which is efficient for 999 + 48: compensation or counting 48 single steps? Explain and solve.

Check the answer

Compensation is efficient: 1 000 + 48 − 1 = 1 047. A correct explained alternative is valid too.

Vocabulary, activities and learning support

Key vocabulary

strategy
a chosen method that fits the numbers and can be explained
partition
split a number into useful parts without changing its value
compensate
make a calculation friendlier, then adjust for the change
inverse
an operation that undoes another: addition and subtraction, or multiplication and exact division
array
objects arranged in equal rows and columns
quotient
the result of division; its meaning depends on what is missing in the story
digital tool
a calculator or spreadsheet used to calculate, organise or check a suitable task

Guided learning activities

1. Compare two accurate strategies

Teacher asks: “Solve 1 999 + 586 in two ways. Which method feels easier to check?” Students record compensation, 2 000 + 586 − 1 = 2 585, and a partition or written method giving the same total.

Look for: a justified choice, not a claim that one method is always fastest. If the answer is 2 587, return to the extra 1 and reverse that change.

2. Build the missing quantity

Give pairs 42 counters. First ask for 6 equal groups: each group has 7. Next ask for groups of 7: there are 6 groups. Students draw both models and label 42 ÷ 6 = 7 and 42 ÷ 7 = 6.

Look for: the quotient changing its meaning. If students swap labels, ask what the divisor counts before calculating.

3. Try and check a digital tool

Enter the four weekly values from the digital-tool worked example into cells B2:B5 and put =SUM(B2:B5) in cell B6 to calculate their sum. Change B5 from 1 130 to 1 230; the total changes from 5 500 to 5 600. If a spreadsheet is unavailable, use a calculator and keep the written list.

Look for: every entry included once, the expected 100 increase and an independent check. Recheck the cell range if changing a listed value does not change the total.

Common misconceptions

  • Use one method for every problem: inspect the numbers and the purpose before choosing.
  • Undo compensation in the wrong direction: write down the extra amount added or removed, then correct it.
  • Subtract the smaller digit from the larger digit: preserve which whole amount is being removed; regroup if needed.
  • Multiply only the tens: every partitioned part must be included.
  • Double both factors to preserve a product: this quadruples the product; double one and halve the other instead.
  • A quotient always counts groups: label the story; it can instead give the amount in each group.
  • Accept a digital display without checking: check entries, operation, scale and units.
  • Efficiency is only speed: reliability and a clear explanation matter too.
