Rounding, Accuracy and Reasonableness — Homework

Teachers can assign selected sections or the full set. Show the deciding digit when rounding and explain why your chosen accuracy suits the context.

Remember: locate exact value first • 0–4 stay, 5–9 round up • package/capacity contexts may require rounding up • estimates should preserve the scale of the original calculation

Part A

Core — 10 questions

  1. Round 47.386 to the nearest whole number, 1 decimal place and 2 decimal places.
  2. Round 12.4879 to 3 decimal places and identify the deciding digit.
  3. Round 3.9996 to 2 decimal places. Explain why the whole-number part changes.
  4. Round 0.0043 to the nearest 0.001.
  5. State the consecutive integers containing 7.32.
  6. State the consecutive integers containing −3.89.
  7. State the consecutive integers containing 12.0001.
  8. State the consecutive integers containing −0.0003.
  9. Round $12,847.32 to the nearest $100 and nearest $1,000.
  10. A value rounds to 6.2 to the nearest tenth. Give three possible original values.

Part B

Reasoning — 8 questions

  1. Explain why 4.99999 lies between 4 and 5 even though it rounds to 5 to the nearest whole number.
  2. A student says −8.0004 lies between −8 and −7. Correct the reasoning using a number line idea.
  3. A value rounds to 4.36 to the nearest 0.01. Describe the full interval of possible original values.
  4. Explain why recording 6.27 mL may be inappropriate if a measuring device is marked only every 0.1 mL.
  5. Compare the estimates 500×20 and 490×20 for 487×19.8. Which is more precise and why?
  6. A calculator gives 82.1×3.9 = 3,201.9. Use estimation to explain why the result is unreasonable.
  7. Explain the difference between rounding to the nearest whole number and rounding up because of package size.
  8. Create an example where too much precision could mislead a reader about the quality of a measurement or estimate.

Part C

Applications — 6 questions

  1. A painter needs 6.13 L of paint sold in 2 L tins. Determine the minimum purchase and explain why ordinary rounding is not enough.
  2. A floor needs 32.4 m² of tiles sold in 12 m² boxes. How many boxes are needed and how much coverage is purchased?
  3. A carpenter needs 11.78 m of timber sold in 5 m lengths. Find the minimum total length purchased and the unused amount.
  4. A school expects 238 people and chairs are delivered in stacks of 25. Determine the number of stacks and total chairs.
  5. A renovation estimate is $24,863.71. Give sensible rounded values for (a) an early planning meeting and (b) a detailed supplier quote. Justify both choices.
  6. A café sells 6 lunches at $18.70 each. A receipt shows $1,122. Use an estimate to diagnose the likely place-value error, then calculate the correct total.

Part D

Extension — 4 questions

  1. A number rounds to 7.4 to the nearest tenth and 7 to the nearest whole number. Describe all possible values.
  2. Investigate the difference between rounding each term before a calculation and rounding only the final answer. Use at least three numbers.
  3. Design two different reasonable estimates for 798×49.6. Compare accuracy and mental efficiency.
  4. Create a purchasing problem where rounding down causes a shortage and over-rounding causes unnecessary cost. Find the best practical decision.

Investigation

How accurate is “accurate enough”?

Choose one real context—construction, shopping, sport timing, laboratory measurement, travel distance or budgeting. Investigate what level of rounding is normally useful, what happens if values are rounded too early or too coarsely, and how estimation can catch errors. Present one worked numerical example.
