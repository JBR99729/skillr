Rounding, Accuracy and Reasonableness

Answer the short questions precisely, then show full contextual reasoning in the 5-mark section. Do not round automatically when a real-world constraint requires a different decision.

Quick reference: identify the requested place • inspect the next digit • distinguish mathematical rounding from purchasing/measurement decisions • use estimation to test whether an answer is sensible

Part A • 10 marks

Five 2-mark questions

  1. Rounding: Round 8.4927 to 2 decimal places. [2]

  2. Integer interval: Identify the pair of consecutive integers containing −6.003. Briefly justify your placement. [2]

  3. Required accuracy: A measurement is 0.00421 m. Round it to the nearest 0.001 m and identify the deciding digit. [2]

  4. Context: A painter calculates 2.91 L of paint is needed. Paint is sold only in 1-L tins. How many tins should be purchased, and why is ordinary rounding not the whole decision? [2]

  5. Precision: Round 0.000873 to 2 significant figures. [2]

Part B • 25 marks

Five 5-mark questions

  1. A laboratory records a mass of 0.009482 kg and must report to the nearest 0.0001 kg. [5]

    (a) Round the mass. (b) State the deciding digit. (c) Explain why reported precision should match the measuring instrument or purpose. (d) Give a context where 0.001 kg would be sufficient. (e) State one risk of reporting more precision than the measurement supports.

  2. A builder calculates that 72.46 m² of tiles are required. Tiles are sold in boxes covering 12 m². [5]

    (a) Give a useful planning estimate. (b) Calculate the minimum number of whole boxes required. (c) Explain why 6 boxes are insufficient even though 72 ÷ 12 = 6. (d) Explain why this is a ceiling/package decision rather than normal nearest-value rounding. (e) Give one practical reason a builder might deliberately purchase an additional box.

  3. A contractor measures a length as 15.487 m. The project specification asks for values to the nearest 0.1 m. [5]

    (a) Report the measurement. (b) Identify the rounding interval containing the original value. (c) Explain why hundredths of a metre would communicate greater precision. (d) Give a context where nearest 1 m could be sufficient. (e) Explain one possible cost consequence of choosing an inappropriate accuracy.

  4. A plumber calculates that 5.87 m of pipe is needed. Pipe is sold in 3-m lengths. [5]

    (a) Estimate the requirement sensibly. (b) Determine the minimum number of pipe lengths to buy. (c) Find the total purchased length. (d) Explain why buying one 3-m length is unreasonable. (e) Use estimation to check that your purchasing decision has the correct scale.

  5. A calculator displays 398 × 21 = 83,580. [5]

    (a) Estimate the product using compatible numbers. (b) Decide whether the displayed answer is reasonable. (c) Explain the size error. (d) Calculate the correct product. (e) Explain why estimation is valuable even when a calculator is used.

Part C • Enrichment

Deep contextual reasoning

A designer estimates 27.63 L of wood stain is needed. Stain is sold in 6-L containers. The aim is to minimise cost while still allowing for multiple coats, absorption differences and a small amount of unavoidable waste.

Task: Decide the most reasonable number of containers to purchase. Support your decision using at least three pieces of mathematical reasoning: an estimate of the requirement, whole-container calculation, and comparison of the consequences of buying one fewer or one more container. Clearly distinguish rounding from the final contextual purchasing decision.
