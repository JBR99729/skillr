Perfect Squares and Square Roots — Homework

This homework is deliberately broader than a fixed worksheet. Work through the sections you need; teachers can select a subset or assign the full set.

Quick reference: n²=n×n • √a reverses squaring • (a+b)²=a²+2ab+b² • (n+1)²−n²=2n+1 • P(square)=4s

Part A

Core fluency — 10 questions

  1. Write the perfect squares from 1² to 15².

  2. Evaluate √64, √121, √196 and √289.

  3. A square has side 18 m. Find its area and write the related square-root statement.

  4. A square has area 324 cm². Find its side length.

  5. Determine between which consecutive integers √70 lies. Justify with two perfect squares.

  6. Determine between which consecutive integers √130 lies.

  7. Calculate 43² using (40+3)².

  8. Calculate 52² using (50+2)².

  9. Which of 338, 361, 390 and 410 is a perfect square? Explain.

  10. List the possible final digits of a perfect square and give one impossible final digit.

Part B

Reasoning — 8 questions

  1. Explain why √100 is 10, not 50.

  2. A student writes 15²=30. Diagnose the misconception and correct it.

  3. A student writes (50+6)²=2500+300+36. Identify the missing structure and calculate 56² correctly.

  4. Explain why (a+b)² is not generally equal to a²+b². Use an area model or numerical example.

  5. Find 13²−12². Then show how the result agrees with 2n+1.

  6. The first differences of 1,4,9,16,25 are 3,5,7,9. Explain why the second difference is 2 and what this tells you about the pattern.

  7. Without calculating a square root, explain why a whole number ending in 8 cannot be a perfect square.

  8. Which perfect square is closest to 730? Explain how you know without testing many numbers.

Part C

Multi-step applications — 6 questions

  1. A square tiled floor contains 576 tiles. Find the side length and perimeter in tile lengths.

  2. A square garden has area 1600 m². Find the side length and metres of fencing needed around it.

  3. A square has perimeter 84 cm. Find its side and area, then verify using a square root.

  4. A square photograph has side 8 cm. Each side is enlarged by a factor of 3. Find the original and new areas and explain the area scale factor.

  5. A 20×20 game board is enlarged to 21×21. How many new cells are added? Solve by subtraction and by the consecutive-square rule.

  6. A school makes a square games area from 900 tiles, each 30 cm by 30 cm. Find tiles per side, physical side length in metres, perimeter, and fencing needed if a 2 m entrance is left open.

Part D

Extension — 4 questions

  1. Calculate 103² efficiently without long multiplication. Explain your strategy.

  2. Two consecutive square numbers differ by 61. Find both square numbers and justify your answer algebraically.

  3. Investigate whether every odd number can be written as the difference of two consecutive square numbers. State and justify your conclusion.

  4. Verify 3²+4²=5². Draw or describe the three square areas and explain how this previews the Pythagorean relationship.

Optional investigation

Design your own square problem

Create a realistic problem involving a square area where the solver must use at least two of these ideas: square root, perimeter, scaling, consecutive-square pattern or two-digit squaring. Solve it fully and explain why your numerical choices make sense.
