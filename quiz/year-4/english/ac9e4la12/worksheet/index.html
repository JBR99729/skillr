Punctuating dialogue

Complete eight original dialogue-writing and editing tasks. An adult checks the actual punctuation and speaker paragraphs.

Open practice

Eight dialogue tasks

Use the space below or separate paper. Model answers use double quotation marks; a consistent single-mark style is also acceptable. Write or dictate the actual marks and paragraph breaks using your usual support. Automatic line wrapping is not a new-speaker paragraph.

Task 1

Read the unpunctuated words: mika said the model has wheels. Rewrite them as one tag-first dialogue sentence, using a capital and paired quotation marks. Explain the job of the comma.

Task 2

Write an original sentence spoken by a character who wants to borrow a pencil. Put the speech before a following speech tag. Explain the punctuation that links them.

Task 3

Correct this draft without changing its words: “Is the paint dry?”, she asked. Explain the correction.

Task 4

Create a short shouted instruction for an invented football scene. Put the speech tag first and use an exclamation mark. Then write a separate action sentence.

Task 5

Write an original two-turn conversation about choosing a story. Make one speaker ask a question and the other answer it. Put each new speaker in a separate paragraph and use speech tags.

Task 6

Write one complete spoken sentence about an imaginary pet. Then interrupt that same sentence with a speech tag, keeping the spoken words unchanged. Label the part that continues after the tag.

Task 7

Edit this draft while keeping all the words: “I have finished,” she closed her notebook. Explain why this is different from a speech tag.

Task 8

Create a four-turn conversation between two invented characters making a paper model. Include one question, one exclamation and one separate action sentence. Start a new paragraph whenever the speaker changes. Explain two punctuation choices.

Examples and adult review guidance

Task 1

Example or expected evidence: Mika said, “The model has wheels.” The comma separates the introducing speech tag from the spoken sentence.

Adult check: Require unchanged words with Mika and The capitalised, a comma after said, paired quotation marks around only the speech and a full stop inside. Accept consistent single or double quotation marks.

Task 2

Example or expected evidence: “May I borrow a pencil?” Ben asked. The question mark is inside the speech quotation; no comma is added before the following tag.

Adult check: Accept a question or polite statement: a question takes a question mark; a statement before a tag takes an inside comma. Require original spoken words, paired quotes, suitable capitals, clear tag and an accurate explanation of the chosen mark.

Task 3

Example or expected evidence: “Is the paint dry?” she asked. Remove the comma after the closing quotation mark; the question mark already punctuates the speech.

Adult check: Require the question mark inside the quotation, no added comma, lower-case she in the continuing tag and full stop after asked. Explanation identifies the unnecessary comma.

Task 4

Example or expected evidence: Lena called, “Pass to the left!” She ran into space.

Adult check: Require original spoken instruction, tag/comma, capital, paired quotes and exclamation inside. The following action must be a separate complete sentence with a capital. Accept safe invented sporting actions.

Task 5

Example or expected evidence: “Which story shall we read?” Amir asked. “Let us try the lighthouse adventure,” Zoe replied.

Adult check: Require two connected original turns, separate paragraphs, matched quotation pairs, a question mark inside the first quotation and correctly attached tags. Accept a range of suitable stories and consistent quotation style.

Task 6

Example or expected evidence: “My dragon likes crunchy carrots.” “My dragon,” Kim said, “likes crunchy carrots.” The continuation is likes crunchy carrots.

Adult check: Require two versions with the same spoken sentence, a tag outside the quotation pairs, commas separating the tag, and a correctly identified continuation. It normally begins lower case, except I or a proper name.

Task 7

Example or expected evidence: “I have finished.” She closed her notebook. Closing a notebook is an action rather than a speech tag, so it begins a new sentence.

Adult check: Require the speech’s full stop inside the closing quotation, capital She and final full stop. Explanation must distinguish the action from a phrase such as she said.

Task 8

Example or expected evidence: “Shall we make a boat?” Aki asked. “Great idea!” Rose replied. “I will fold the paper,” Aki said. He picked up a sheet. “I will draw a flag,” Rose said. Aki’s question mark belongs inside the quotation. The action He picked up a sheet is a new sentence.

Adult check: Require four meaningful original turns with identifiable speaker changes, a question, exclamation, separate complete action and two accurate punctuation explanations. Adult checks actual writing or dictated marks/paragraphs. Accept consistent single or double quotation marks and different grammatical tag placements.
