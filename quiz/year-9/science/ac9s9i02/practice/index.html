Valid and Reproducible Investigations (AC9S9I02)

Practise identifying confounding variables, improving methods and judging error, risk and ethics. Use the worked example, then explain why each planning decision matters.

Plan a valid and reproducible investigation

A valid method tests the intended relationship. A reproducible method gives enough detail for someone else to repeat the investigation and obtain compatible results. Repeating a flawed comparison does not remove its flaw.

Worked example: identify the confounding variable

A group tests whether temperature changes a tablet's dissolving time. It uses a whole tablet in cooler water and a crushed tablet in warmer water. Both temperature and tablet surface area changed, so the difference cannot be attributed to temperature alone.

Repair the comparison: keep tablet mass and form, water volume, stirring and the timing start/end rules consistent while varying temperature. Record actual temperatures and repeated results. Specify the equipment, quantities and steps so another group can follow the method.

Error: identify whether equipment, timing, sampling or an uncontrolled variable could affect the result; choose a correction that addresses that source.

Risk and ethics: assess hazards and agree controls before practical work. Do not treat an online answer as approval to conduct an experiment.

Country/Place: check relevant permissions and heritage responsibilities before planning sampling. Do not collect from or disturb a heritage site or artefact.

Check: would taking more repeats fix the whole-versus-crushed comparison?

Check the explanation

No. Repeats help assess consistency, but both variables still change together. Repair the control of variables first, then repeat the improved method.

Read the investigation-planning lesson for further examples, or use the matching worksheet. This activity checks planning decisions; it does not certify a practical investigation as safe or complete.
