YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3I06

Make the findings clear for your audience

Learning intention: Create texts for an identified purpose and audience, using accurate scientific vocabulary and suitable digital tools.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One investigation, two ways to explain it

Purpose: explain a sleeve investigation to families. In this supplied comparison, equal water volumes in matching cups start at 36°C. After ten minutes, the sleeved cup is at 33°C and the bare cup at 28°C. A family poster can show the table or graph with a short finding. A teacher report can add the detailed procedure and controls. The scientific facts stay the same.

CupStartAfter 10 minTemperature decrease
With sleeve36°C33°C3°C
No sleeve36°C28°C8°C

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Choose purpose, audience and format

State who the communication is for and what it should help them understand. Select a report, poster, labelled diagram, pictorial map or another suitable text.

Audience and purposeHelpful format choice
Families: understand the sleeve findingShort poster with labelled results
Teacher: assess how the test was conductedReport with question, procedure, results and conclusion
Younger students: understand the main ideaSimple language with labelled drawings
Try it · May a simpler family poster change 33°C to 35°C to make the design seem better?

No. Adapt the explanation and layout, while keeping the evidence accurate.

2. Connect the finding to evidence and vocabulary

Name the observation and explain the science using precise words. In this test, water with the sleeve cooled less; insulation can reduce heat transfer to cooler surroundings.

A short report model

We compared matching cups with and without a sleeve for 10 minutes. Both started at 36°C. The water cooled by 3°C with the sleeve and 8°C without it. In this test, the sleeve reduced cooling.

Try it · Does “the sleeve makes heat” accurately explain these findings?

No. The evidence supports reduced heat transfer, not the sleeve producing heat.

3. Use tools to clarify, then check

A digital table, drawing, photograph, graph or recording can help communicate. Check labels, units, values, audience clarity and whether a reader can distinguish results from predictions or further questions.

CheckExample
UnitsTemperature is in °C; duration is in min
Evidence agreementGraph columns show decreases of 3°C and 8°C
Claim limitSay “in this ten-minute test”, not “works forever”
Try it · Does a polished digital graph automatically make a report accurate?

No. Check it against the original observations and the intended message.

Common mistakes

“A scientific report needs complicated language.” Use accurate language the audience can understand; explain important scientific terms.

“Decoration can replace results.” Use visuals that communicate actual observations, with matching labels, units and a clear finding.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create and check a family explanation

2 minutes: identify the audience and purpose. 5 minutes: make a short poster or report using the supplied data and a clear finding. 3 minutes: ask a partner to explain the finding from your text, then improve an unclear label or sentence.

Adjust the challenge Support: dictate sentences and use a provided table. Extend: create two versions for different audiences and explain why their format differs while their evidence agrees.

Use supplied records. Share photographs, audio and video only with adult guidance and appropriate permission; avoid private information.

Three tricky questions

1. The sleeve’s column is shorter. Does that mean its final water temperature was lower?

No. The graph shows temperature decrease. A smaller decrease from the same starting temperature means a higher final temperature here.

2. Can an honest report include a prediction that was not supported?

Yes. Clearly distinguish the original prediction from the observed results and conclusion.

3. Can a factual life-cycle story or pictorial soil map communicate science?

Yes, when it suits the purpose and audience and uses accurate stages, locations, labels and scientific terms.

FAQ

What should a results caption explain?

What was measured or observed, the relevant conditions and units, and the main message supported by the evidence.

Can a spoken recording be useful?

Yes. Choose digital tools that support the purpose, while also creating an appropriate text or representation and keeping the science accurate.

What students learn in AC9S3I06

Students learn to write and create texts to communicate findings and ideas for identified purposes and audiences, using scientific vocabulary and digital tools as appropriate. They use the visual model, precise vocabulary and evidence to explain and apply the concept.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Record → Explain

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: write and create texts to communicate findings and ideas for identified purposes and audiences, using scientific vocabulary and digital tools as appropriate
  • E1: discussing how to construct simple reports of their investigations to share their predictions, methods, results and conclusions with their peers
  • E2: consulting First Nations Australians’ representations of living things as evidenced and communicated through formal and informal sharing of information (teaching context)
  • E3: writing a life-cycle story from the perspective of a living thing, including appropriate scientific terms for life stages
  • E4: creating posters to display around school on the importance of placing compostable lunchtime food scraps such as apple cores in compost bins for use in kitchen gardens
  • E5: collaborating to create a pictorial map of the school grounds showing where different rocks or soils can be found as part of the built or natural environment and creating a class display of rocks and soils collected
  • E6: creating an advertisement to promote a new insulated container design to parents of primary-school-aged children
  • E7: representing heat transfer using diagrams, digital drawings, arrows or labels using scientific vocabulary
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use reliable, community-approved sources and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Do not present diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before reproducing cultural material or inviting community participation.

Common misconceptions
  • Data dumped without message — State the finding and connect it to evidence.
  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
  • One example treated as a rule — Test the idea with another example.
