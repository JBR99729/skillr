YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3H02

Use science to solve a real problem

Learning intention: Connect scientific explanations to practical needs. Use evidence and criteria to compare and improve solutions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Choose a sleeve for a warm-water cup

The design need is to keep a cup’s water at or above 32°C after ten minutes, starting at 36°C in the same room. The cup must also be stable. In the supplied comparison, sleeve B meets both requirements but is heavier. Choosing a solution means checking the criteria and any trade-offs.

DesignAfter 10 minStable?Carrying weight
Sleeve A29°CYesLighter
Sleeve B33°CYesHeavier

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Define the need and criteria

A criterion is something used to judge a solution. Make the important criteria observable or measurable rather than just saying “best”.

NeedCheckable criterion
Keep water warmAt least 32°C after 10 min
Avoid tippingCup remains stable
Try it · Does “looks great” alone show that a sleeve meets the warm-water target?

No. Check temperature and stability.

2. Explain how science informs the design

Insulation can reduce heat transfer. Designers use that explanation to propose a sleeve, then test whether the actual design works.

Need → science → design

Keep water warm → reduce heat transfer to the surroundings → try an insulating sleeve.

Try it · Does calling a material “insulating” guarantee the finished design will work?

No. The design still needs a suitable test.

3. Use evidence to improve

Compare results against the criteria. A design can work well in one respect but need improvement in another.

Design B resultPossible next improvement
Meets temperature and stability criteriaTry reducing weight while keeping those criteria
Try it · Which design meets the temperature criterion in the supplied results?

B, because 33°C is at least 32°C. A at 29°C does not meet it.

Common mistakes

“Choose a product before stating the problem.” Define the need and criteria first.

“A failed test should be hidden.” Record the result and use it to improve the solution.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Recommend and improve a design

2 minutes: read the criteria. 5 minutes: compare both designs and recommend one using the supplied data. 3 minutes: name one trade-off and propose a change to test next.

Adjust the challenge Support: tick each criterion in a comparison table. Extend: propose an improved design and explain how you would check that it still meets the original criteria.

Use supplied records or an adult-approved, comfortably warm setup. Do not drink experimental water or use unstable containers.

Three tricky questions

1. A design is lighter but misses the temperature target. Does lightness erase the failure?

No. Check each criterion separately.

2. Must the first working design be the final design?

No. It may be improved for weight, cost, accessibility or another stated need.

3. Can soil and life-cycle knowledge also help solve practical problems?

Yes. For example, relevant science can guide crop care or pest-management decisions, with suitable local advice.

FAQ

What is a trade-off?

Improving one feature may make another less suitable, so the priorities need to be considered.

Can scientific explanations alone settle every design decision?

No. Evidence, user needs, safety, available materials and other criteria also matter.

What students learn in AC9S3H02

Students learn to consider how people use scientific explanations to meet a need or solve a problem. They use the visual model, precise vocabulary and evidence to explain and apply the concept.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Record → Explain

Success looks like

  • Recognise need
  • Use criterion
  • Explain solution
  • Apply in a new context
  • Justify with evidence

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: consider how people use scientific explanations to meet a need or solve a problem
  • E1: recognising how First Nations Australians observe and describe developmental changes in plants and animals to make decisions about when to harvest certain resources (teaching context)
  • E2: exploring the history of manure and compost use in agriculture and how composting can help improve soil condition and plant growth
  • E3: investigating how understanding of life cycles of insect pests such as fruit flies led to effective control strategies
  • E4: investigating why salt, was so important to people’s diets, food preservation and medicine in ancient times that it was known as ‘white gold’
  • E5: exploring how science knowledge of heat transfer has helped people develop different ways to cook food, such as by boiling, frying or roasting
  • E6: investigating how engineers test the insulation properties of materials, and how this information is used to design food and beverage packaging, building insulation or clothing
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use reliable, community-approved sources and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Do not present diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before reproducing cultural material or inviting community participation.

Common misconceptions
  • Solution chosen before understanding need — Define the problem and criteria first.
  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
  • One example treated as a rule — Test the idea with another example.
