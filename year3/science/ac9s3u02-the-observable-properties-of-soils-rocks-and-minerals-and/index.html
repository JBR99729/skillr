YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3U02

What can Earth materials tell us?

Learning intention: Compare observable properties of soils, rocks and minerals. Explain why these Earth resources matter.

Learn the skill

1. Observe properties carefully

Compare colour, texture, grain or crystal size and other safely observable features. Soil can contain mineral particles, water, air, organic material and living organisms.

SampleUseful observation
RockRough surface with visible grains
SoilDifferent particle sizes and plant fragments
Mineral sampleCrystal shape and surface appearance
Try it · Can colour alone identify every mineral reliably?

No. Different minerals can share a colour. More evidence is needed.

2. Connect rocks and minerals

Rocks are made from one or more minerals. Minerals can contribute to a rock’s visible grains and properties. Soil is a mixture, not one pure mineral.

Part and whole

A speckled rock may contain several different mineral grains. A single mineral and a rock are not always the same thing.

Try it · Does every rock contain only one mineral?

No. Some contain several minerals.

3. Explain why a property is useful

Soil supports plant growth and habitats. Rocks and minerals provide building materials and many useful products. Match a use to a relevant property, while considering care for places and resources.

ResourceExample use
SoilGrowing crops and providing habitat
Hard, durable rockSuitable building material
Graphite mineralMaking marks in pencils
Try it · Does soil A’s greater drainage prove it is best for every garden plant?

No. Different plants have different needs; drainage is only one property to consider.

Common mistakes

“All soils are the same.” Compare their particles, organic material and water behaviour.

“A rock and a mineral mean exactly the same thing.” A rock can be made from several minerals.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make a property-and-use card

2 minutes: an adult supplies safe samples or the illustrated examples. 5 minutes: record two observable properties and suggest a use linked to one property. 3 minutes: explain what further evidence would help choose a material.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose from property words. Extend: identify a trade-off, such as drainage versus retaining water for a particular plant.

Do not taste samples or inhale dust. Use adult-approved specimens, wash hands and obtain permission before collecting. Never use collected drainage water for drinking.

Three tricky questions

1. A shiny sample is attractive. Is that enough reason to use it for a bridge?

No. The use needs relevant evidence such as strength and durability.

2. Does the soil illustration prove which sample drains fastest?

No. Use the supplied investigation records; a picture does not measure drainage.

3. Should a culturally important stone be taken for a classroom collection without permission?

No. Respect Country, ownership, cultural authority and collecting rules.

FAQ

What is an Earth resource?

A material from Earth that living things depend on or people use.

Can a soil contain living and non-living parts?

Yes. It may contain organisms as well as mineral particles, air, water and dead organic material.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: compare the observable properties of soils, rocks and minerals and investigate why they are important Earth resources
  • E1: examining different soils from local areas and using magnifying glasses to observe their components, such as pebbles, sand or plant matter as well as living things such as earthworms and insects
  • E2: exploring the school grounds or a local area and observing or collecting different types of rocks and describing similarities or differences such as texture, colour, grain or crystal size
  • E3: recognising that minerals are the building blocks of rocks and that the different characteristics of rocks depend on the minerals they are made up of
  • E4: identifying rocks as key components of the built and natural environment and recognising uses of minerals such as gemstones in jewellery, graphite in pencils, and table salt in food
  • E5: investigating First Nations Australians’ knowledges of different rock and mineral types, and how they were used such as for stone blades, grindstones and pigments (teaching context)
  • E6: describing ways in which living things including humans depend on soils, such as for food, growing plants, providing habitat for organisms, and holding and cleaning water
  • E7: examining information on plant tags and exploring the vocabulary used to describe soils and different plant soil requirements
  • E8: investigating which rocks or minerals are quarried or mined locally or regionally and how those resources are used
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use reliable, community-approved sources and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Do not present diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before reproducing cultural material or inviting community participation.

Common misconceptions
  • All soil is the same — Soil samples can differ greatly in particles, organic matter and water movement.
  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
  • One example treated as a rule — Test the idea with another example.
