Solids, liquids and changes of state

Learning intention: Compare solids and liquids using observable properties. Explain melting and freezing through adding or removing heat energy.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A new shape is not always a new state

A paper sheet can fold without melting. Ice can melt into liquid water when it gains enough heat energy. Shape change alone does not identify melting: look at whether the material has changed from solid to liquid.

ActionObservationChange of state?
Fold paperStill a solid sheetNo
Melt iceSolid ice becomes liquid waterYes
Freeze waterLiquid water becomes solid iceYes

Learn the skill

1. Compare observable properties

A solid usually keeps its own shape. A liquid flows and takes the shape of the part of its container it occupies. A liquid still has an amount and takes up space.

MaterialUseful evidence
Ice blockKeeps a shape unless it melts or is broken
Liquid waterFlows when poured and settles in the container
Try it · Does water have no volume because it changes shape when poured?

No. It still takes up space and has a measurable volume.

2. Add heat energy: melting

Melting is a change from solid to liquid. A material can gain heat energy from warmer surroundings; melting does not require a visible flame.

Melting model

Solid ice + heat energy gained → liquid water.

Try it · Is cutting a solid into smaller pieces the same as melting it?

No. The pieces can remain solid.

3. Remove heat energy: freezing

When water loses enough heat energy in suitable conditions, it freezes. Ice and liquid water are the same substance in different states.

Freezing model

Liquid water − heat energy transferred away → solid ice.

Try it · Does water disappear when it becomes ice?

No. It changes state. Its shape and volume may change, but it has not vanished.

Common mistakes

“Every liquid fills the entire container.” It takes the shape of the part it occupies, rather than expanding to fill all empty space like a gas.

“Melting destroys the material.” Track the material before and after; melted ice is liquid water.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Sort actions and explain the energy change

2 minutes: read the action table. 5 minutes: make labelled solid → liquid and liquid → solid models. 3 minutes: explain why folding paper does not belong on either state-change arrow.

Adjust the challenge Support: use ice/water picture cards. Extend: explain how a sealed, undamaged bag can help keep track of the water as it changes state.

Use supplied examples or adult-supervised ice observations. An adult handles freezers and any warming equipment. Do not heat wax, glass or plastics yourself; avoid spills.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a tiny grain of sand be a solid even if a pile pours?

Yes. Each grain remains a solid; many grains can move past each other.

2. Must melting need a cooker?

No. Warmer surroundings can transfer heat energy to ice.

3. Can we assume freezing water keeps exactly the same volume?

No. Water expands when it freezes; do not use a full sealed rigid container.

FAQ

Is every physical change reversible?

No. Melting and freezing water can be reversed, but tearing a sheet is a different kind of physical change.

Are particle calculations needed here?

No. Focus on observable properties, heat-energy transfer and the solid/liquid state change.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: investigate the observable properties of solids and liquids and how adding or removing heat energy leads to a change of state
  • E1: observing the properties of substances and classifying them as solids (that hold their shape) or liquids (that fill the bottom of containers)
  • E2: investigating ice melting or water freezing in a sealed bag and explaining their observations
  • E3: using ice cubes, butter or chocolate to explore how changes of state involve the removal of heat or the addition of heat
  • E4: investigating how changes of state in materials used by First Nations Australians such as beeswax or resins are important for their use (teaching context)
  • E5: exploring how changes from solid to liquid and liquid to solid can help us recycle materials such as glass or plastics
