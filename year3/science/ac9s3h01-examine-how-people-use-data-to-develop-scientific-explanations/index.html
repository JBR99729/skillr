YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3H01

Use data to build an explanation

Learning intention: Explain how people use observations and measurements to develop and check scientific explanations.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Why did one cup cool less?

A class compares matching cups holding equal volumes of water at the same starting temperature. One has a fabric sleeve and the other does not. In the same room after ten minutes, the wrapped cup has cooled less. The data support an explanation that the sleeve reduced heat transfer in this setup.

CupStartAfter 10 minTemperature decrease
Fabric sleeve36°C33°C3°C
No sleeve36°C28°C8°C

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Find the relevant data

Data are recorded observations or measurements. A preference about which sleeve looks nicer is different from temperature evidence.

StatementRole
Wrapped cup fell by 3°CRecorded numerical evidence
I like the blue sleeveA preference
Try it · Which better supports a claim about cooling: colour preference or temperature measurements?

The temperature measurements.

2. Connect claim, evidence and reasoning

State what the records show, cite the values and explain the scientific connection. Do not simply repeat a result and call it a cause.

A supported explanation

The sleeve reduced cooling in this test: the wrapped cup dropped 3°C compared with 8°C without a sleeve. Insulation can reduce heat transfer to the cooler surroundings.

Try it · Does one comparison prove every fabric works equally well?

No. The evidence is limited to the material and conditions tested.

3. Check, compare and revise

People strengthen explanations by checking methods, repeating observations and comparing other evidence. Unexpected observations should be retained and investigated.

Evidence issueUseful response
Another trial gives a different resultCompare setup and measurement procedure
A measurement was not recordedMark it missing; do not invent it
Try it · Can a scientific explanation change when better data become available?

Yes. Revising an explanation in response to evidence is part of science.

Common mistakes

“An opinion is enough evidence.” Use observations relevant to the scientific claim.

“Repeating a claim is explaining it.” Connect the claim to specific evidence and scientific reasoning.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a claim–evidence–reasoning explanation

2 minutes: compare the two temperature decreases. 5 minutes: write a claim, quote the relevant values and explain the heat-transfer connection. 3 minutes: name an observation that would make you check the explanation again.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “The data show… because…”. Extend: suggest another trial and say which parts of the explanation it could test.

Use supplied records. An adult supervises any real water-temperature comparison; experimental water is not for drinking.

Three tricky questions

1. Should an unexpected reading be deleted to make the explanation neat?

No. Keep the reading and investigate its cause.

2. A wrapped cup starts warmer than the bare cup. Is final temperature alone a clear comparison?

No. Different starting temperatures can confuse the explanation.

3. Can a labelled drawing be scientific data?

Yes, if it records relevant observations accurately and includes the needed context.

FAQ

What is reasoning?

The explanation of why the evidence supports a claim.

Do scientists only use numbers?

No. Relevant descriptions, images and other systematic observations can also be evidence.

What students learn in AC9S3H01

Students learn to examine how people use data to develop scientific explanations. They use the visual model, precise vocabulary and evidence to explain and apply the concept.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Record → Explain

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: examine how people use data to develop scientific explanations
  • E1: investigating the stories of people who used multiple observations to develop scientific explanations, such as 17th-century entomologist and naturalist Maria Sibylla Merian, who documented insect metamorphosis through detailed observations
  • E2: exploring how farmers use soil tests to monitor and manage the health of farms
  • E3: investigate how 18th-century physicists such as Jean Ingenhousz and Sir Benjamin Thompson collected data on conduction of heat to determine the best conductors or insulators
  • E4: exploring age-appropriate science reports and journal articles and identifying where in the text the author has included data, findings or explanations
  • E5: viewing a documentary or webinar and observing how scientists and researchers share their data and explanations

Common misconceptions
  • Opinion used as evidence — Scientific evidence comes from relevant observations or measurements.
  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
  • One example treated as a rule — Test the idea with another example.
