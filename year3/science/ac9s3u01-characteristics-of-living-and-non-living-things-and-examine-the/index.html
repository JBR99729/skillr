YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3U01

Living things change through life cycles

Learning intention: Compare living and non-living characteristics. Explain similarities and differences between plant and animal life cycles.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A living seedling, a dead leaf and a stone

The seedling is living: it takes in resources and grows through life processes. The dried fallen leaf was part of a living plant but is now dead. The stone was never alive. Movement alone is not enough evidence: a wind-up toy can move without being living.

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Use several characteristics

Living things need resources and energy, grow and develop, and belong to kinds that reproduce. Not every living individual reproduces or shows every characteristic at every moment.

Evidence check

A toy moves, but it does not grow through life processes. A dormant viable seed can be living even when no movement is visible.

Try it · Does not moving prove that a plant is non-living?

No. Movement from place to place is not a requirement for a plant to be living.

2. Follow a flowering plant’s cycle

A bean seed can germinate into a seedling. It grows into a mature plant that can flower and produce new seeds. New seeds begin another generation.

  1. Seed
  2. Germination and seedling
  3. Mature flowering plant
  4. Fruit containing new seeds
Try it · Does the adult bean plant turn back into its original seed?

No. It can produce new seeds. The cycle continues through new plants.

3. Compare animal life cycles

A butterfly changes greatly during metamorphosis. A human develops from baby to child to adult without a caterpillar or pupa stage. Plants and animals both grow and can produce a new generation, but their stages differ.

OrganismExample stages
ButterflyEgg → caterpillar → pupa → adult; adults can lay eggs
HumanBaby → child → adult; adults may have babies
Try it · Does every animal pass through a pupa stage?

No. Butterflies do, but humans and many other animals do not.

Common mistakes

“Anything that moves is living.” Use several life characteristics. Toys, clouds and water can move without being organisms.

“All life cycles have the same stages.” Compare the actual plant or animal. Do not add butterfly stages to every animal.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build and compare two cycles

2 minutes: classify the seedling and stone using a reason. 5 minutes: arrange the supplied bean and butterfly stages into two cycles. 3 minutes: explain one shared feature and one difference.

Adjust the challenge Support: use stage cards and read them aloud. Extend: compare butterfly development with human development and explain why the stages differ.

Observe without harming organisms. Do not disturb nests or remove living things to make a model.

Three tricky questions

1. A viable seed has not sprouted today. Must it be dead?

No. It may be dormant. Suitable observations and conditions are needed.

2. A sponge gets bigger when it absorbs water. Does that alone prove it is living?

No. Swelling with water is not the same as biological growth.

3. Does a caterpillar become a different species when it becomes a butterfly?

No. It is a different life stage of the same organism.

FAQ

What does once living mean here?

Material that was living, or part of a living organism, but is now dead, such as a dead fallen leaf.

Can local First Nations knowledge support life-cycle learning?

Yes. Use accurate, community-authorised knowledge from particular Peoples and Countries, respecting restrictions on sharing.

What students learn in AC9S3U01

Students learn to compare characteristics of living and non-living things and examine the differences between the life cycles of plants and animals. They use the visual model, precise vocabulary and evidence to explain and apply the concept.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Record → Explain

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: compare characteristics of living and non-living things and examine the differences between the life cycles of plants and animals
  • E1: classifying a collection of objects as living, once living or non-living and explaining their reasoning
  • E2: observing and describing differences between metamorphic (such as butterflies, beetles or frogs) and non-metamorphic life cycles of animals, including humans
  • E3: comparing the physical characteristics of an animal such as a frog or moth with its activity at different stages of its life cycle
  • E4: representing stages of a plant or animal’s life cycle using drawings, digital photographs, graphic organisers or concrete materials
  • E5: investigating how First Nations Australians understand and utilise the life cycles of certain species (teaching context)
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Common misconceptions

  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
