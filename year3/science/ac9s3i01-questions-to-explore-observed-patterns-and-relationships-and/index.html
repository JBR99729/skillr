YEAR 3 SCIENCE · AC9S3I01

Ask a question the evidence can answer

Learning intention: Pose questions about observed patterns and relationships. Make predictions supported by observations.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Will the drainage pattern repeat?

Earlier fair comparisons used matching setups, equal soil volumes and 60 mL of added water. After two minutes, soil A allowed more water to collect below than soil C in both trials. A useful new question asks whether that relationship will repeat under similar conditions.

Earlier trialWater collected from AWater collected from C
146 mL25 mL
248 mL24 mL

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Describe the observed pattern

Name the compared conditions and measured outcome. Read all recorded trials, including variation.

Pattern in these records

A allowed more water to collect than C in both trials; the exact volumes varied.

Try it · Do these records show exactly the same A result each time?

No. A gave 46 mL and 48 mL.

2. Turn the pattern into a testable question

State what you will compare and what you will observe or measure. Clarify vague words such as “better”.

Vague questionClearer investigation question
Which soil is best?Which sample lets more water drain through in 2 min under the same setup?
Try it · Would asking which soil colour you prefer test the drainage pattern?

No. Preference is different from measured drainage.

3. Predict and give the evidence

Predict before testing. Link the expected outcome to the earlier observations without guaranteeing an exact value.

Prediction frame

If we repeat the same comparison, A may allow more water to drain than C, because that happened in both earlier trials.

Try it · Does the evidence guarantee A will give exactly 48 mL next time?

No. The earlier volumes varied, and the next trial may vary too.

Common mistakes

“A prediction is a result already measured.” Keep the predicted outcome separate from the later observation.

“Any reason is relevant evidence.” Use the observations connected to the outcome being predicted.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Improve a question and justify a prediction

2 minutes: read both trials. 5 minutes: improve “Which soil is better?” and write a prediction with a reason. 3 minutes: identify a condition that should stay similar when the question is tested.

Adjust the challenge Support: use compare/measure sentence frames. Extend: suggest a different question that uses the same samples but needs a different measurement.

Use supplied records. Any real soil work requires adult-approved samples and handwashing; never taste soil or drainage water.

Three tricky questions

1. If a third trial differs, was asking the question pointless?

No. Keep the result; it may lead to a better explanation or further question.

2. Does a picture of dark soil alone support a precise drainage prediction?

No. Use relevant observations and measurements.

3. Can a question be explored by repeated observation instead of changing something?

Yes. For example, observing stages of an organism’s life cycle can answer some questions.

FAQ

Must a question contain a scientific word?

No. It needs a clear, observable meaning. Use precise vocabulary where it helps.

What is the difference between a question and a prediction?

The question asks what to find out. The prediction states what you think may happen and why.

What students learn in AC9S3I01

Students learn to pose questions to explore observed patterns and relationships and make predictions based on observations. They use the visual model, precise vocabulary and evidence to explain and apply the concept.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Record → Explain

Success looks like

  • Recognise observation
  • Use testable question
  • Explain prediction
  • Apply in a new context
  • Justify with evidence

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: pose questions to explore observed patterns and relationships and make predictions based on observations
  • E1: acknowledging and using information from First Nations Australians to guide the development of questions regarding life cycles (teaching context)
  • E2: posing questions about the relationship between soil characteristics and the growth of particular plants, such as: ‘Will beans grow best in sandy, loamy or clay soils?’
  • E3: comparing simple maps of Australian agriculture and soil types and posing questions about observed patterns, such as: ‘Does wheat grow in particular soils?’
  • E4: posing questions about substances that are difficult to classify as a solid or liquid, such as toothpaste, slime or hair gel
  • E5: predicting whether the mass of ice in a sealed container will change when the ice has melted
  • E6: predicting which material will be the most effective insulator of heat
  • E7: predicting how quickly ice will melt at different ambient temperatures based on previous observations
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use reliable, community-approved sources and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Do not present diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before reproducing cultural material or inviting community participation.

Common misconceptions
  • Question is opinion-based — Use an observable or measurable outcome.
  • Feature named without effect — Explain how the feature changes meaning or outcome.
  • One example treated as a rule — Test the idea with another example.
