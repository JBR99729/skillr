Choose verbs for different kinds of meaning AC9E3LA07

Learning intention: I can explain how verbs tell about doing, saying, thinking, feeling and relating.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Verbs let us see events and know thoughts

Read this original story: “Mila lifted the lid. ‘It is empty,’ she whispered. She remembered the missing seeds and loved the idea of finding them. The box had a loose hinge.” Lifted tells an action; whispered tells how words were spoken; remembered tells thinking; loved tells feeling. Is relates it to empty, and had relates the box to something it possessed. The verbs give different kinds of information.

Learn the skill

1. Follow doing and saying in a story

Doing verbs tell what happens or what a character does. Saying verbs tell that someone communicates words. Choose a precise verb and explain what it contributes in this clause.

In the story, lifted shows Mila’s action with the lid. Whispered is a saying verb: it tells us she spoke quietly. Compare “Mila said, ‘It is empty’” with “Mila shouted, ‘It is empty’”. The words stay the same, but shouted suggests a louder delivery; the reason for it still needs context.

Try it · In “Jo pushed the gate. ‘Come in,’ Jo called”, find a doing verb and a saying verb. What does each tell us?

Pushed tells what Jo did to the gate. Called tells us Jo spoke the invitation, with a voice intended to reach someone. Do not decide Jo is angry from called alone; context matters.

2. Notice thinking and feeling

Some verbs let the reader know a character’s thoughts or feelings, even when these cannot be seen directly. Read the surrounding words to decide the kind of meaning being expressed.

“Ari remembered the old path” tells a thought from memory. “Ari disliked the loud bell” tells a feeling about it. These are sensing meanings. Neither has to name a visible movement. A face may give a clue, but these verbs tell the reader directly about the inner experience.

Try it · In “Nia believed the story. She loved its friendly dragon”, which verb tells thinking and which tells feeling?

Believed tells what Nia thought was true. Loved tells her feeling about the dragon. The sentence context makes the meanings clear. Some verbs can be discussed in more than one way in other contexts; explain the meaning here.

3. Use relating verbs to describe and define

Relating verbs connect a subject with what it is, what it is like or what it has. They are useful in descriptions and definitions. Look at the whole clause: is and has can also help other verbs.

“A triangle is a shape with three straight sides.” Is relates triangle to a definition. “The model bird has a long beak.” Has relates the model bird to a feature. Compare “The bird is hopping”: is helps hopping; the whole group describes doing.

Try it · In “The toy boats are small. Each boat has a sail. The boats are moving”, identify the relating verbs and the whole doing verb group.

Are in “are small” relates the boats to their size. Has relates each boat to a sail. Are moving is the whole group telling an action; are is helping moving there. Classify the meaning of the clause, not a word on its own.

Common mistakes

“A verb always means an action I can act out.” Thinking, feeling and relating verbs also carry meaning: remembered, loved and is are verbs in the model.

“Has is always a relating verb.” Has relates possession in “She has a bag”. In “She has packed”, it helps packed. Read the whole group.

“A louder saying verb proves anger.” A character might shout to be heard over a crowd. Use the whole story before deciding the feeling.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Tell a small story with five meanings

Use the complete story and verb cards in More examples. Sort each highlighted verb by its meaning in that clause, then explain the information it gives. Write your own three or four connected sentences about finding a lost pencil. Include doing, saying, thinking, feeling and relating meanings; more than one can appear in a sentence.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare a doing clause with a relating clause using the given cards. Core: explain all five meanings in the supplied story and use them in your own writing. Stretch: change one saying or doing verb and explain the new detail without inventing an unsupported feeling.

Use invented characters and feelings; learners need not disclose personal experiences. An adult may read the text or scribe the learner’s own clauses.

Three tricky questions

1. Can “is” be a complete verb?

Yes: “The box is empty” uses is to relate the box to a description. It does not need an action verb.

2. Are “think” and “thinking” always the whole verb group?

No. In “Jo was thinking”, keep was with thinking when naming the whole group.

3. Must one word always keep one process label?

No. Meaning depends on its use in a clause. Give a reason from this sentence; use a clearer example if a classification is uncertain.

FAQ

Why choose a precise verb?

It can add useful information: crept suggests a different movement from ran. Choose the one that matches your intended event.

What does relating mean?

It connects someone or something to an identity, description or possession, as in “The box is empty” and “It has a lid”.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand how verbs represent different processes for doing, feeling, thinking, saying and relating. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include clause, character.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Doing and saying in a complete scene

Read: “Jo opened the gate. ‘Come in,’ Jo whispered.” Opened tells what Jo did to the gate; whispered tells how Jo spoke the invitation. Compare “Jo shouted, ‘Come in.’” Shouted is also a saying verb, but suggests louder delivery. The verb does not tell us why Jo spoke loudly.

Thinking and feeling

Read: “Jo remembered the ending. Jo liked the story.” Remembered gives a thinking meaning: Jo recalls something. Liked gives a feeling meaning: Jo has a positive response to the story. Compare “Jo loved the story.” Loved usually expresses a stronger positive feeling here; liked and loved both tell feeling. No knowledge of the story’s events is needed to explain these verbs.

Relating a subject to a description or feature

Read these complete sentences: “The brilliant new playground is exciting.” “The playground opened on Monday.” “It has two slides.” Is relates the playground to a description. Has relates it to a feature it possesses. Opened tells an event. Brilliant and exciting contribute descriptions, but is is the relating verb in the first sentence. Explain what each verb tells us in its clause.

Read the whole verb group in context

Compare “The gardener is quiet” and “The gardener is digging beside the fence”. Find the relating verb in the first clause and the whole doing verb group in the second. Check: is relates the gardener to a description in the first clause; is digging is the whole group describing an action in the second. Is helps digging there. The meaning depends on the complete clause, not is alone.

A complete story with all five verb meanings

“Fen opened the box. ‘Here it is,’ Fen said. Fen remembered the missing key and loved its shiny ribbon. The key had a round handle.”

Find opened, said, remembered, loved and had. Explain the different information each gives. Is in the quoted speech also relates it to here in this context.

Check and explain

Opened: doing, an action with the box. Said: saying, a spoken message. Remembered: thinking, a memory. Loved: feeling about its ribbon. Had: relating, a feature possessed by the key. These are different kinds of meaning in these exact clauses.

Use the earlier evaluation examples to inspect verbs

The earlier examples also contain verbs. In “I liked the story”, liked expresses a feeling. In “The playground opened on Monday”, opened presents an event. In “The playground is exciting”, is relates the playground to a description. The words brilliant and exciting evaluate the playground but are not whole verb groups in that description.

Complete fresh comparison: “Jo whispered, ‘The gate is open.’” Change whispered to shouted. Both are saying verbs. Shouted changes the indicated delivery; it does not establish Jo’s reason or feeling. For the earlier stronger/weaker judgement task, compare “I liked the ending” and “I loved the ending”. Both express a positive feeling, with loved usually stronger in this context. Accept a supported explanation of a different intended feeling.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.

A concrete quick correction

In “Fen remembered the key”, remembered is a verb even though it does not name visible movement. In “The key has a round handle”, has relates a feature to the key. Naming the verb is a start; explain the particular information it gives.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

A fresh five-meaning check

“Sam carried the tray. ‘It is heavy,’ Sam said. Sam remembered the spilled water and disliked the slippery floor. The tray had two handles.” Find a verb for doing, saying, thinking, feeling and relating. Explain the information each gives.

Check and explain

Carried: doing, the movement of the tray. Said: saying, the spoken comment. Remembered: thinking, a memory. Disliked: feeling about the floor. Had: relating, possession of handles. Is also relates it to heavy. Accept either clear relating example. Use meanings in the actual clauses.

Earlier worked model

Which word tells what the gardener does? The gardener digs beside the fence.

Answer: digs

Digs is the verb. It tells the gardener's action.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E3LA07: understand how verbs represent different processes for doing, feeling, thinking, saying and relating

  • exploring “doing” and “saying” verbs in narrative texts to understand how they give information about what characters do and say
  • exploring the use of sensing verbs and how they allow readers to understand what characters think and feel; for example, “He remembered his first day at school.”
  • exploring the use of relating verbs in constructing definitions and descriptions; for example, identifying the relating verb “is” or “are”, “has” or “have” in descriptions of animals
  • identifying different types of verbs and the way they control meaning in a clause
