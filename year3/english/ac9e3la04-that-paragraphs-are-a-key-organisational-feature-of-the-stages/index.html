YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Use paragraphs to group and develop ideas AC9E3LA04

Learning intention: I can explain how paragraphs group related information, improve their order and compare informative and narrative paragraph choices.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

An opening gives the details a shared focus

“Our model garden has a clear layout. A wide path runs between the beds. Labels name the plants beside each bed. A sign by the gate shows the route.” The first sentence introduces the layout; the other sentences develop it with a path, labels and a route sign. “The sign is by the gate” would introduce only one narrow detail. A useful topic sentence prepares us for the whole paragraph.

Learn the skill

1. Group information and develop the topic

A paragraph groups related information. In an informative text, a topic sentence often comes first and predicts the focus. Supporting sentences explain, describe or give examples of that focus. Read all the details before deciding whether an opening is too narrow, too broad or suitable.

Model A: “Our class display is easy to explore. Each model has a clear label. A wide gap lets visitors move between tables. Arrows show the route.” All details explain ease of exploring. Model B: “Our model box protects the contents. Thick sides surround the models. Folded paper separates them. A lid keeps loose pieces inside.” All details explain protection. The paragraphs develop different topics even though both concern models.

Try it · Details: “Two handles make the basket easy to lift. A wide opening lets us reach the books. A flat base keeps it steady.” Choose an opening: A “Our book basket is useful.” B “Our basket has two handles.” C “All baskets are wonderful.” Explain using all three details.

A is best supported: the handles, opening and base each explain a useful feature of this basket. B covers only the handles. C claims something about every basket that these details do not show. Other accurate openings with a similar whole-paragraph focus are valid.

2. Order and repair paragraphs for the reader

Choose which details belong together, then arrange them so readers can follow references and explanations. Introduce a thing before using it or its when needed for clarity. Move an unrelated detail to a suitable paragraph instead of deleting useful information without a reason. A paragraph break should mark an idea or stage, not simply a fixed number of sentences.

Draft: “The class display has three models. They stand on a low table. Our labels use large writing. Each label names a model. It is wide enough for visitors to see all three.” The final it is unclear. Repair: “The class display has three models. They stand on a low table. The table is wide enough for visitors to see all three.” New paragraph: “Our labels use large writing. Each label names a model.” The repair groups arrangement details separately from label details and names the table. Contrast: placing every sentence in a new paragraph would hide these useful groups.

Try it · Arrange these sentences into two paragraphs and explain the groups: A “Each pot has a plant name beside it.” B “Our pots have clear labels.” C “The watering can is kept by the door.” D “Our watering tools are easy to find.” E “A jug stands beside the can.” F “The names help us identify the plants.”

One clear arrangement is B, A, F for the label paragraph, then D, C, E for the tools paragraph. The first sentence in each group introduces its topic; the details develop it. C before E identifies the can before the reference the can. Reversing the two whole paragraphs may also work; explain a useful order rather than insist on one unsupported order for independent topics.

3. Notice narrative paragraph choices

Narrative paragraphs can mark a new event, time, place, speaker or moment of emphasis. They vary in length and do not always begin with a topic sentence that announces the idea. Look at the actual break and explain what changes there. A one-sentence paragraph can be purposeful; length alone does not decide quality.

Narrative model: “At the display table, Isla searched for her paper bird. She looked behind the box and under the cloth.” New paragraph: “A soft rustle came from the doorway.” New paragraph: “Isla hurried over. Her paper bird was caught beside the door. She lifted it carefully and carried it back.” The short middle paragraph gives the sound its own moment, before the action shifts to the doorway. Contrast the informative model “Our box protects the models. Thick sides surround them.” Its opening predicts supporting information; the narrative does not follow that same formula.

Try it · Read these narrative paragraphs: “At lunchtime, Ravi left his model on the classroom shelf. He planned to add its label later.” New paragraph: “After lunch, the shelf was empty.” New paragraph: “Ravi asked the teacher. She had moved the model to the display table.” Explain what changes at the first break and why the short middle paragraph may be useful.

The first break marks a time change from lunchtime to after lunch and a new problem. The short middle paragraph puts attention on the missing model; the next paragraph gives the search and explanation. It does not need an informative opening such as “This paragraph is about a missing model.” Other reasonable effect explanations are valid when linked to the actual break.

Common mistakes

“Every paragraph must have three sentences.” Paragraph length depends on purpose and the amount of related information. Narrative paragraphs can be very short.

“A topic sentence repeats one detail.” It should prepare the reader for the paragraph’s whole focus. Check it against all supporting details.

“A new line after every sentence creates good paragraphs.” Group related sentences and use breaks for meaningful changes. Blank lines help show the groups but cannot organise unrelated ideas for you.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build two paragraphs and explain one break

Use the complete sorting and narrative cards in More examples. Group the information sentences into two focused paragraphs, choose an opening for each and read them back. Repair any unclear reference. Then read the supplied short narrative and explain one paragraph break using its time, event or emphasis change.

Adjust the challenge Starter: an adult reads and displays sentence cards; sort two clear topics, then choose an opening from two options. Core: arrange and explain two paragraphs and one narrative break. Stretch: offer another workable narrative break or paragraph order, preserving meaning, and explain the effect on the reader.

Use cut-up copies or point to sentence letters without cutting. Allow dictation or oral explanation and note the support. Judge grouping and reasoning rather than handwriting speed or a fixed paragraph length.

Three tricky questions

1. Does every narrative paragraph begin with a topic sentence?

No. A narrative may begin with a sound, action or speech. Its paragraph choices can organise events or create emphasis without announcing a main idea.

2. Must independent information paragraphs always go in one order?

No. Sometimes either order is useful. Follow any stated purpose or reference links, and explain your choice.

3. Should I delete an unrelated but useful sentence?

It may fit a different paragraph. Move it to the matching group when that preserves useful information and improves the organisation.

FAQ

What makes a supporting detail useful?

It develops the paragraph’s focus by explaining, describing, giving a reason or offering an example.

How do I check a paragraph repair?

Read each paragraph as a group. Name its focus, check every sentence belongs and make sure references and sequence remain clear.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand that paragraphs are a key organisational feature of the stages of written texts, grouping related information together. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include paragraph, sentence.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Structure follows purpose

A procedure uses ordered steps because sequence matters; a story may organise events to build tension; an information report groups related facts under headings.

Cohesion across sentences

Compare “Mia found a shell. Mia washed the shell.” with “Mia found a shell. She washed it.” The pronoun connects ideas without unnecessary repetition.

Paragraph focus

A paragraph is easier to follow when its sentences develop one main idea rather than jumping between unrelated points.

Transfer

Use the complete mixed-up text below. Make a useful organisation or connection change, then explain exactly how it helps the reader.

Complete mixed-up text for the retained transfer task

Draft: “Our seed pots have name labels. The labels stand beside each plant. Our tools hang by the door. Large writing makes the labels easy to read. A watering can stands under the tools.”

Choose a useful paragraph break or move, make it, and explain how it helps.

One repair groups the first, second and fourth sentences as labels, then groups the third and fifth as tools: “Our seed pots have name labels. The labels stand beside each plant. Large writing makes the labels easy to read.” New paragraph: “Our tools hang by the door. A watering can stands under the tools.” The related information stays together; no facts need to be removed.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

Fresh check · group and open two paragraphs

Sentence cards: A “A tray catches loose paper.” B “Our art shelf keeps materials in groups.” C “A pot holds the pencils.” D “Our display table gives models space.” E “Wide gaps separate the models.” F “A clear edge keeps the route open.”

Make two paragraphs, choose their openings and explain why the details belong together.

One arrangement is B, C, A for grouped art materials, and D, E, F for the display’s space and route. B and D introduce the corresponding topics. A/C can swap without harming the meaning. Either whole-paragraph order is acceptable when justified; do not impose an arbitrary order.

Fresh check · repair mixed groups

Draft: “Our tool box is easy to carry. It has two strong handles. Our signs use large writing. Each sign names a display. A light lid covers the box.”

Repair the paragraph grouping while keeping all the useful information.

For example: “Our tool box is easy to carry. It has two strong handles. A light lid covers the box.” New paragraph: “Our signs use large writing. Each sign names a display.” The box details and sign details now stay in useful groups. The box is introduced before It; all supplied information is retained.

Fresh check · narrative breaks

Paragraph 1: “Before school, Bea placed a paper boat on the window ledge. She left to fetch its label.” Paragraph 2: “When she came back, the boat had vanished.” Paragraph 3: “Bea looked down. The boat lay on the floor below the open window. She picked it up and closed the window.”

Explain one reason for the first break and one possible effect of the short middle paragraph.

The first break marks a time change and introduces the missing-boat problem. The short middle paragraph gives that problem emphasis before the next paragraph develops the search and recovery. It does not need the same topic-and-support pattern as an informative paragraph.

Earlier worked model

Choose the opening for this paragraph: “[blank]. There are soft cushions near the window. A low lamp lights the shelves. A small rug makes the floor comfortable.”

Answer: Our reading corner is a comfortable place.

The soft cushions, lighting and rug all develop comfort in this reading corner, so the opening gives the paragraph a shared focus.
