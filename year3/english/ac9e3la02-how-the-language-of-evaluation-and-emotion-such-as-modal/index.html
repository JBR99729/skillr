YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Choose how strongly to express a view or feeling AC9E3LA02

Learning intention: I can explain how words change a message’s certainty, requirement, judgement or emotion, and choose words that fit the context.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A requirement and a possibility do different jobs

A class rule says, “You must wear shoes in the workshop.” Here, must states a requirement. A weather prediction says, “It might rain at lunchtime.” Here, might expresses a possibility. “You might wear shoes in the workshop” would weaken the rule into a possibility or suggestion, so it would not communicate the same requirement. Read the whole situation before explaining how forceful a word is.

Learn the skill

1. Read modal verbs in context

Modal verbs such as must, might and could help express requirements, possibilities and other meanings. Ask what the speaker is doing: requiring an action, suggesting it or predicting what may happen. Compare versions of the same message in a stated context. There is no single strength ladder that explains every use of every modal.

Model A, a rule: “You must put the lid on the paint pot” makes the action required; “You could put the lid on the paint pot” offers it as a possibility or suggestion. Model B, a prediction: “The new lid might stop the paint drying out” leaves uncertainty; “The new lid will stop the paint drying out” presents the result more confidently. In “You must be tired after that long walk”, must expresses a strong conclusion, not an order to become tired.

Try it · The teacher is offering a choice of drawing materials, not making a rule: “You ___ use a pencil.” Choose could or must and explain. Then compare “The parcel might arrive today” with “The parcel will arrive today” as predictions.

Could suits the offered choice; must would make using a pencil sound required. Might presents arrival as possible, while will presents it more confidently. These are claims made by the speaker, not proof that the parcel will arrive. Context determines the intended meaning.

2. Notice how a narrator evaluates a character

An evaluation is a judgement or impression. Nouns can label a character, verbs can present how an action is done, and adverbs can add an attitude or degree. Compare exact words and explain the impression they invite. A changed word may add or change meaning, so it is not always a fact-preserving replacement.

Model A: “The visitor asked for another turn” presents a request; “The visitor demanded another turn” presents a more forceful insistence. Model B: “The guide patiently explained the route” suggests willingness to help; “The guide impatiently explained the route” suggests irritation. Calling a fictional character “a helper” rather than “a nuisance” also expresses approval rather than disapproval. Those noun labels are the narrator’s evaluations; they are not fair fixed labels for real people.

Try it · Compare these complete fictional versions: A “The captain calmly told the crew to wait.” B “The captain angrily ordered the crew to wait.” Which verb and adverb choices create the different impression? Give a more neutral version without claiming the captain’s feeling.

Told versus ordered changes the force of the instruction; calmly versus angrily changes the suggested attitude or emotion. A neutral version is “The captain told the crew to wait.” It removes the stated feeling. Another clear neutral reporting verb may work. Explain the changed meaning rather than say that one version is always better.

3. Choose emotion and judgement words with evidence

Words such as pleased, delighted, worried and terrified express different kinds or degrees of feeling. A judgement such as thoughtful or inventive evaluates a character or event. Use the text’s actions and words to support your choice. More forceful language is useful only when it suits the meaning and purpose.

Story A: “Tess found a missing library card. She took it straight to the desk so its owner could collect it.” “Tess was thoughtful” is a supported approving judgement because she considered the owner’s need. Story B: “A fox made a hidden trap to take all the other animals’ picnic food.” “The fox was sneaky” is supported by the secret, unfair plan. These judgements evaluate actions; neither word simply names an emotion. For feelings, “I am pleased with my drawing” can express mild satisfaction, while “I am delighted with my drawing” expresses stronger pleasure in this comparison.

Try it · Read this complete story: “Rae’s first paper tower fell. Rae changed the base and tried again. The second tower stood, and Rae smiled.” Choose persistent, careless or unkind as a supported judgement of Rae’s actions and explain. Then change “Rae felt pleased” to express stronger pleasure without changing it into fear.

Persistent fits because Rae kept trying and changed the design after a failure. The story does not support careless or unkind. “Rae felt delighted” is one stronger-pleasure version. Accept another suitable word with an explanation; smiling alone does not prove an exact hidden emotion. Distinguish your proposed wording from what the story directly states.

Common mistakes

“Must always gives an order.” In “Visitors must sign in” it expresses a requirement. In “You must be tired” it expresses a strong conclusion. Read the whole situation.

“Every stronger word improves a text.” A strong word can overstate a feeling, claim or instruction. Choose the force you actually mean.

“Asked and demanded mean exactly the same thing.” Both can involve wanting something, but demanded adds forceful insistence. Substitution can change the character’s behaviour and the reader’s impression.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make three purposeful word changes

Use the three complete revision cards in More examples. Make a suggestion less compulsory, change how a fictional narrator evaluates an action, and express a stronger feeling of pleasure. Explain the effect of each change using its actual context. Then write your own brief rule and uncertain prediction, and tell a partner how they differ.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose between two words after an adult reads the complete context and explains an unfamiliar word. Core: make the three changes and explain the effect. Stretch: give two different context-appropriate versions of one message and explain why the more forceful one is useful in one situation but excessive in another.

Discuss fictional actions and viewpoints without labelling classmates. Learners may explain orally, in writing or with communication support. Record support; choosing a word with a prompt is different from independently explaining its effect.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “must not” the same as “do not have to”?

No. “You must not draw on the cover” forbids it. “You do not have to draw on the cover” says it is not required; that wording does not by itself forbid it.

2. Can one action have different evaluations?

Yes. A narrator might describe a firm request as helpful leadership or as bossy behaviour. Check the actual wording, evidence and viewpoint rather than treating an evaluation as a neutral fact.

3. Does an emotion word tell us how certain a prediction is?

Not by itself. Delighted describes pleasure. Might in “It might happen” expresses possibility. They change different parts of meaning.

FAQ

What is a modal verb?

It is a helping verb such as must, might, could or will that contributes meanings including requirement or possibility. Explain its job in the actual sentence.

Can two readers choose different judgement words?

Yes, if each meaning is accurate and supported by the text. An unsupported extreme judgement needs more evidence or a more suitable word.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand how the language of evaluation and emotion, such as modal verbs, can be varied to be more or less forceful. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include language, vocabulary, character.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Opinion plus reason

Move from “I liked the story” to “I found the ending effective because the repeated warning built suspense.” The second response gives a judgement and evidence.

Strength of judgement

In a prediction about a new lid, compare “The lid might help keep the paint wet” with “The lid will help keep the paint wet”: the second sounds more certain. In a recommendation, “You should put the lid on” advises an action. A recommendation and a prediction do different jobs; do not rank their modals on one universal ladder.

Subjective and objective wording

“The brilliant new playground is exciting” evaluates; “the playground opened on Monday” reports a checkable fact. Discuss why a writer may choose either.

Transfer

Use the complete revision cards below. Identify the word choice, change it for the stated context and explain the changed force, attitude or emotion.

The complete warning story behind the opinion

“Keep the lid shut or the paper stars will blow away,” said the keeper. Dara nodded and put the lid down. A gust shook the table. “Keep the lid shut,” the keeper warned again. Dara reached for the ribbon, the lid lifted, and the stars scattered across the floor. Dara and the keeper gathered every star and tied the lid down.

A reader can support “I found the ending effective because the repeated warning built suspense” by naming the repeated warning and the later scattering. That is one supported judgement, not a response everyone must share.

A complete record and a judgement

Prepared fictional record: “The new playground opened on Monday. It has two swings.” Response: “The brilliant new playground is exciting.” The record supplies checkable model facts; brilliant and exciting add evaluation or feeling. Explain whose judgement is being expressed rather than claiming every reader must feel excited.

Revision card 1 · an offered choice

A group leader wants to offer drawing as one possible quiet activity. The draft says, “You must draw while we wait.”

Replace the modal so it offers the activity as a choice, and explain the effect.

“You could draw while we wait” offers a possibility or suggestion instead of a requirement. Other clear choice wording, such as “You can choose to draw”, can work; explain how it fits the stated purpose.

Revision card 2 · a narrator’s impression

Version A: “The rabbit pushed past the other animals.” Version B: “The rabbit politely waited for the other animals.” These fictional versions deliberately give different actions and attitudes.

Name a verb and an adverb that create the different evaluations. Does changing A into B preserve all facts?

Pushed contrasts with waited, and politely presents an approving attitude in B. The action has changed as well as the impression, so the versions do not preserve all facts. A writer may choose either for a different fictional character portrayal.

Revision card 3 · stronger pleasure

“At last, the repaired kite flew. Sol felt pleased with the result.” The writer wants stronger pleasure, not anger or fear.

Replace pleased and explain why your word fits the intended feeling.

Delighted is one suitable choice: it expresses stronger pleasure in this context. Thrilled may also work. Terrified would change the emotion to fear, and furious would change it to anger.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

Fresh check · probability and obligation

A rule says, “Visitors must stay behind the line.” A prediction says, “The bus could arrive early.”

Explain the modal’s job in each sentence. Rewrite the prediction to sound more certain without turning it into a rule.

Must expresses a requirement to stay behind the line. Could expresses a possible arrival. “The bus will arrive early” sounds more certain as a prediction; it is still the speaker’s claim rather than proof of the future.

Fresh check · noun, verb and adverb choices

Fictional narrator A: “The helper gently guided the lost cub.” Narrator B: “The troublemaker roughly shoved the lost cub.”

Compare one noun, one verb and one adverb and explain the impressions. Are these merely different spellings of the same facts?

Helper/troublemaker gives approving/disapproving labels; guided/shoved changes the action and force; gently/roughly changes manner. The versions present meaningfully different behaviour and evaluations, so they are not fact-preserving swaps. Discuss fictional actions rather than fixed labels for classmates.

Fresh check · supported judgement

“Uma saw that the display sign had fallen. She set it upright so visitors could read it. Then she returned to her own work.”

Give a supported judgement of Uma’s action and a reason. Then express mild or strong pleasure in your own response and name which you chose.

Helpful or thoughtful is supported by setting the sign upright for visitors. For example, “I was pleased that Uma helped” expresses pleasure; “I was delighted that Uma helped” expresses stronger pleasure in this comparison. Accept another accurate judgement or response with evidence; do not infer an exact hidden feeling for Uma.

Earlier worked model

A sign says, “Visitors must sign in.” What does “must” show here?

Answer: Signing in is required.

In this instruction, “must” expresses a requirement rather than a suggestion.
