YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Share ideas and choose language for the occasion AC9E3LA01

Learning intention: I can share turns, build on other people’s ideas and use language that suits our agreed way of working.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Show that you heard the idea before adding yours

Asha says, “Let’s put the model bridge on a wide base so it will stand up.” Ben waits until she finishes, then says, “Your wide base could help it stand. Could we also add a support underneath?” Ben restates Asha’s point and adds a connected suggestion. He does not have to agree with every idea to work cooperatively. If the group has not agreed how to take turns, first ask, “How shall we make sure everyone can contribute?”

Learn the skill

1. Share turns and check understanding

A conversation works when people understand how to join, finish and hand over a turn. Listen or attend to the whole contribution, then respond to its meaning. Invite a contribution without forcing someone to speak. A check such as “What will we do first?” gives better evidence of understanding than assuming everyone followed.

Model A: Jo says, “First we will label the pots; then we will add soil.” Lee waits and says, “So labels come before soil. Have I understood?” Jo confirms. Model B: Pat says, “I have finished my idea. Sam, would you like to add anything, or pass?” Sam chooses to pass. The group leaves space for Sam to contribute later. Both exchanges make the shared plan clearer.

Try it · Mira says, “Put the blue label on the empty tray before we fill it.” You did not hear which tray. What could you say next? Then suggest a question that checks the order of the jobs.

For example, “Which tray should have the blue label?” asks for the missing detail. “Do we label the tray before we fill it?” checks the order. Other relevant clear questions are valid. Give Mira time to answer; do not pretend you heard the missing information.

2. Match the occasion and the agreed roles

Formality is how the language and conventions fit an occasion and relationship. Familiar pair work may allow a quick request; a chaired meeting may have a named person who invites questions. Clear, respectful short words can suit both. For First Nations Australian events, check the roles and local guidance with the hosts; one community’s arrangements do not set rules for every community.

Pair work model: a friend has finished speaking, so Kim asks, “Can I add an idea?” Chaired school meeting model: the chair has asked students to wait to be invited; Kim signals in the agreed way, waits, then says, “Thank you. Could I ask how the groups will be chosen?” The difference is the shared convention, audience and role, not the length of the words. Reconciliation Australia explains that a Welcome to Country is delivered by Traditional Owners, or Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people given permission by Traditional Owners; anyone can offer an Acknowledgement of Country. The purposes and roles differ. Follow checked local guidance rather than inventing ceremony words.

Try it · At this fictional visit, the host has specifically told your class, “Please save questions until I invite them. You can ask me afterwards if you prefer.” You have a question during the explanation. What could you do, and what could you ask the host if the arrangement is unclear?

Wait for the invitation or use the offered chance afterwards. If unsure, ask at an appropriate pause, “Could you explain when we may ask questions?” These are this host’s supplied directions, not a universal First Nations speaking rule. Check the actual host’s arrangements in a real visit. Do not assume that offering an Acknowledgement gives someone the role of delivering a Welcome.

3. Develop ideas together

Start a topic clearly, affirm a useful contribution, ask a relevant question and add a reason. To change topics, negotiate rather than abruptly taking over. Useful feedback names a specific feature and a possible improvement. Check both individual and group understanding before acting.

Model A: “Shall we decide how to label our display?” starts a topic. “Your large labels will be easy to read. Could we put each label beside its model?” affirms and builds. Model B: “I think the map needs a key because I cannot tell what the dots mean” gives specific feedback. “Have we finished discussing the map? Could we talk about the title next?” negotiates a change. “What will each of us do first?” checks the shared plan.

Try it · Noor says, “Let’s put all our labels at the bottom of the display.” You think visitors may not know which label belongs to each object. Give a respectful response with a reason and a suggestion. Then ask to move on to choosing the display title.

One response is “The labels will be together, but visitors may not know which object each one names. Could we put a label beside each object?” Then ask, “Have we finished deciding the labels? Shall we choose the title next?” Accept other relevant wording that explains the concern, offers a workable suggestion and lets the group respond. Agreement alone is not required.

Common mistakes

“Taking turns means everyone must speak immediately.” Invite people and allow thinking time or a pass. Use the group’s agreed communication modes.

“Formal language must use long words.” Clear, respectful words and the occasion’s agreed roles matter. A short question can suit a formal meeting.

“Useful feedback is just ‘good’ or ‘bad’.” Name the feature and explain how it helps or what could change: “The labels are clear; could we put each one beside its object?”

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Plan a shared class display

With a partner, plan a display of three paper models. Decide together how to take turns. Start the topic, invite a contribution, restate one idea, add a related suggestion, ask a relevant question and give one specific piece of feedback. Negotiate a change from arranging the models to choosing the title. Finish by checking what each person will do. Use the complete display cards in More examples for another short round.

Adjust the challenge Starter: an adult models one exchange about a different display; choose a useful question and complete one turn with a cue. Core: take part in the whole exchange and explain how one response helped. Stretch: use the same ideas in a chaired class meeting and explain which convention or wording you changed.

Use speech, sign, AAC, writing or an agreed combination. Note prompting or modelling; a written choice supports understanding but does not demonstrate a real exchange. Do not require eye contact, a particular accent or immediate speech. Cultural learning uses named sources and checked host guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Can I disagree and still cooperate?

Yes. Explain the concern, connect it to the task and give others space to respond. Cooperation does not require everyone to hold the same view.

2. Can the same polite question suit a friend and a visitor?

Yes. Relationship, role, purpose and shared conventions matter. There is no need to force a different phrase when the same words work clearly.

3. Does one host’s speaking rule apply everywhere?

No. It tells you what to do in that supplied setting. Ask the actual hosts and use community-approved guidance for real events.

FAQ

How can I check that the group understands?

Ask someone to explain the next step or what they will do, then clarify any difference. “Does everyone understand?” alone may not reveal a misunderstanding.

What is the difference between a Welcome and an Acknowledgement?

A Welcome to Country has a specific Traditional Owner permission and delivery role. Anyone can offer an Acknowledgement of Country respectfully. Consult hosts and local representatives about the actual occasion.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand that cooperation with others depends on shared understanding of social conventions, including turn-taking language, which vary according to the degree of formality. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include context, language, speaking.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Listen for the idea

After a short spoken message, restate the key point before adding a response.

Build on another speaker

Use a response such as “I agree with ___ because…” or “I see it differently because…” and connect directly to what was said.

Voice supports purpose

Practise pace, volume and emphasis on the same sentence, then discuss which delivery best suits the audience and purpose.

Use source-based cultural evidence

When a text, language name or cultural protocol is discussed, identify the source and describe what it supports without treating one example as representing all First Nations Peoples or Countries/Places.

A complete message to hear and restate

Adult or partner models this whole message in the learner’s communication mode: “Our paper bridge needs a wide base. That will help it stand. Then we can add the road.” A possible restatement is “The wide base comes first so the bridge can stand.” A connected addition is “Could we add supports beside the base?” Rephrase rather than demand exact repetition.

One sentence in two occasions

Sentence: “Could we compare the two designs?” Occasion A: two familiar partners are choosing a bridge. Occasion B: a chaired school meeting; the chair invites a student question. Model the same clear sentence in both contexts, attending to the agreed turn. Discuss how the wording can suit both and how the occasion changes when a turn is taken. Pace and comfortable volume support communication; they are not separate performance targets here.

Source-backed roles and checked host guidance

Reconciliation Australia explains that a Welcome to Country is delivered by Traditional Owners, or Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people given permission by Traditional Owners. Anyone can offer an Acknowledgement of Country. The roles and purposes differ; arrangements and appropriate wording depend on context. Consult local representative bodies and the actual hosts when planning an event.

For a real visit, ask the host how to participate and when to ask questions. The fictional host directions used in this lesson are supplied for that scenario only. Do not turn them into rules for every First Nations community, invent local language or imitate a ceremony.

Display discussion · another round

The group has three paper models: a boat, a bridge and a bird. Proposed plan: all models go on a low table; labels go beside their objects; a wide gap stays clear for visitors. Eva says, “Let’s put the bridge in the middle so people can see both sides.”

Restate Eva’s idea, add a connected suggestion, ask a useful question and negotiate moving on to the display title.

For example: “You want the bridge in the middle so both sides can be seen. Could the boat go at one end? How much space should we leave? When we have agreed on places, could we choose the title?” Accept equivalent relevant language and a real chance for others to reply. Check the group’s final plan by asking what each person will do.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

Fresh check · clarify a whole message

The group has agreed to let each person finish. Alex says, “Put the large label above the boat and the small label beside the bird.” You heard the boat instruction but missed where the small label belongs.

Give a suitable next response and a way to check the plan.

Wait for Alex to finish, then ask a relevant clarification such as “Where should the small label go?” A check could restate “Large label above the boat; small label beside the bird—is that right?” Accept equivalent wording that checks the actual missing information.

Fresh check · choose the convention

In pair work, your partner has just finished an idea. At a separate class meeting, the chair has asked everyone to wait for an invitation before asking questions.

Explain how you could ask a question in each setting. Must you use longer words in the meeting?

In the pair, ask after the partner finishes. In the meeting, use its agreed invitation process, then ask clearly. The words may be the same, such as “Could you explain that part?” Longer words are not required. The conventions, roles and context explain the difference.

Fresh check · specific feedback

Maya says, “Let’s write our display title on the smallest card.” The display will be read from across the classroom. A larger blank card is available.

Give useful feedback and a suggestion, then check whether Maya agrees.

For example: “The title tells visitors our topic, but the small card may be hard to read from across the room. Could we use the larger card? What do you think?” It names a relevant issue and improvement while leaving space for a response. Other clear, respectful versions are valid.

Earlier worked model

Lina is still explaining her bridge design. Tom has a new idea. How can Tom join without cutting her off?

Answer: Let Lina finish, then ask to add an idea.

Waiting lets Lina finish her meaning; asking to add an idea creates a clear next turn.
