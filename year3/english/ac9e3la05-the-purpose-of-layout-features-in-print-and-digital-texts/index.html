Use headings and links to find your way through texts AC9E3LA05

Learning intention: I can explain how print and digital layout features guide readers, follow useful navigation words and check the information I find.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A specific label helps you choose a useful route

In the prepared class garden guide, the broad heading “Visiting” groups two subheadings: “Opening times” and “Where to enter”. To find when the garden opens, choose “Opening times” and read its information: “Our model garden opens after the morning bell.” The broad heading suggests a route; the specific subheading names the information you need. A heading is a guide, so check the actual text after choosing it.

Learn the skill

1. Read headings, subheadings and chapter titles

A heading names a topic or section. A subheading names a smaller part within it. Their size and position can show the relationship. A narrative chapter title may hint at an event or make us curious without giving the whole event away. In print, use a contents page and page number when supplied, then check the actual chapter or section.

Print guide model: title “Class garden guide”; heading “Visiting”; subheadings “Opening times” and “Where to enter”. “Opening times” contains “Our model garden opens after the morning bell.” “Where to enter” contains “Use the gate beside the shed.” Contrast a narrative contents card: Chapter 1 “The empty shelf”, page 3; Chapter 2 “A rustle by the door”, page 7. Chapter 2 begins, “Mina heard paper rustling by the door. Her missing paper bird was caught beside it.” The chapter title hints at a sound; the actual text explains its source.

Try it · A print model has the heading “Making our display”, with subheadings “Materials” and “Labels”. Under Materials it says, “We used paper and card.” Under Labels it says, “Write the model’s name on each label.” Which is the most specific subheading for finding what the models are made from? Then explain what a chapter called “The hidden box” might make you expect and what you still need to check.

Materials is the most specific route; its text supplies paper and card. “The hidden box” may suggest that a box is concealed or discovered, but the title does not prove who hid it or what is inside. Read the actual chapter before claiming those details. Different reasonable predictions are valid when clearly labelled as predictions.

2. Follow meaningful hyperlink words

A hyperlink connects to a destination. Its words should help predict what will be found there. Read the link text, follow it and check the destination heading and content. A menu groups routes; words such as return or next describe movement within a particular prepared text. The working model below uses real links on this page.

In the working garden model, “Opening times” leads to a section with that heading and the morning-bell information. “Where to enter” leads to a different section with the shed-gate information. Each destination has a “Return to garden menu” link that leads back to the menu. Contrast an unclear label “More”: by itself it does not say which garden information is behind it. Meaningful link words help a reader choose a route, but the destination still needs checking.

3. Compare routes and check what you found

Print and digital texts can share useful headings while offering different ways to reach them. A printed page reference guides you to a page; a digital link can move directly to a destination. Choose a route for the information need, then verify the heading and relevant sentence. If the information does not match, return and choose another route rather than guessing.

Print comparison: the prepared guide’s contents lists “Visiting”, page 4; on page 4, “Opening times” and “Where to enter” divide the information. Digital comparison: the prepared garden menu links directly to those two sections. Both can answer an entry question. A page number tells where to look in the model book; hyperlink words tell what the digital destination concerns. Neither replaces reading the information itself.

Common mistakes

“The largest heading must give the exact answer.” A large heading often names a broad topic. A subheading may give the more specific route; read its text to check.

“A chapter title tells me every event.” It can hint or create curiosity. Predictions need checking against the chapter.

“A useful-looking link proves the destination is right.” Follow the link and check the destination heading and information. Clear words help you predict; the text confirms.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a broad heading and a subheading both help?

Yes. Visiting can lead you to the right broad section; Opening times is the more specific label for when visits are possible. State whether you are naming a route or the most specific destination.

2. Does “Next” always mean the same place?

It means the next destination in that particular text or sequence. Check where it leads; the word alone does not name a universal page.

3. Can I infer what is in “The hidden box” from the title alone?

You can make a prediction about a hidden box, but the title cannot establish its contents. Read the actual chapter to check.

FAQ

What is a hyperlink?

It is a link to another location, here a real named section within the prepared page. Its visible words should help you predict the destination.

Why compare print and digital versions?

Both organise information for readers. Comparing them shows how headings, page references, menus and links provide routes, and why readers still need to check what they find.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.

Find when the garden opens and where watering cans belong. Name the page route and most specific subheading for each.

Visiting → page 4 → Opening times gives after the morning bell. Tool storage → page 6 → Watering cans gives beside the door. A broad section is a route, while the subheading narrows the search; the actual sentence confirms the answer.

Fresh check · digital confirmation

Use the actual working garden and tool menus in this section. The tasks are to find the entry gate and where trowels belong.

Follow both useful links, check their headings and information, then return to each menu. Explain one reason the link words help.

Where to enter reaches the shed-gate information; Trowel storage reaches the lower-shelf information. Return links reach the corresponding menus. The link words name the information need. Observe actual use of the links for digital navigation; a spoken route description alone is only planning evidence.

Fresh check · a complete chapter-title comparison

Prepared narrative contents: Chapter 1 “The locked drawer”, page 2; Chapter 2 “A key by the paint pot”, page 5. Chapter 2 text: “Ari moved the empty paint pot. A small key lay underneath it. Ari tried the key in the drawer, and the lock opened.”

Which chapter title suggests where to look for the key event? Check what the actual text says: was the key inside the pot?

Chapter 2 points to the key event. The supplied text says the key was underneath the pot, not inside it. The title suggests the event and creates interest, but the detailed text is needed to check location. A chapter title need not provide a precise complete account of every event.
