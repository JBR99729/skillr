YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Explain what a picture adds to words AC9E3LA09

Learning intention: I can use visible details to explain how an image adds meaning, while separating evidence from a possible interpretation.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The words name a meeting; the picture adds a relationship

The meeting illustration says “Jo met Kim.” Its image adds that the two figures stand near one another, turn towards each other and smile. Their hands reach towards the same box. These details can suggest a friendly shared activity. The words alone do not tell us this, and the image does not prove how long they have known each other. Describe what is visible before explaining what it may suggest.

Learn the skill

1. Look at relationships between characters

Notice who faces whom, the space between figures, their depicted sizes, where they look, and their expressions and gestures. Use several details together. Looking up at a larger figure may suggest unequal power in a story, but size can also show age or distance from the viewer.

In the meeting model, closeness, faces turned towards each other, smiles and hands reaching towards one box support a possible cooperative relationship. In the gate model below, one figure is drawn larger, looks down and points; the other looks up. That arrangement may make the larger figure seem in charge. It does not prove that the figure is unkind.

Try it · Use the separate gate try illustration. What does the image add to “The two people waited”? Give two visible details and one cautious interpretation.

The figures are far apart and face away from each other, with their arms folded. These details may suggest distance or a disagreement in this scene. They could have other reasons for waiting that way; the image does not tell us the cause. Accept another plausible interpretation supported by the actual details.

2. Notice the relationship with the viewer

Gaze means the direction someone looks. A character looking towards the viewer can invite us into the scene. A close view makes a face prominent; a wide view can make the setting and space more noticeable. These choices can affect our response without proving one emotion.

The viewer model below pairs the words “Ari waited” with two images. The close face looks out towards us, which can invite attention to Ari. The wide view shows a small Ari turned away in a large empty space, which can suggest distance or loneliness. The written words stay the same.

Try it · Compare the two library try pictures below, both captioned “May chose a book”. Which view invites more attention to May’s expression? Which shows more of the space? Use visible evidence.

The close view makes May’s smiling face large and shows a gaze towards the viewer, inviting us to notice her response. The wide view makes May much smaller and shows the surrounding shelves and open floor. Learners may respond differently; judge the visible evidence and explanation, not a required feeling.

3. Separate words, visible facts and possibilities

First say what the words tell you. Next name information actually visible in the image. Then explain a possible meaning supported by those details. Say what remains unknown. A map or diagram can add useful information in the same way.

For the arrival picture below, the words say “We finally arrived.” The image adds mud marks on two children’s clothes and a map with a jagged tear. Those details may suggest a difficult journey, but do not tell us exactly what happened. The retained creek map adds the location of a bridge and a route that its sentence does not give.

Try it · For the arrival picture, sort these claims: “They arrived”; “Their clothes have mud marks”; “They fell into the creek”. Which comes from the words, which is visible and which is not established?

“They arrived” comes from the words. Mud marks are visible in the illustration. Falling into a creek is not established: neither words nor picture shows how the mud got there. A hard journey is a supported possibility, not a known backstory.

Common mistakes

“The picture is nice” explains its meaning. Name the detail and its contribution: the figures face one another and share a box, which may suggest cooperation.

“A big character must be powerful or bad.” Describe size alongside gaze, gesture and story context. A close figure can look bigger because of position.

“I can treat my guess as a fact.” Separate what is stated, what is visible and what might be true. Explain the evidence for your interpretation.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Same words, different visual choices

Use the actual meeting, gate and viewer illustrations in More examples. Choose one caption and draw a different image for it. Change two useful details, such as spacing and gaze. Explain what your picture adds and compare a possible interpretation with the original. Finish by naming something neither version establishes.

Adjust the challenge Starter: point to two visible details with an adult, using the supplied image descriptions if needed. Core: explain what the picture adds and support an inference with two details. Stretch: keep the words the same, change a visual choice and explain a different possible reader response.

Accessible descriptions supply the depicted evidence without assigning a feeling. Discuss constructed fictional images; do not judge real people’s feelings, honesty or ability from eye contact, body position or size.

Three tricky questions

1. Does facing away always mean disagreement?

No. People might be looking at something else. It is a possible story meaning when other details support it.

2. Does a close-up guarantee everyone feels involved?

No. It makes selected detail prominent. Explain what you notice and how it may invite attention in this context.

3. Can a useful image add information without showing faces?

Yes. The creek map adds a bridge location and walking route. A diagram can also show how parts connect.

FAQ

What counts as evidence?

A specific visible feature, such as the gap between figures, the direction of gaze, a smile, a labelled bridge or the route line.

Can two interpretations be reasonable?

Yes, if each fits the actual words and image. Accept supported possibilities and identify what the text does not establish.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to identify how images extend the meaning of a text. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include image, character.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Words and image together

A caption may name what a photograph shows while the image supplies colour, scale or expression that the words do not. Explain the contribution of each mode.

Framing directs attention

A close-up makes a face or object prominent; a wide view can emphasise setting and distance. Ask what the viewer is guided to notice.

Layout supports navigation

Headings, labels, diagrams and white space can help readers locate and connect information rather than merely decorate the page.

Transfer

Show a new still or multimodal text. Students identify one visual choice and explain how it adds, extends or changes the written meaning.

Actual images for the earlier visual tasks

Check and explain

The gate try shows widely spaced figures facing away, folded arms and straight mouths. These details may suggest distance or disagreement. The written caption alone supplies only waiting. The reason and earlier events remain unknown.

A useful labelled diagram

Use the preserved picnic-area map in Worked example and reasoning checks. The labels identify the picnic area, creek and bridge; the dotted line shows a walking route to the bridge. That image supplies route information beyond “The picnic area is beside the creek”. Layout can help us locate a label; the route itself supplies the added meaning.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Naming a camera angle, colour or sound is not analysis; explain its effect.
  • One fixed effect: Interpret the feature in context and alongside other modes.
  • Separate-mode lists: Explain how image, words and sound reinforce, contradict or qualify one another.
  • Retelling: Analyse how the representation was constructed, not only what it shows.

A concrete quick correction

In the meeting drawing, the shared box and facing figures may suggest cooperation. “They have been best friends for years” goes beyond the evidence. A close face may invite attention, but viewers do not all have to feel the same emotion.

A fresh visual version to create

Use this new caption: “The friends found the box.” Draw two figures far apart, one facing the other and offering the box. Add a second version with the figures close together, both reaching for it. Explain a visible difference and a possible difference in relationship. Name something the words and images leave unknown.

Check and explain

Check the actual drawing: the caption stays the same and the specified position or gesture changes are visible. For example, an offered box may suggest an invitation, while both reaching could suggest sharing or competing, depending on the drawn expressions. Accept supported possibilities; the drawings do not prove ownership or earlier events.

Worked example and reasoning checks

Notice information supplied by an image

Explain how an image adds meaning beyond the accompanying words.

Worked example

The words say ‘The picnic area is beside the creek.’ A map also marks a bridge upstream and a walking route to that bridge. What does the map add?

  1. First identify the written information: the picnic area's position beside the creek.
  2. Read the map's symbols and route. They provide extra information about how to reach the other side.
  3. Explain the added meaning: the map shows where to cross at a bridge, which the sentence does not tell us.

Watch for this mistake: Saying ‘the picture makes it interesting’ does not explain added meaning. Name a particular visible detail and the information it gives.

Try it, then explain

  1. A story says ‘We finally arrived.’ Use the arrival illustration supplied below. Name information added by the image.

    Hint

    Use specific details visible in the arrival illustration, rather than guessing a whole backstory.

    Answer and explanation

    The children are muddy and the map is torn; these details suggest a difficult journey, although its exact events are not certain.

  2. Why is ‘The map shows a bridge upstream’ stronger evidence than ‘The map is nice’?

    Hint

    Choose the comment that names visible information.

    Answer and explanation

    It identifies a specific feature and location the reader can use.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E3LA09: identify how images extend the meaning of a text

  • recognising how the relationship between characters can be depicted in images through the positioning of the characters; for example, facing each other or facing away from each other, the distance between them, the relative size, one character looking up (or down) at the other (power relationships), facial expressions and body gestures
  • recognising how images construct a relationship with the viewer through direct gaze into the viewer's eyes, inviting involvement, and how close-ups are more engaging than distanced images, which can suggest alienation or loneliness
