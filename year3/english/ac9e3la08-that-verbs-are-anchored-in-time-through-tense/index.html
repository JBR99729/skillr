YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Use verb forms to show time clearly AC9E3LA08

Learning intention: I can use verb forms and time clues to explain when events happen and keep a short text clear.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The verb carries a time clue

Compare “Yesterday Nia walked to the gate”, “Nia is walking to the gate now” and “Tomorrow Nia will walk to the gate”. Walked shows past time, is walking shows an action happening now in this context, and will walk points to a future event here. Yesterday, now and tomorrow add time information; they do not replace the need for matching verb forms.

Learn the skill

1. Read the whole verb group and context

Find the verb or whole group, then read the time words and surrounding sentences. Present forms can describe habits or facts as well as what happens now. A present form can also describe a future arrangement when the context makes that clear.

“Nia walks to school each day” describes a habit. “Nia is walking now” describes the current action. “It is Monday. Nia is arriving on Tuesday” uses is arriving with a future-time phrase. Look at both form and context rather than claiming that every present form means right now.

Try it · Explain the time in “Every morning Jo feeds the hens” and “Tomorrow Jo will feed the hens”. Name each whole verb group.

Feeds describes a repeated habit, supported by every morning. Will feed points to a future event, supported by tomorrow. Keep both words in will feed. The verb forms and time expressions work together.

2. Change regular and irregular forms

Many past forms use -ed, such as play → played. Other verbs change differently, such as catch → caught. Learn and check common forms instead of adding -ed to every word. When using will for a future event, use the base form after it.

“She catches the ball” becomes “She caught the ball” for the past event. “They jump” becomes “They jumped”. “She will catch the ball tomorrow” uses catch after will, without the present-tense -es.

Try it · Tell yesterday’s events: “Lee carries the tray. Lee takes a cup.” Then complete “Tomorrow Lee will ___ the tray” using carry.

Write “Lee carried the tray. Lee took a cup.” Carry changes to carried; take has the irregular past took. Write “Tomorrow Lee will carry the tray.” Use the base carry after will.

3. Keep a sequence clear, and change time for a reason

For events in one past recount, use matching past forms unless you mean to change time. A new time phrase can help the reader understand a deliberate change to a current fact or a future plan. Consistency means clear intended timing, not forcing every verb in a text into one tense.

Draft: “Yesterday we packed the seeds. We carry the box to the shed.” Revised: “Yesterday we packed the seeds. We carried the box to the shed.” Both are past events. “Yesterday we planted seeds. Today we water them” deliberately changes time and says so.

Try it · This draft tells two events from yesterday and one plan for tomorrow: “Yesterday Sam found a pot. He puts soil in it. Tomorrow he will plant a seed.” Repair the unclear verb and explain which change of time should stay.

Change puts to put: “Yesterday Sam found a pot. He put soil in it. Tomorrow he will plant a seed.” Found and put are the two past events; put looks the same as its base form. Keep will plant because tomorrow marks the intended future plan.

Common mistakes

“Yesterday makes any verb form past.” Read the verb too. In standard written recounts, yesterday we walk needs walked for the intended completed past event.

“Every past verb ends in -ed.” Common irregular forms include took, caught, went and put. Check the particular word.

“A text must never change tense.” It may move from a past event to a current fact or future plan. Make the intended change clear.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Move a message through time

Use the complete time cards in More examples. Read the context, change the marked verbs to fit it, and explain the evidence for each form. Finish with a three-sentence garden message: one past event, one present habit and one future plan. Give enough time clues for a reader to follow the changes.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare past and present cards with a supplied verb bank. Core: repair a short recount and explain one irregular form. Stretch: write a clear text that deliberately moves from a past event to a future plan, and explain why the shift belongs.

An adult can read or scribe while the learner chooses and explains verb forms. Accept a clear oral explanation before written practice; record the support used.

Three tricky questions

1. Does present always mean this very second?

No. “Birds have feathers” states a general fact; “Jo walks each morning” states a habit.

2. Is “will carries” right for tomorrow?

Use “will carry”. The base form follows will in this construction.

3. How can I know the time if a word does not change?

Use the whole clause and context. “Yesterday I put it here” describes the past even though put keeps the same form.

FAQ

What are time adverbials?

Words or groups that add time information, such as yesterday, now or in the morning. They work alongside verb forms.

Must I use will for every future event?

No. “We are going to plant tomorrow” and “We are planting tomorrow” can also express future plans in a clear context. Will plus a base verb is one useful pattern.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand that verbs are anchored in time through tense. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include meaning, language choices, audience.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Build the sentence

Start with “Birds fly.” Add precise information: “Small shorebirds fly quickly across the inlet.” Identify what each added word or group contributes.

Clause relationship

Compare “We stayed inside because it rained” with “It rained, so we stayed inside.” Both connect ideas, but the clause structure foregrounds the relationship differently.

Grammar changes meaning

Compare “The dog chased the ball” with “The ball was chased by the dog.” The event is similar, but the sentence foregrounds different information.

Transfer

Students change one grammatical feature in a new sentence, then explain what changed in meaning, emphasis, time or detail.

Complete time cards

  1. Habit: Each morning Jo ___ the plants. Use water.
  2. Completed yesterday: Jo ___ the plants and ___ home. Use water and go.
  3. Plan for tomorrow: Jo will ___ the plants. Use water.
  4. Recount to repair: Yesterday we made a sign. We put it by the bed and carry the tools home.

Choose the forms and explain the time. Finally write a new garden message with one past event, one present habit and one future plan.

Check and explain

1: waters, a present habit. 2: watered and went, past events. 3: water, the base form after will. 4: change carry to carried. Made and put already fit the past recount. An original message might be “Yesterday we planted beans. We water them each morning. Tomorrow we will check the soil.” Accept other clear messages with matching forms and explicit intended time changes.

Apply time changes to the earlier examples

“Small shorebirds fly quickly across the inlet” uses fly; to describe one completed event yesterday, write “Small shorebirds flew quickly across the inlet yesterday”. Flew is the past form of fly. The added describing words do not create the tense change.

“We stayed inside because it rained” and “It rained, so we stayed inside” both use past verbs, stayed and rained. Their different ordering shows the reason/result relationship, not a change in time. “The dog chased the ball” and “The ball was chased by the dog” both describe the past chasing event. Was chased is the whole group in the second form; formal passive terminology is not required.

Fresh transfer: change “The children carry the box” into a completed event yesterday and a plan for tomorrow. Answers: “Yesterday the children carried the box” and “Tomorrow the children will carry the box”. Explain carried and will carry.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.

A concrete quick correction

In “Yesterday Jo took a cup”, took supplies past form and yesterday makes the time explicit. Do not replace took with taked. An explanation should name both the form and intended time rather than saying it sounds better.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

A fresh time check

This message has two yesterday events and one tomorrow plan: “Yesterday Jo caught a ball. Jo carry it home. Tomorrow Jo will play again.” Repair the middle verb, identify the whole future group and explain why one time change stays.

Check and explain

Use carried. Caught and carried describe yesterday’s events; will play is the whole future group. The tomorrow plan is a justified change of time. “Will plays” would not fit this construction.

Earlier worked model

Compare these complete sentences: “The child waved from the bus.” “The child waves from the bus each day.” “The child will wave from the bus tomorrow.” Which tells a completed event before now?

Answer: The child waved from the bus.

Waved is past tense. It places the wave before now.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E3LA08: understand that verbs are anchored in time through tense

  • learning how time is represented through the tense of a verb; for example, “She arrived.” “She is arriving.” and adverbials of time; for example, “She arrived yesterday.” “She is arriving in the morning.”
  • learning that tenses for some verbs are formed by changing the word; for example, “She catches the ball.” “She caught the ball.”
