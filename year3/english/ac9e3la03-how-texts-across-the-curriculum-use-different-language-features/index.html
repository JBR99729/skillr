Explain how a text’s organisation serves its purpose AC9E3LA03

Learning intention: I can compare complete texts from different subjects and explain how their order, sections and word choices help do their jobs.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The same topic can be organised for different jobs

Information: “Our class book box holds picture books and short chapter books. It stands beside the reading mat.” Request: “Please keep our class book box tidy. First, tidy books are easier to find. Second, returning each book helps the next reader. Let’s put books back after reading.” Both concern a book box. The information text groups facts to tell us about it. The request states a view, gives two reasons and ends with a call to act. Topic alone does not tell us purpose.

Learn the skill

1. Compare a factual recount and an autobiography

A factual recount tells what happened on a particular occasion, usually placing events in time order. An autobiography is someone’s account of their own life or experiences. It commonly uses I or my, but I alone does not prove a text is autobiographical: fictional narrators use it too. Check who wrote it and whose experience is being recounted.

Prepared factual recount, written by the class reporter from the class record: “On Tuesday, Class 3 visited the school display. First, the class examined the paper models. Next, the guide explained how the labels were made. Finally, the class returned to its room.” Fictional model of an autobiographical account, written in the voice of the invented character Eli about Eli’s own life: “I moved to this town when I was six. At first, I did not know anyone at school. Later, I joined the drawing club and made a friend. Now I enjoy showing new children around.” The recount records one class event with past-tense actions and time links. Eli’s model selects personal experiences and ends by reflecting on the present.

Try it · Text A, written by a reporter: “On Friday, the team set up a paper display. First, they arranged the models. Then they added labels. They finished before lunch.” Text B, a fictional autobiographical model written in invented character Lani’s voice: “I learned to make models with my uncle when I was seven. At first, my towers fell. After many tries, I could build a tall one. I still enjoy designing new towers.” Explain one shared feature and one difference that helps each text’s purpose.

Both place past experiences in a useful time order. A recounts a particular team event and uses they. B presents Lani’s own selected experience through I/my and includes an ongoing personal reflection. Accept another accurate text-based comparison. A real autobiography is written by the person whose life it describes; the labelled classroom model is fictional.

2. Compare a report and a sequential explanation

An information report groups facts about a subject, often under headings. A sequential explanation shows how or why something happens through connected stages. Read all supplied facts: first/next links are useful, but an explanation also makes a process or connection clear. These choices help a reader find facts or follow how a result occurs.

Design report, “Our paper bridge”: “Materials: The bridge has a folded paper deck and two cardboard supports. Size: The deck is 20 centimetres long. Use: The model spans the gap between two trays.” Design explanation, “How the bridge holds the toy”: “First, the toy rests on the paper deck. The deck presses on the supports underneath it. The supports stand on the table, so they hold up the deck and toy.” The report groups material, size and use facts. The explanation follows the support relationship to explain how this prepared model holds the toy. It is a model-specific description, not a claim that every paper bridge supports every toy.

Try it · Report: “Class sorting boxes. Materials: Both boxes are cardboard. Labels: One box says Paper and the other says Card.” Explanation: “How we sort a card. First, we read its material label. If the label says card, we put it in the Card box. That keeps card together in this class sorting system.” Which text helps you find what the boxes are made of? Which helps you follow how a labelled card is sorted? Explain using organisation and words.

The report’s Materials section gives the cardboard fact directly. The explanation gives ordered actions and an if connection that explains the route to the Card box. It explains this supplied sorting system; no outside recycling rules are needed. Merely spotting the word first is less complete than explaining what the sequence does.

3. Compare a narrative, verse and a literary response

A narrative can introduce characters and a setting, develop a problem and show what follows. Verse poetry uses lines and may use sound, rhythm or imagery; it does not have to rhyme. A personal literary response gives a reader’s view of a supplied text and reasons grounded in it. These are useful patterns rather than rules every literary work must follow.

Narrative, “The runaway hat”: “In the playground, Nia’s paper hat blew over the bench. Nia chased it, but it caught in a bush. Omar held the branch still while Nia lifted the hat free. Nia thanked Omar and tucked the hat into her bag.” Verse, “Paper hat”: “Paper hat, a little sail, / skips along a windy trail. / Catch it gently, hold it tight; / fold the wind away tonight.” Personal response to “The runaway hat”: “I enjoyed the ending because Omar helps Nia solve the problem. Holding the branch is a small action that makes their teamwork clear.” The story links a problem and solution; the verse uses four lines, rhyme and an imagined sail; the response names a judgement and supports it with the actual story.

Try it · Narrative, “The lost label”: “Ari could not find the label for a model boat. Bea checked under the display cloth and found it. Together they put the label beside the boat before the visitors arrived.” Verse, “Little label”: “Small paper sign, / beside the boat: / you tell its name / without a voice.” Which text uses lines and an imagined voice to focus attention on the label? Give a personal response to the narrative with a reason from its events.

The verse uses four lines and imagines the sign communicating without a voice; it does not rhyme, which is allowed. A possible response is “I liked the teamwork because Bea finds the label and both children put it in place.” Other preferences, including a reasoned dislike, are valid when grounded in an actual event. A retelling alone does not express and support a personal judgement.

4. Follow the stages of a simple argument

A simple argument aims to persuade a reader to accept a view or act. In this model, an introduction states the view, two arguments give relevant reasons, and a conclusion returns to the view. A fact that merely mentions the topic does not necessarily support the view. Compare a complete argument with information about the same subject.

Argument: “Our class should keep a paper-scrap tray. First, usable scraps would be easy to find for small drawings. Second, keeping them in one place would help us reuse paper. For these reasons, let’s put a scrap tray beside the art shelf.” Introduction: the first sentence states the proposal. Argument one: finding usable scraps. Argument two: reusing paper. Conclusion: the last sentence returns to the proposal and asks for action. Information comparison: “The paper tray is beside the art shelf. It is shallow and blue. It holds pieces of drawing paper.” That comparison describes the tray without asking the reader to support it.

Try it · Read this whole argument: “We should put names on our model labels. First, visitors could see who made each model. Second, a visitor with a question could find its maker. Let’s add our first names before the display opens.” Identify the introduction, both reasons and conclusion. Explain why “The labels are rectangular” would not be a useful replacement for the second reason.

The introduction proposes names; reason one links names to identifying makers; reason two links names to asking the maker questions; the final sentence asks for action. Rectangular describes shape but does not explain why names should be added. It is related to labels without supporting the stated proposal. For this fictional class task, use invented names rather than sharing personal details online.

Common mistakes

“A text’s topic tells me its purpose.” Two texts about a tray can describe it or persuade people to use it. Examine what the writer asks the reader to know, imagine, feel or do.

“One signal word proves the text type.” Read the whole text and explain how its organisation and language work together. First can appear in a recount, explanation or argument.

“Every poem rhymes and every story has the same shape.” Poems can use unrhymed lines. Stories can arrange events in different ways. Describe the choices in the actual text.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Sort texts by the job they do

Read the complete comparison cards in More examples, one pair at a time. For each text, name its main purpose, point to an organisation choice and one language clue, then explain how they help the reader. Finish by comparing the information card and argument about the same object. Use the texts themselves instead of guessing from a label alone.

Adjust the challenge Starter: an adult reads one contrasting pair; choose between two purposes and point to a supporting phrase. Core: compare both structure and language for the supplied pairs. Stretch: change an informative model into a short persuasive request with an introduction, two relevant reasons and a conclusion; explain the changes.

All classroom texts are supplied original models; the autobiographical voices are explicitly fictional. Read a pair in a short session and allow oral explanation or a scribe. Record support and use a fresh card for independent evidence.

Three tricky questions

1. Does I always mean a real autobiography?

No. A fictional narrator can say I. Check whether the writer is recounting their own actual life; these classroom autobiographical models are labelled fictional.

2. Is every ordered text an explanation?

No. A recount orders what happened, a procedure tells someone what to do, and a sequential explanation shows how or why a process happens. Explain the job of the sequence in the actual text.

3. Must I agree with a model response to a story?

No. Give your own view and a relevant reason grounded in the supplied story. Different supported responses can be valid.

FAQ

What should I say after naming a structure?

Point to the actual feature and explain its use: “Materials groups the making facts, so I can find what the model is made from.”

Can texts mix purposes or structures?

Yes. These short models make their main purposes clear for learning. In a longer text, describe the feature and its local job rather than force every part into one label.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to describe how texts across the curriculum use different language features and structures relevant to their purpose. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include purpose, language, text structure, literary, argument.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Structure follows purpose

A procedure uses ordered steps because sequence matters; a story may organise events to build tension; an information report groups related facts under headings.

Cohesion across sentences

Compare “Mia found a shell. Mia washed the shell.” with “Mia found a shell. She washed it.” The pronoun connects ideas without unnecessary repetition.

Paragraph focus

A paragraph is easier to follow when its sentences develop one main idea rather than jumping between unrelated points.

Transfer

Use the complete mixed-up text below. Make a useful organisation or connection change, then explain exactly how it helps the reader.

Complete extra structure models

Procedure, “Make a paper name card”: “Materials: a sheet of paper and a pencil. First, fold the paper in half. Next, write the model’s name on the front. Finally, stand the folded card beside the model.” The sequence tells the reader what to do.

Information report, “Our name cards”: “Materials: Each card is paper. Shape: Each folded card stands like a small tent. Use: A card names the model beside it.” Grouped facts help the reader find materials, shape or use.

Narrative, “The missing card”: “Jo placed a card beside the model boat. A breeze blew the card under the table. Jo found it and put a small weight on it. The visitors could read the boat’s name.” The linked events introduce and resolve a problem.

Complete mixed-up recount for the retained transfer task

Sentence cards: A “Finally, we put the labelled models on the table.” B “On Thursday, our class prepared a display.” C “First, we made the models; then we wrote their labels.”

Arrange the three cards into a clear recount and explain one organising change.

B, C, A gives the occasion before the ordered events and ending. The time words and the need to make and label models before displaying them support that order. The repair helps a reader follow what happened; it is more than changing a pronoun.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

Fresh comparison · recount and autobiographical model

Class reporter’s factual recount: “On Monday, our class measured the display tables. First, the teacher gave each group a ruler. Next, the groups recorded the lengths. Finally, the class compared the records.” Fictional autobiographical model written in invented character Dev’s voice: “I first enjoyed measuring when I helped make a toy house. I measured its walls with my aunt. Later, I started keeping drawings of my designs. I still like finding out how things fit.”

Compare purpose, viewpoint and one organising choice in the two complete texts.

The factual recount records a particular class event in time order, using first/next/finally. Dev’s model selects experiences from the character’s own life in I/my voice and ends with a personal reflection. Both use past experiences. The autobiographical model is explicitly fictional; I alone is not proof that a text is a true autobiography.

Fresh comparison · report and explanation

Report, “Our balancing model”: “Parts: A card strip rests on a block. A small toy sits on each end. Position: The block is under the middle of the strip.” Explanation, “How this model tips”: “At first, the strip rests level on the block. When we move one toy farther from the middle, that end of this prepared model goes down. The changed toy position makes this model tip.”

Which text groups parts and position, and which follows a change and its result? Explain how the arrangement serves each purpose.

The report groups facts under Parts and Position so a reader can find a property of the model. The explanation connects the changed position to the observed tipping result in this prepared model. It explains how the change occurs rather than listing parts. No calculation or general physics rule is required.

Fresh comparison · story, verse and response

Narrative, “The loose wheel”: “In art time, Lin’s paper cart lost a wheel. Lin could not make it stand. Ari held the cart while Lin fixed the wheel. The cart rolled across the desk, and both children cheered.” Verse, “Paper cart”: “Wobble, little paper wheel; / wait beside the line. / Steady hands can help you stand; / now your turn to shine.” Personal response: “I liked The loose wheel because Ari’s small act of holding the cart makes the repair possible.”

Describe one organisation or language choice in each text and connect it to its purpose.

The story links a problem, help and resolution to entertain through events. The verse uses four lines, repeated sounds or imagined address to a wheel, creating a playful focus rather than merely listing facts. The response gives a view and a story-based reason. Accept other accurate feature-effect links; do not require everyone to like the story.

Fresh comparison · argument and information

Argument: “We should keep a ruler beside the display table. First, makers could check whether a model fits the space. Second, visitors could measure a model if the teacher invites them to. Let’s keep one ruler in a labelled place.” Information: “The class ruler is 30 centimetres long. It is kept beside the display table. Its marks show centimetres.”

Identify the four argument stages and compare the main purposes of the texts. Why would “The ruler is yellow” be a weak extra reason for this proposal?

The argument introduces a proposal, gives a fit-checking reason, gives a visitor-measuring reason, and concludes with an action request. It aims to persuade; the other text informs about the ruler. Colour is merely related information unless a further relevant reason is explained. It does not by itself support keeping a ruler there.

Earlier worked model

A report has sections called “Materials”, “Size” and “Uses” for a class model. Where does “The base is made of cardboard” belong?

Answer: Materials

The sentence identifies what the base is made from, so it belongs with materials.
