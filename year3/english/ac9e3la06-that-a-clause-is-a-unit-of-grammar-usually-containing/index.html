YEAR 3 ENGLISH

Find clauses and match subjects with verbs AC9E3LA06

Learning intention: I can find the subject and verb in a clause and make them agree in standard written English.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Find each subject–verb partnership

Read the whole sentence: “While the puppy slept, the kittens played beside the basket.” Slept links to the puppy; played links to the kittens. There are two clauses. “While the puppy slept” has a subject and verb, but it needs the rest of the sentence here. A clause is usually built around a subject and a verb; it does not always stand alone as a sentence.

Learn the skill

1. Find the verb and its subject

Look for the verb or whole verb group. Ask who or what that verb tells us about. Include the words that belong with the subject. A subject can name someone doing something, or something being described.

In “The little gate is open”, the subject is “The little gate” and the verb is “is”. Is relates the gate to a description. In “While the puppy slept, the kittens played beside the basket”, the pairs are the puppy / slept and the kittens / played.

Try it · Find the two subject–verb pairs in “While the rain fell, the children were painting indoors.”

The rain / fell is one pair; the children / were painting is the other. Keep were with painting as the whole verb group. The while clause belongs with the rest of this sentence; it is not a complete sentence by itself here.

2. Match one or more than one

In ordinary present-tense examples with he, she, it or one named person or thing, many verbs end in -s: the duck swims. With a plural noun subject, use the matching plural form: the ducks swim. Find the main naming word in a longer subject rather than matching the nearest noun.

“The box of pencils sits on the shelf.” Box is one thing, so sits matches it. “The pencils in the box sit on the shelf.” Pencils is plural, so sit matches it. “Mina and Jo carry the box” has two people joined by and, so carry is the matching form.

Try it · Choose the form in each sentence: “The bag of shells rest/rests here.” “Sam and Lee carry/carries it.” Explain which words helped.

Use rests because the subject names one bag. Use carry because Sam and Lee names two people. Of shells does not turn the one bag into a plural subject. The -s pattern is for these present-tense verbs, not a rule for every verb and tense.

3. Check special forms and repair

Read the whole clause, find the subject, and check its verb form. Be and have have special forms: I am, she is, they are; she has, they have. In the past, I/he/she/it was and we/you/they were are useful standard written patterns.

Draft: “The twins is ready. Their sister have a bag.” Revised: “The twins are ready. Their sister has a bag.” Twins is plural; sister is singular. These are agreement repairs. The clauses already contained verbs, even though their forms needed changing.

Try it · Repair “We was by the gate. The small dog have a lead.” Keep the first event in the past and the second description in the present.

Write “We were by the gate. The small dog has a lead.” Were agrees with we in this past-time clause; has agrees with the singular dog in the present-time description. Different times are allowed when that is the intended meaning.

Common mistakes

“Every clause is a complete sentence.” A clause can depend on nearby words: “While the puppy slept” needs the rest of the model sentence.

“Match the verb to the closest noun.” Find the subject’s main naming word: the bag of shells rests; the shells in the bag rest.

“All singular verbs end in -s.” Check the particular verb and pronoun. I swim, you swim and she swims are standard present forms; be has special forms.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build, match and explain

Use the complete clause cards in More examples. Read each sentence, mark the whole subject and verb group, and repair any agreement error. Then join the two supplied clauses with while and explain why the while part needs its partner. Say which naming word controls each repair.

Adjust the challenge Starter: use the first two cards with an adult and mark subject and verb in two different ways. Core: complete all cards and explain the agreement repairs. Stretch: expand a subject with of or in, then explain why that added noun does not control the verb.

An adult may read the sentences aloud. Compare the standard written forms being taught without judging a learner’s home dialect or language. Note any modelling used.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “is” a verb in “The gate is open”?

Yes. It relates the gate to its description; a verb need not describe an action.

2. Does every plural subject end in -s?

No. Children is plural. Context also tells us whether sheep names one or more than one.

3. Can a command have a subject we cannot see?

Yes. In “Close the gate”, you is understood. Most examples here show the subject explicitly, which makes the partnership easier to inspect.

FAQ

Do I circle only the action word?

Find the whole verb group when there is more than one word: were painting, not painting alone.

Why learn agreement?

Matching forms helps readers follow standard written sentences. First identify who or what the clause is about, then check the form.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to understand that a clause is a unit of grammar usually containing a subject and a verb that need to agree. They identify the relevant language or text feature, point to evidence and explain how it contributes to meaning or purpose. Key concepts include clause.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Build the sentence

Start with “Birds fly.” Add precise information: “Small shorebirds fly quickly across the inlet.” Identify what each added word or group contributes.

Clause relationship

Compare “We stayed inside because it rained” with “It rained, so we stayed inside.” Both connect ideas, but the clause structure foregrounds the relationship differently.

Grammar changes meaning

Compare “The dog chased the ball” with “The ball was chased by the dog.” The event is similar, but the sentence foregrounds different information.

Transfer

Students change one grammatical feature in a new sentence, then explain what changed in meaning, emphasis, time or detail.

Complete clause cards

  1. The small rabbit is quiet.
  2. The rabbits beside the pond are quiet.
  3. The basket of leaves rest by the door.
  4. Mina and Jo carries the basket.
  5. While the wind blew, the children were reading indoors.

Mark each whole subject and verb group. Repair cards 3 and 4. Then join “The dog slept” and “The children painted” with while. Say which clause needs the rest of the resulting sentence.

Check and explain

1: The small rabbit / is. 2: The rabbits beside the pond / are. 3: The basket of leaves / rests. 4: Mina and Jo / carry. 5: the wind / blew; the children / were reading. One joined version is “While the dog slept, the children painted.” The while part depends on its partner here. A valid alternative is “The dog slept while the children painted”; identify the while clause in the actual version.

Answers for the earlier grammar comparisons

In “Small shorebirds fly quickly across the inlet”, small describes the shorebirds, quickly describes their manner of flying, and across the inlet adds location. The whole subject is Small shorebirds; fly is the verb. “We stayed inside because it rained” contains we / stayed and it / rained. The because part gives the reason. “It rained, so we stayed inside” presents the rain first and makes the result explicit. Formal clause labels are not required.

In “The dog chased the ball”, the dog is the subject and chased is the verb. In “The ball was chased by the dog”, the ball is the subject and was chased is the whole verb group. The subject is not always the doer. These forms retain the same chasing event; this is a supported comparison, not a requirement to master passive forms.

Fresh transfer: change “The dog near the trees runs” so it names two dogs. One answer is “The dogs near the trees run”. Changing the subject number requires changing this present verb too.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Explain how the exact feature shapes meaning, communication or response.
  • Retelling: Select precise evidence and analyse it rather than repeating content.
  • Automatic effect: Interpret choices through audience, purpose, context and surrounding text.
  • Vague explanation: Replace “this makes it better” with the exact feature, evidence and likely effect.

A concrete quick correction

“The box of pens are full” names one box; write is full. Pointing only to pens misses the main subject word. In “The box is full”, is is already a verb. A correct explanation names the subject and the matching form.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh example that has not been modelled. Ask the student to show the exact clue, feature or language choice and explain or use it independently.

A fresh clause and agreement check

Read: “While the bell rang, the children was packing. The bag of books rest by the door.” Find the subject–verb pairs, then repair the two agreement errors. Keep the first sentence in the past and the second in the present.

Check and explain

The bell / rang; the children / were packing; The bag of books / rests. Change was to were for children, and rest to rests for one bag. Keep were packing together. The while clause needs its partner in this sentence.

Earlier worked model

In “The small dog chased a ball”, which words make up the whole subject?

Answer: The small dog

The whole subject is the noun group “The small dog”; the clause tells what that dog did.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E3LA06: understand that a clause is a unit of grammar usually containing a subject and a verb that need to agree

  • identifying clauses in texts by locating verbs and the key words that link to the verbs; for example, “While the cat slept, the mouse scurried across the path.”
  • identifying that a singular subject has a singular verb and a plural subject has a plural verb; for example, “The girls play cricket.” “The girl plays cricket.”
