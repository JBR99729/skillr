Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Turn a situation into a labelled picture or number sentence, choose a strategy, solve it and explain what the answer means. Check that the result fits the real situation.

Before you start: Use addition, subtraction, equal groups and basic dollar–cent relationships.

Useful words: model: a representation of a situation; unknown: what needs finding; budget: the amount available to spend; check: test the result against the situation.

1. Understand and represent the question

Identify what you need to find, the useful quantities and their units. Ask for missing information rather than inventing it.

Worked example: a hall needs 64 chairs and 28 are already set out. The missing amount is more chairs, not all chairs. A bar for 64 has a known part of 28 and an unknown part: 28 + □ = 64.

A note that the chairs are blue does not change the calculation. But “some chairs are set out” is insufficient unless their number is given or counted.

Watch for this: Do not automatically use every number in a story.

Try it: There are 75 tickets, and 36 have sold. Write a model for the tickets left.

Check the answer and explanation

36 + □ = 75, or 75 − 36 = □. The unknown counts unsold tickets.

2. Model joining, separating and comparing

Think about which quantity is the whole and which are parts. Comparison stories need careful reading of who has more.

Worked example: Mia has 46 cards, 18 more than Leo. Mia is the larger amount, so Leo has 46 − 18 = 28 cards. Check: 28 + 18 = 46.

For a joining story, 46 cards plus 18 new cards gives 64. The same numbers support a different calculation because the situation changed.

Watch for this: The word “more” does not always mean you should add the given numbers.

Try it: A club gives away 27 badges and has 45 left. How many did it start with?

Check the answer and explanation

72 badges. □ − 27 = 45, so 45 + 27 = 72.

3. Model equal groups

Check that the groups really are equal, then label the group count, amount per group and total.

Worked example: seven trays have four seedlings each. Draw seven equal parts labelled 4: 7 × 4 = 28 seedlings. Repeated addition, 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4, shows the same total.

If one tray has fewer seedlings, update the model instead of pretending that all seven groups still have four.

Watch for this: Label what each factor counts.

Try it: Six teams each need five bibs. How many bibs are needed?

Check the answer and explanation

30 bibs. Six equal groups of five gives 6 × 5 = 30.

4. Model sharing and grouping

Decide whether you know the number of groups or the size of each group. State what the division answer counts.

Worked examples: share 32 pencils among four tables: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 pencils per table. Put 32 pencils into packs of eight: 32 ÷ 8 = 4 packs.

Draw the four containers for sharing, or draw repeated eight-pencil packs for grouping. Check by rebuilding all 32 pencils: 4 × 8 = 32.

Watch for this: A bare answer such as “4” leaves the interpretation unfinished.

Try it: Twenty-four rolls are packed six per bag. What is unknown, and what is the answer?

Check the answer and explanation

The number of bags is unknown. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 bags; 4 × 6 = 24.

5. Make a money decision

Separate the cost, money available and any change or shortfall. Use one unit consistently.

Worked example: a notebook costs $4 and a pen costs $3. With $10, the total cost is 4 + 3 = $7, so $3 remains. Check $7 + $3 = $10.

For a price of $3.65 paid with $5, count up: 35c reaches $4, then $1 reaches $5. The change is $1.35. Keep dollars and cents clearly labelled.

Watch for this: Money left and money spent are different quantities.

Try it: You have $12. Three packs cost $3 each. Can you buy them, and how much remains?

Check the answer and explanation

Yes. 3 × $3 = $9; $12 − $9 = $3 remains.

6. Connect steps, use tools and check the answer

Name an intermediate result before using it in the next step. A calculator checks arithmetic after you decide what to calculate.

Worked example: four tables need six cups each, and nine cups are available. First find the need: 4 × 6 = 24 cups. Then find the extra cups: 24 − 9 = 15 cups. Check 15 + 9 = 24.

Estimate first, enter the planned calculation in a suitable tool if useful, then check the display against the units and question. Explain a final sentence, not only a number.

Watch for this: Do not stop at the first calculation if it does not yet answer the question.

Try it: Five teams need four balls each. There are 13 balls. How many more are needed?

Check the answer and explanation

Need 20 balls; 20 − 13 = 7 more. Check 13 + 7 = 20.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Do not automatically use every number in a story.
  • The word “more” does not always mean you should add the given numbers.
  • Label what each factor counts.
  • A bare answer such as “4” leaves the interpretation unfinished.
  • Money left and money spent are different quantities.
  • Do not stop at the first calculation if it does not yet answer the question.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Sam has 52 stamps, 17 more than Jo. How many has Jo?
  2. Thirty-six items are packed six per box. How many boxes?
  3. You have $20 and buy four $4 items. What remains?
Answers and next step
  1. 35 stamps; 35 + 17 = 52.
  2. 6 boxes; 6 × 6 = 36.
  3. $4; total cost $16, then $20 − $16.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Act out the story with objects. Ask “What are we finding?” and label the unknown before choosing an operation.

Extend within this topic: Create two stories using the same three numbers but different unknowns. Explain why the equations differ.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
