AC9M3N06 • Year 3 Maths

Mathematical Modelling in Practical Problems

Mathematical modelling turns a practical situation into a useful representation, calculation and interpretation. Students choose additive or multiplicative strategies and communicate what the answer means in context.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

Learning intention: use mathematical modelling to solve practical problems involving additive and multiplicative situations including financial contexts; formulate problems using number sentences and choose calculation strategies, using digital tools where appropriate; interpret and communicate solutions in terms of the situation.

  • Identify the question, relevant quantities and relationships in a practical situation.
  • Represent the situation with a diagram, table, number sentence or another suitable model.
  • Choose addition, subtraction, multiplication or division strategies that match the structure.
  • Check the solution and explain what it means in the original situation.

Concept boundary: students use accessible Year 3 arithmetic and modelling; algebraic formalism and complex multi-variable modelling are not required.

Key vocabulary and representations

A mathematical model is a mathematical representation of a situation. A strategy is a chosen method for solving. To interpret is to explain the result in terms of the situation.

Useful models include bar diagrams, arrays, equal-group drawings, tables, number sentences and simple digital representations. The representation should make the relationship in the problem clearer.

Teaching progression and worked examples
  1. Understand: identify what is known and what must be found.
  2. Represent: choose a diagram, table or number sentence that matches the situation.
  3. Choose and solve: select one or more suitable operations and calculate.
  4. Check: use estimation, inverse reasoning or another representation.
  5. Interpret and communicate: answer the practical question with units and context.

Example 1: Three notebooks cost $4 each and five pencils cost $2 each. Model 3 × 4 = 12 and 5 × 2 = 10, then 12 + 10 = 22. The purchase costs $22.

Example 2: Four boxes hold 6 items each, giving 24 items. Shared equally among 3 children, 24 ÷ 3 = 8, so each child receives 8 items.

Teacher language: “What does the problem ask? Which representation shows the relationship? Which operation matches it? What does your answer mean here?”

Curriculum elaborations and how they are taught
  • Formulate number sentences from practical additive and multiplicative situations rather than selecting an operation from a keyword alone.
  • Use diagrams, tables and digital tools where appropriate to organise information and test a model.
  • Solve financial-context problems with suitable Year 3 addition, subtraction, multiplication and division strategies.
  • Compare possible strategies, check solutions and interpret units, remainders or totals in the context.
  • Communicate the reasoning and conclusion clearly enough that another person can follow the model.
Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Using every number automatically: identify which quantities answer the actual question.
  • Choosing operations from keywords only: describe the relationship before writing the number sentence.
  • Stopping at a bare number: add the unit and explain what the result means.
  • No check: estimate or use an inverse/second representation before accepting the solution.
Support, Core and Extend
  • Support: provide a modelling scaffold: Understand → Represent → Solve → Check → Communicate, with concrete materials.
  • Core: independently model mixed additive/multiplicative practical problems and explain the chosen strategy.
  • Extend: compare two valid representations or strategies for the same problem and discuss which communicates the situation more clearly.
Quick check and mastery evidence
  1. Model 4 packs of 6 shared among 3 children. Expected: 4 × 6 = 24, then 24 ÷ 3 = 8 each.
  2. Three $4 items and five $2 items cost how much? Expected: $22.
  3. Why should a solution be interpreted after calculating? Evidence: explains that the number must be connected to the practical question, unit and situation.

Mastery means selecting a meaningful representation and operations, checking the result and communicating a contextually complete solution.
