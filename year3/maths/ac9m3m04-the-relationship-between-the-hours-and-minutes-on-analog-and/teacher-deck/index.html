How Clocks Work

Analogue time • digital time • minute accuracy • clock geometry

Teacher goal: secure the meaning of the hands first. Geometry and the angle formula come only after students can read clocks accurately.

Learning intentions

  • Identify the hour, minute and second hands.
  • Read analogue and digital time to the nearest minute.
  • Connect digital notation with “past”, “to”, quarter and half.
  • Explain why the hour hand moves between hour numbers.
  • Extension Use 360°, 30° and 6° to reason about clock angles.

Success criteria: students justify both the minute-hand position and the hour-hand position.

Meet the analogue clock

  • Hour hand: shorter; shows the hour.
  • Minute hand: longer; counts minutes.
  • Second hand: usually thin and fast.

Clock designs vary. Teach the purpose and movement of each hand, not appearance alone.

A reliable clock-reading routine

  1. Read the minute hand precisely.
  2. Identify the hour that has just passed.
  3. Write the hour before the colon.
  4. Write two minute digits after the colon.
  5. Check that the hour hand is in a sensible position.

Prompt: “How much of this hour has already passed?”

Count minutes around the clock

1 → 5 min
2 → 10 min
3 → 15 min
4 → 20 min
6 → 30 min

After counting by 5s is secure, count the small ticks for exact minutes such as 2:07 or 3:23.

Nearest-minute reading

The minute hand is on minute 7 and the hour hand is just past 2.

Time = 2:07

Not 2:7: the minute part uses two digits.

Year 4 extension

Clocks are circles

  • A complete turn is 360°.
  • 12 equal hour sections: 360° ÷ 12 = 30°.
  • 60 minute ticks: 360° ÷ 60 = 6°.

Link this to students’ prior language of full turn, half turn, quarter turn and right angle.

Special angles on a clock

3:00 → 90°

6:00 → 180°

9:00 → 90° smaller angle

3:00 and 9:00 are easy exact-hour examples, but the hands form right angles at other times too.

Advanced extension

Angle formula

Let H be the hour on a 12-hour clock and M be the minutes. Treat 12 as H = 0.

d = |30H − 5.5M|
smaller angle = min(d, 360° − d)

The second line corrects a common mistake: the raw difference can be greater than 180°.

Worked example: 3:15

d = |30(3) − 5.5(15)|

d = |90 − 82.5|

d = 7.5°

The hour hand has moved 7.5° beyond the 3.

15 × 6° = 90°

Fun fact: overlapping hands

The hour and minute hands overlap 11 times in a 12-hour cycle.

Why not exactly once every hour? The hour hand keeps moving, so the minute hand needs a little more than 60 minutes to catch it again.

This is enrichment reasoning, not a required AC9M3M04 assessment item.

Common misconceptions

  • Minute hand on 7 means 7 minutes ✗ → it means 35 minutes.
  • Hour hand stays exactly on 4 at 4:45 ✗.
  • 7:5 is standard digital notation ✗ → write 7:05.
  • 3:45 is quarter past 3 ✗ → quarter to 4.
  • Count printed numbers for an angle ✗ → count spaces.
  • |30H − 5.5M| always gives the smaller angle ✗.

Guided classroom practice

  1. Hour hand between 8 and 9; minute hand on 4. 8:20
  2. Minute hand turns for 15 minutes. 90°
  3. Shorter angle from number 2 to number 5. 90°
  4. Smaller hand angle at 3:15. 7.5°
  5. At 4:45, where is the hour hand? Three quarters towards 5

Exit ticket and next steps

  1. Why does the hour hand sit between numbers?
  2. Write “five minutes past seven” digitally.
  3. How many degrees are between adjacent clock numbers?
  4. What extra step is needed when d is greater than 180°?

Answers: it moves continuously; 7:05; 30°; use 360° − d and choose the smaller angle.
