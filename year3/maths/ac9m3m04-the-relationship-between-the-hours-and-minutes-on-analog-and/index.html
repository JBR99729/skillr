AC9M3M04: Telling Time on Analogue and Digital Clocks

Describe how hours and minutes work together, read analogue and digital time to the nearest minute, use everyday time language, and understand where the hour hand should be throughout the hour.

Topic guideTeacher slidesWorksheetPracticeTest

What students learn in AC9M3M04

AC9M3M04 is about understanding the relationship between hours and minutes on analogue and digital clocks and reading time to the nearest minute. Students should do more than memorise quarter-hour times: they need to read exact minute marks, interpret the hour hand when it sits between numbers, and connect the same time across analogue, digital and spoken forms.

Big idea: minutes show how far the minute hand has travelled through the current hour; the hour tells us which hour has most recently started. The hour hand moves gradually toward the next hour as the minutes pass.
  • Identify the hour hand, minute hand, hour numbers and minute marks.
  • Read and represent time to five-minute intervals and to the nearest minute.
  • Connect analogue clocks, digital notation and spoken time.
  • Use o’clock, quarter past, half past, quarter to, minutes past and minutes to.
  • Explain why the hour hand is usually between two hour numbers.
  • Check whether the positions of both hands agree with a stated digital time.
  • Apply clock reading in school, transport, sport, cooking and daily-routine contexts.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

  • Content description: describe the relationship between the hours and minutes on analog and digital clocks, and read the time to the nearest minute.
  • AC9M3M04_E1: represent and read time on an analog clock using the markings and positions of the hands, to the nearest minute mark or five-minute interval.
  • AC9M3M04_E2: read and connect analogue and digital time and interpret time language such as 12:15 as quarter past 12, 12:45 as quarter to 1, and 10:05 as 5 minutes past 10.
  • AC9M3M04_E3: read analogue clocks throughout the day and connect the position of the hour hand with the distance travelled by the minute hand during the current hour.

Meet the analogue clock

Hour hand

The shorter hand. It shows the current hour and moves slowly and continuously toward the next hour.

Minute hand

The longer hand. It travels around all 60 minute marks once every hour.

Minute marks

The 60 small marks around the clock face. These allow students to read times that are not exact multiples of 5.

Minute map: 12→00, 1→05, 2→10, 3→15, 4→20, 5→25, 6→30, 7→35, 8→40, 9→45, 10→50, 11→55.

When the minute hand falls between numbered positions, count by 5s to the previous number and then count the extra minute marks. For example, 3 marks past the 4 gives 20 + 3 = 23 minutes.

A reliable routine for reading any clock

  1. Read the minute hand. Count by 5s, then individual minute marks if needed.
  2. Find the hour that has just passed. If the hour hand lies between 4 and 5, the hour is still 4 until the hand reaches 5.
  3. Write the digital time. Put the hour before the colon and use two minute digits, such as 7:05.
  4. Say the time in words. For example, 7:05 is 5 minutes past 7.
  5. Check both hands. The hour hand should have moved a sensible distance toward the next hour.

How the hour hand and minute hand move together

The minute hand completes one full turn every hour. During that same hour, the hour hand moves from one hour number toward the next.

  • 3:00: hour hand exactly on 3.
  • 3:15: hour hand a quarter of the way from 3 to 4.
  • 3:30: hour hand halfway between 3 and 4.
  • 3:45: hour hand three quarters of the way toward 4.
  • 3:59: hour hand almost at 4.

This relationship is essential for avoiding a common mistake: leaving the hour hand exactly on the hour number when many minutes have already passed.

Connecting analogue and digital time

Digital time is written as hour : minutes. The digits after the colon show how many minutes have passed since the current hour began.

  • 12:15 = quarter past 12 = 15 minutes past 12.
  • 3:30 = half past 3.
  • 12:45 = quarter to 1 = 15 minutes before 1.
  • 10:05 = 5 minutes past 10.
  • 3:50 = 10 minutes to 4.
  • 8:58 = 2 minutes to 9.
Digital notation: minutes below 10 are normally written with two digits. Write 7:05, not 7:5.

Using “past” and “to”

Past tells how many minutes have happened after the current hour began. To tells how many minutes remain before the next hour.

  • 4:23 = 23 minutes past 4.
  • 4:40 = 40 minutes past 4, or 20 minutes to 5.
  • 7:45 = 45 minutes past 7, or quarter to 8.
  • 11:59 = 59 minutes past 11, or 1 minute to 12.

Real-life scenarios for students

  • School: recess begins at 10:45. Students identify the minute hand at 9 and the hour hand close to 11.
  • Transport: a bus arrives at 3:27. Students show the time on an analogue clock and explain both hand positions.
  • Sport: training begins at 5:30. Students write “half past 5” and place the hour hand halfway to 6.
  • Cooking: a timer begins at 4:05. Students recognise the leading zero and show the minute hand at minute 5.
  • Appointments: compare 9:05, 9:37 and 9:50 and explain how the hour hand changes.
  • Class transitions: read the classroom clock to the nearest minute and predict how the hands will move before pack-up time.

Suggested teaching sequence

  1. Identify clock parts and count by 5s around a model clock.
  2. Read o’clock, half-hour and quarter-hour times.
  3. Move to all five-minute intervals.
  4. Count individual minute marks to read exact times.
  5. Connect analogue, digital and spoken forms.
  6. Explicitly teach that the hour hand moves continuously.
  7. Use mixed non-quarter-hour examples such as 2:07, 3:23, 4:38, 5:52 and 9:58.
  8. Apply the skill in real-life contexts and ask students to justify both hand positions.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Reading the minute hand on 7 as 7 minutes instead of 35 minutes.
  • Reading only the minute hand and ignoring the hour hand.
  • Leaving the hour hand exactly on a number after minutes have passed.
  • Thinking the hour is the number the hour hand is moving toward rather than the hour most recently passed.
  • Writing 7:5 instead of 7:05.
  • Calling 3:45 quarter past 3 instead of quarter to 4.
  • Reversing the hour and minute parts of digital time.
  • Counting printed numbers instead of minute marks.
  • Getting a correct answer but being unable to explain why both hands make sense.

Student FAQ

Which hand shows the hour? Usually the shorter hand.

Why is the hour hand between numbers? Because it moves slowly and continuously as the minutes pass.

How do I read an exact minute? Count by 5s to the nearest numbered position, then count the extra small minute marks.

Why do we write 7:05? The minute part of digital time uses two digits.

Can one time have two spoken descriptions? Yes. For example, 2:40 can be 40 minutes past 2 or 20 minutes to 3.

Optional extension: clocks are circles

Extension only: this material is not required for core AC9M3M04 mastery. Use it after students can accurately read clocks to the nearest minute.
  • A full clock face is 360°.
  • 12 equal hour sections give 30° between adjacent numbers.
  • 60 minute ticks give 6° per tick.
  • The hour hand moves 0.5° per minute.
15 minutes

90°, a quarter-turn.

30 minutes

180°, a half-turn.

3:00 and 9:00

90° smaller angle.

6:00

180° straight angle.

Numbers 2 to 5 have 3 spaces: 3 × 30° = 90°. The hands overlap 11 times in 12 hours, an enrichment fact.

Optional advanced angle formula

For advanced learners only, let H be the hour and M the minutes, treating 12 as H = 0.

d = |30H − 5.5M|
smaller angle = min(d, 360° − d)

At 9:00, d = 270°, but the smaller angle is 90°. At 3:15, the smaller angle is 7.5°. At 8:20 it is 130°. At 12:15 it is 82.5°.
