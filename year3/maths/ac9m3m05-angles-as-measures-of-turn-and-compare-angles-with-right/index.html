AC9M3M05 • Year 3 Maths

Angles as Measures of Turn

An angle describes an amount of turn. Students use a right angle, or quarter turn, as a benchmark to compare smaller and larger turns in shapes and everyday situations.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

Learning intention: identify angles as measures of turn and compare angles with right angles in everyday situations.

  • Recognise an angle as the amount of turn between two directions or arms.
  • Locate the vertex where the arms meet.
  • Use a right angle as a benchmark for comparison.
  • Describe an angle as less than, equal to or greater than a right angle without needing degree measurement.

Concept boundary: Year 3 comparison is based on turn and the right-angle benchmark; formal degree measurement and angle classification terminology are later developments.

Key vocabulary and representations

An angle is a measure of turn between two directions. A right angle is a quarter turn. The vertex is the point where the two arms meet.

Useful representations include hinged strips, turns made by a person or object, corners of squares and rectangles, clock hands and door/scissor openings.

Teaching progression and worked examples
  1. Make physical turns: small turn, quarter turn and larger-than-quarter turn.
  2. Connect a quarter turn with the corner of a square as a right-angle benchmark.
  3. Compare drawn or physical angles by placing or imagining the right-angle benchmark at the vertex.
  4. Find and explain examples in the environment.

Example: A book corner shows a right angle. An opening smaller than that corner is less than a right angle; an opening wider than the corner is greater than a right angle.

Teacher language: “Where is the vertex? How much turn is shown? Is the turn less than, equal to or greater than a quarter turn?”

Curriculum elaborations and how they are taught
  • Use body turns and rotating objects to establish angle as an amount of turn.
  • Use a square corner or quarter-turn template as a portable right-angle benchmark.
  • Identify and compare angles formed by objects and shapes in the classroom and outdoors.
  • Vary arm length while keeping the same turn to show that arm length does not determine angle size.
Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Longer arms mean a bigger angle: angle size depends on the turn, not arm length.
  • The vertex is ignored: align the comparison benchmark at the point where the arms meet.
  • Shape size is confused with angle size: compare the opening/turn itself.
  • A right angle must face one direction: rotate the benchmark to show orientation does not change angle size.
Support, Core and Extend
  • Support: use hinged strips and a physical square-corner benchmark.
  • Core: identify and compare angles with a right angle and justify each comparison.
  • Extend: find multiple environmental examples and explain why arm length/orientation do not alter the amount of turn.
Quick check and mastery evidence
  1. What turn makes a right angle? Expected: a quarter turn.
  2. Do longer arms automatically make a larger angle? Expected: no.
  3. Compare three displayed angles with a right-angle template. Evidence: correct less/equal/greater descriptions and placement at the vertex.

Mastery means interpreting angle as turn, using the right-angle benchmark flexibly and explaining comparisons independently.
