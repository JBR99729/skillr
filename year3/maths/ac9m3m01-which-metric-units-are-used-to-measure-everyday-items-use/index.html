AC9M3M01: Identify which metric units are used to measure everyday items; use measurements of familiar items and known units to make estimates

Prerequisite knowledge

Students should be able to compare everyday attributes, choose familiar informal or metric units where appropriate, and read simple measuring tools or time displays. Check that the unit and the quantity being measured are clearly distinguished.

Concept teaching

Key idea: Metric units must match the attribute and size of the item. Familiar benchmarks help make sensible estimates.

Learning routine: Identify attribute → Choose unit → Use benchmark → Estimate → Check

metric unit
a standard unit such as metre, kilogram or litre
attribute
the feature being measured, such as length, mass or capacity
estimate
a sensible measurement made before measuring exactly
Worked and modelled examples

Choose an appropriate unit

pencilcm
roomm
appleg
bag of ricekg
cupmL
bucketL

Choose smaller units for smaller quantities and larger units for larger quantities.

  1. Read every label and identify what each quantity or feature represents.
  2. Describe the relationship shown by the model.
  3. Check that the conclusion answers the original question.

Estimate from a familiar benchmark

door ≈ 2 mdesk ≈ 1 mpencil ≈ 15 cm

benchmarks support estimates

An estimate should use a known measurement, not a random guess.

Apply the same relationship to the new context, then justify the answer using the labels and evidence shown.

Common misconceptions and corrections
  • Unit does not match attribute — Length, mass and capacity use different units.
  • Large unit for tiny object — Choose a unit that gives a useful number.
  • Estimate without benchmark — Refer to a familiar measured item.

2. Benchmark hunt

Find classroom objects close to 1 cm, 1 m, 100 g and 1 L.

1 cm1 m100 g1 L

3. Estimate then measure

Estimate an item, measure it and compare the difference.

benchmark→estimate→measure→compare
Independent practice

  1. Which unit best measures a pencil's length?

    • centimetres
    • litres
    • kilograms
    • seconds

  2. Complete the unit choice.

    Prompt: A classroom door is about 2 □ high.

  3. Which unit best measures the mass of an apple?

    • grams
    • metres
    • litres
    • minutes

  4. Name a familiar benchmark that could help estimate 1 metre.

  5. Match each item to a suitable unit.

    Match: bucket capacity • bag of rice mass • room length ↔ metres • kilograms • litres

  6. Complete the estimate.

    Prompt: A small drink bottle may hold about 600 □.

Reasoning and problem-solving

  1. Create a classroom benchmark chart for six metric units and justify each example.

  2. Estimate three measurements of one object using different attributes, then explain how you would check them.

Explain the reasoning, not only the final answer.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Model the idea with concrete or visual representations first, then ask students to explain the relationship in words before moving to symbolic work.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to show the idea with a drawing, objects or a simple example and explain how they checked the answer.

Read the topic guide and use the teacher slide for instruction. Students can then use the worksheet for written work, open Practice for supported feedback, or take the Test when they are ready.

Curriculum alignment

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: identify which metric units are used to measure everyday items; use measurements of familiar items and known units to make estimates
  • E1: examining the packaging on supermarket items to determine the metric unit used to describe the mass or capacity of the contents
  • E2: identifying items that have a mass of one kilogram or 500 grams, or a capacity of one litre or 500 millilitres and using these benchmarks to estimate the mass or capacity of other things, explaining their reasoning
  • E3: estimating the height of a tree by comparing it to the height of their friend and quoting the result as “the tree is about 3 times as tall”; estimating the capacity of a fish tank by using a litre milk carton as a benchmark
  • E4: choosing and using metres to estimate the dimensions of the classroom
