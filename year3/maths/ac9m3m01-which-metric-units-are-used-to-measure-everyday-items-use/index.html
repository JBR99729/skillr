Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Choose a unit that measures the right attribute. Use a known length, mass or capacity as a benchmark, make a sensible estimate and explain your reasoning.

Before you start: Recognise length, mass and capacity; compare familiar objects.

Useful words: length: how far from one end to another; mass: how heavy in g or kg; capacity: how much a container can hold; benchmark: a known measurement.

1. Match the attribute and unit

Decide what is being measured before choosing a unit. Packaging can show mass in grams or kilograms and capacity in millilitres or litres.

Worked example data

Worked examples: use centimetres (cm) for a pencil length, metres (m) for a classroom width, grams (g) for a small packet of rice and litres (L) for a bucket capacity.

A milk label reading 1 L describes how much it holds. A rice label reading 1 kg describes mass. The printed numbers alone do not tell the attribute.

Watch for this: A bigger numerical value does not necessarily mean a bigger quantity when the units differ.

Try it: Choose a suitable unit for a lunchbox length, the mass of a schoolbag and the capacity of a cup.

Check the answer and explanation

cm; kg; mL. Other compatible units can describe the quantities, but these are convenient familiar choices.

2. Use a length benchmark

Measure or receive a known reference before using it to estimate. Compare real dimensions rather than the size of a picture on a page.

Worked example: a one-metre strip fits approximately three times across a mat. Estimate the mat width as about 3 m. State that the fit is approximate.

Use a metre reference to estimate classroom dimensions. If a tree appears about three times as tall as a friend of known height, use that comparison cautiously from a suitable common viewpoint.

Watch for this: The printed size of a tree photograph is not the tree’s actual height.

Try it: A 20 cm reference seems to fit about four times along a shelf. Estimate the shelf length.

Check the answer and explanation

About 80 cm. Check that the references are aligned end to end without gaps or overlaps.

3. Use a mass benchmark

Handle a known mass or use supplied comparison information. Size or appearance alone cannot establish mass.

Worked example: a sealed 500 g packet is the reference. A bag feels about twice as heavy when compared carefully, so an initial estimate is about 1 kg. Use scales afterwards to check.

Compare with both 500 g and 1 kg references. Two objects of the same size can have very different masses.

Watch for this: Do not infer mass from a larger drawing or a physically larger container.

Try it: A parcel has a supplied estimate of three times a 500 g reference. What approximate mass is suggested?

Check the answer and explanation

About 1 500 g, or 1 kg 500 g. A scale is needed for a measured value.

4. Use a capacity benchmark

Use a known 500 mL or 1 L container. Estimate how many full reference quantities would fill the target container.

Worked example: a tub appears to hold about three full 500 mL pours. Estimate about 1 500 mL. A check finds three full pours plus another measured 100 mL, giving a capacity of 1 600 mL.

The check improves the next estimate. Tallness alone does not settle capacity because width matters too.

Watch for this: Capacity is the amount the container can hold; its current contents may be smaller.

Try it: A container holds about four 500 mL reference quantities. Estimate its capacity.

Check the answer and explanation

About 2 L, because four lots of 500 mL make 2 000 mL.

5. Scale or combine known measurements

Use repeated or combined benchmarks to estimate a larger measurement, keeping the units consistent.

Worked example: a table seems to be one metre plus about half a metre long. Estimate about 1 m 50 cm. For a tank, five approximately one-litre reference quantities suggest about 5 L.

Say which part was known and which comparison was approximate. Do not report the final estimate as an instrument reading.

Watch for this: Repeating a benchmark requires comparable units and a sensible fit.

Try it: Three similar boxes each have a supplied approximate mass of 1 kg. Estimate their combined mass.

Check the answer and explanation

About 3 kg, assuming the boxes have similar contents and mass.

6. Check, revise and explain an estimate

Explain the reference used, compare with a measured result and identify whether the estimate was too large or too small.

Worked example: estimate a mat width as 3 m; a tape measures 2 m 80 cm. The estimate was 20 cm too long. The one-metre reference was useful, but its third fit was incomplete.

Real estimates may allow a reasonable range. Improve the reference or measure when a precise quantity is needed.

Watch for this: Do not mark every explained estimate wrong merely because it differs from the exact measurement.

Try it: An estimated mass is 1 kg, but the scale shows 900 g. How should the estimate be described?

Check the answer and explanation

It was 100 g too high. Use the measured comparison to improve the next estimate.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • A bigger numerical value does not necessarily mean a bigger quantity when the units differ.
  • The printed size of a tree photograph is not the tree’s actual height.
  • Do not infer mass from a larger drawing or a physically larger container.
  • Capacity is the amount the container can hold; its current contents may be smaller.
  • Repeating a benchmark requires comparable units and a sensible fit.
  • Do not mark every explained estimate wrong merely because it differs from the exact measurement.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Which unit is convenient for the capacity of a water bottle?
  2. Five known 20 cm lengths fit approximately along a board. Estimate its length.
  3. Can a drawing alone prove which of two parcels is heavier?
Answers and next step
  1. mL or L, depending on the bottle size.
  2. About 100 cm, or 1 m.
  3. No; use measured or supplied mass evidence.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Calibrate one safe reference for each attribute: a metre strip, a sealed labelled mass and a measured container.

Extend within this topic: Compare two estimates made from different benchmarks. Explain which reference is more trustworthy and what measurement would settle the question.
