Learn the skill

1. Match the question and variable

State the group and what will be recorded. Some answers are categories; others are counts.

Try it · Is “number of library books borrowed this week” a category label or a count?

A count: a discrete numerical variable. Each learner’s result can be a non-negative whole number.

2. Choose and use a relevant source

Observation, a survey, an experiment or existing records can supply data. Check the group, date and meaning of each record.

Try it · To find which fruit classmates prefer, would today’s canteen sales necessarily give the same information?

No. Sales record purchases and may depend on stock, price or availability. Ask a clear preference question if that is the purpose.

3. Keep an accurate raw record and tallies

Record each case once. Keep category names consistent and group every fifth tally across the preceding four.

Try it · Tally red, blue, red, red, blue, red. Give the frequencies and total.

Red 4, blue 2; total 6. The learner should show the matching tally marks as well as numbers.

Common mistakes

Collecting responses that do not answer the question. Write the question first and decide what you need to record before collecting.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Record data for a question

Ask how people in your household travel to a familiar place. Record one response per person and group the answers by travel method. Check that the tally total matches the number of responses.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two travel categories. Extend by discussing how you would record a response that does not fit either.

FAQ

Why keep the original responses?

They let you check that every answer is counted once and placed in the right category.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Start with a clear question. Choose a way to obtain relevant data, record each observation accurately and organise the results in frequency tables or spreadsheets.

Before you start: Count accurately, sort by a stated category and group tally marks in fives.

Useful words: categorical variable: a feature recorded as a label; discrete numerical variable: a count with separate whole-number values; frequency: how often a value occurs.

1. Match the question and variable

State the group and what will be recorded. Some answers are categories; others are counts.

Worked examples: “How did each learner in our class travel to school today?” records categories such as walk or bus. “How many pets does each learner have?” records counts such as 0, 1 or 2.

A bus route number is a label, not a count of buses. A useful question must supply data relevant to the purpose.

Watch for this: Digits used as names, such as house or bus numbers, do not automatically make count data.

Try it: Is “number of library books borrowed this week” a category label or a count?

Check the answer and explanation

A count: a discrete numerical variable. Each learner’s result can be a non-negative whole number.

2. Choose and use a relevant source

Observation, a survey, an experiment or existing records can supply data. Check the group, date and meaning of each record.

Worked example: to count bikes arriving at school today, observe the agreed arrival period or use relevant arrival records. Asking children about their favourite transport answers a different question.

Existing digital data can help when it matches the purpose. With appropriate adult support, retrieve a small weather table for the selected place and dates, noting the source and units.

Watch for this: Do not silently replace the target variable with an easier but different one.

Try it: To find which fruit classmates prefer, would today’s canteen sales necessarily give the same information?

Check the answer and explanation

No. Sales record purchases and may depend on stock, price or availability. Ask a clear preference question if that is the purpose.

3. Keep an accurate raw record and tallies

Record each case once. Keep category names consistent and group every fifth tally across the preceding four.

Worked example: responses are walk, bus, walk, cycle, bus, walk, bus, bus. Tallying gives walk 3, bus 4, cycle 1; the total is 8, matching all eight responses.

Check both the total and the individual categories. A total can remain correct even if one response is copied to the wrong row.

Watch for this: Do not count the category heading or key as a response.

Try it: Tally red, blue, red, red, blue, red. Give the frequencies and total.

Check the answer and explanation

Red 4, blue 2; total 6. The learner should show the matching tally marks as well as numbers.

4. Build a categorical frequency table

Label the category and what its frequency counts. Include any required category with zero responses.

Worked example data
Travel to school todayNumber of learners
Walk4
Bus6
Cycle2
Total12

Worked example: a twelve-learner travel record has walk 4, bus 6 and cycle 2. The table’s frequencies total 12 learners.

If the survey allowed one answer per learner, the total should match the number who answered. If more than one choice was allowed, the table may count choices instead; label that difference explicitly.

Watch for this: Frequency counts responses or cases, not the numeric value of the category.

Try it: A single-choice survey of ten learners records 4 apples, 3 bananas and 2 pears. What must be checked?

Check the answer and explanation

The recorded total is 9, so check for a missing, unanswered or omitted response before declaring the table complete.

5. Organise discrete numerical frequencies

Place each observed count in the matching value row. Distinguish the recorded value from how often it occurred.

Worked example data
PetsLearners
02
13
21
31

Worked example: pet counts are 0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 3, 1. The table is 0→2 learners, 1→3, 2→1, 3→1. Frequencies total seven learners.

Zero is a valid observation: “no pets” is different from a missing answer. Seven learners answered; that does not mean there are seven pets altogether.

Watch for this: A frequency of two beside value zero means two cases with zero, not no cases.

Try it: For counts 2, 0, 2, 1, 0, give the frequency of 0, 1 and 2.

Check the answer and explanation

0 occurs twice; 1 once; 2 twice. Total frequency 5.

6. Use a spreadsheet to store, sort and total

Keep headings, values and frequencies linked. Sort whole records and select the intended cells for a total.

Worked example data
AB
1TravelLearners
2Walk4
3Bus6
4Cycle2
5Total=SUM(B2:B4)

Worked example: put category headings in A1 and B1, then walk/4 in row 2, bus/6 in row 3 and cycle/2 in row 4. Enter =SUM(B2:B4) in B5 to total the three frequencies: 12.

In a suitable spreadsheet, type this small table, calculate its total and sort the complete category–frequency rows. A printed cell diagram supports planning but does not demonstrate actual software use.

Watch for this: Exclude the total cell from its own SUM range and avoid mixing unrelated values.

Try it: Why should sorting a table move category names and their frequencies together?

Check the answer and explanation

They describe the same records. Sorting just one column can attach a frequency to the wrong category.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Digits used as names, such as house or bus numbers, do not automatically make count data.
  • Do not silently replace the target variable with an easier but different one.
  • Do not count the category heading or key as a response.
  • Frequency counts responses or cases, not the numeric value of the category.
  • A frequency of two beside value zero means two cases with zero, not no cases.
  • Exclude the total cell from its own SUM range and avoid mixing unrelated values.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Give a categorical and a count question about a class.
  2. What do frequencies in a single-answer class survey count?
  3. Which cells does SUM(B2:B4) include?
Answers and next step
  1. Example: preferred fruit; number of pets. Specify the group and time where needed.
  2. The number of responses or learners in each category, under the stated recording rule.
  3. B2, B3 and B4.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use a small supplied list and one answer per case. Match each response to a row before counting tallies.

Extend within this topic: Collect a small safe class or household dataset with permission. Preserve zero values, check totals and demonstrate a whole-row spreadsheet sort.
