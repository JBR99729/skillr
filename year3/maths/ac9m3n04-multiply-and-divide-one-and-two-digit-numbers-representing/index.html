Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Equal groups, arrays and related facts show why multiplication and division work. Split two-digit numbers into useful parts and explain what each answer counts.

Before you start: Make equal groups; use known 2, 3, 4, 5 and 10 facts and place value.

Useful words: factor: a number multiplied; product: the multiplication total; quotient: the division answer; array: objects arranged in equal rows and columns.

1. Connect equal groups with multiplication

Multiplication describes equal-sized groups. State both the number of groups and the amount in each group.

Worked example: four trays each hold six rolls. That is 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 4 × 6 = 24 rolls. The 4 counts trays; the 6 counts rolls per tray.

Draw one loop around each equal group before writing the equation. If the groups differ in size, one repeated equal-group fact does not describe them. No groups of four contain no counters: 0 × 4 = 0. One group of four contains four: 1 × 4 = 4.

Watch for this: 4 × 6 is not 4 + 6.

Try it: Write and solve a multiplication for five bags with four marbles each.

Check the answer and explanation

5 × 4 = 20 marbles. Five is the number of bags; four is the number per bag.

2. Turn and split arrays

An array lets you see rows, columns and smaller useful rectangles. Turning it changes the arrangement but preserves the total.

Worked example: split a 6-by-4 array into 5-by-4 and 1-by-4. There are 20 + 4 = 24 counters, so 6 × 4 = 24.

Turn the complete array to see 4 × 6 = 24. For a larger calculation, 14 × 3 can be split into 10 × 3 and 4 × 3.

Watch for this: Every object must appear in exactly one part of a split array.

Try it: Use 5 × 3 to work out 7 × 3.

Check the answer and explanation

Five groups give 15. Two more groups give 6. 15 + 6 = 21.

3. Distinguish sharing from grouping

Division can find how many are in each equal group or how many equal groups can be made.

Worked examples: share 24 stickers equally among four children: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 stickers each. Put 24 stickers into packs of six: 24 ÷ 6 = 4 packs.

The same total appears in both problems, but the known group information and answer units differ. Rebuild the total with multiplication to check.

Watch for this: Always attach a unit to a division answer.

Try it: Share 30 counters among five teams. Then group 30 counters into sets of five. What does each answer count?

Check the answer and explanation

Sharing gives 6 counters per team. Grouping gives 6 sets. The numerical answers match, but their meanings differ.

4. Use related facts to find an unknown

Multiplication and division undo each other for these equal-group situations. Use the same whole, group count and group size.

Worked example: from 4 × 8 = 32, write 32 ÷ 4 = 8 and 32 ÷ 8 = 4. To solve □ × 4 = 32, use 32 ÷ 4 = 8.

To find a missing starting total in □ ÷ 5 = 7, rebuild seven groups of five: 7 × 5 = 35.

Watch for this: Division cannot be reversed freely: 32 ÷ 4 and 4 ÷ 32 do not mean the same thing.

Try it: Complete 42 ÷ □ = 7. Check your answer.

Check the answer and explanation

6. Seven groups of six make 42, and 42 ÷ 6 = 7.

5. Partition two-digit numbers

Split the number into parts whose facts you know, calculate each part and recombine.

14 × 310 × 3 = 304 × 3 = 12

Worked example: 14 × 3 = (10 × 3) + (4 × 3) = 30 + 12 = 42.

For 48 ÷ 4, split the total into 40 + 8. Divide both parts by 4: 10 + 2 = 12. Each part has been divided by the same four groups.

Watch for this: Split the total being divided, keeping the divisor unchanged.

Try it: Solve 16 × 4 and 84 ÷ 4 using useful partitions.

Check the answer and explanation

16 × 4 = 40 + 24 = 64. For 84 ÷ 4, use 80 ÷ 4 + 4 ÷ 4 = 20 + 1 = 21.

6. Choose a model and create a story

A multiplication or division sentence should match what the story asks. A picture or bar model can make the missing quantity visible.

Worked example: Mia has 4 beads; Ari has 3 times as many. The comparison bars show three equal parts of 4 for Ari: 3 × 4 = 12 beads. “Three more” would instead mean 4 + 3 = 7. To find Mia’s amount from Ari’s 12 and the three-times relationship, divide 12 by 3.

For grouping, 36 apples packed six per bag give 36 ÷ 6 = 6 bags. Create a new story for 12 × 4 = 48 and label what each number counts. A calculator may check arithmetic after the model is chosen.

Watch for this: A keyword alone does not choose the operation: identify what is known and what is missing.

Try it: Write one grouping story and one sharing story that both use 40 ÷ 5 = 8.

Check the answer and explanation

Examples: 40 beads in packs of 5 make 8 packs; 40 beads shared among 5 children give 8 beads each. Accept other stories with the same equal-group meaning.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • 4 × 6 is not 4 + 6.
  • Every object must appear in exactly one part of a split array.
  • Always attach a unit to a division answer.
  • Division cannot be reversed freely: 32 ÷ 4 and 4 ÷ 32 do not mean the same thing.
  • Split the total being divided, keeping the divisor unchanged.
  • A keyword alone does not choose the operation: identify what is known and what is missing.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Calculate 13 × 4.
  2. Explain two meanings of 28 ÷ 4.
  3. Find □ × 5 = 45.
Answers and next step
  1. 40 + 12 = 52.
  2. Share 28 among four groups: seven each; make groups of four from 28: seven groups.
  3. 9; 9 × 5 = 45.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Build and label the groups with counters. Use exact divisions first, then fade the picture.

Extend within this topic: Find two different useful partitions for 18 × 4. Explain why both preserve all eighteen groups.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
