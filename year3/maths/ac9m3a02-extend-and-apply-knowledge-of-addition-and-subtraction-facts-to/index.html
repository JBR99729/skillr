Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Known small-number facts can help with larger numbers. Extend facts through place value, bridge a useful number, use doubles or compensate, then explain your mental route.

Before you start: Recall facts to 20 and understand hundreds, tens and ones.

Useful words: mental strategy: a way to calculate using number relationships; bridge: pass through a useful benchmark; compensate: adjust and correct.

1. Connect and organise facts to 20

Know how the parts and whole connect, rather than memorising each fact in isolation. Make a systematic list when investigating all possibilities.

Worked example: 8 + 7 = 15 gives 15 − 7 = 8 and 15 − 8 = 7. For two labelled boxes holding 12 counters altogether, begin (12, 0), (11, 1), (10, 2) and continue until (0, 12).

One part falls by one as the other rises by one. Including every first part from 12 to 0 shows why no whole-number pair was skipped.

Watch for this: State whether the boxes are labelled before claiming how many arrangements there are.

Try it: List all pairs for two labelled boxes holding 4 counters altogether.

Check the answer and explanation

(4, 0), (3, 1), (2, 2), (1, 3), (0, 4). The labels make swapped unequal pairs different placements.

2. Extend a fact using place value

Keep the unit attached to the fact. Seven tens plus eight tens is fifteen tens.

7 + 8 = 1570 + 80 = 150150 − 80 = 70

Worked example: from 7 + 8 = 15, derive 70 + 80 = 150 and 150 − 80 = 70. From 6 + 6 = 12, derive 600 + 600 = 1 200.

For 160 + 70, think 16 tens + 7 tens = 23 tens: 230. The same number relationship can cross a hundred.

Watch for this: Fifteen tens is 150, not 15.

Try it: Use 9 + 6 = 15 to solve 90 + 60 and 150 − 90.

Check the answer and explanation

150 and 60. Explain that the units are tens.

3. Bridge through a useful ten or hundred

Split the amount being added or removed so the first step reaches a convenient benchmark.

Worked example: 68 + 7: add 2 to reach 70, then the remaining 5. Answer 75. For 203 − 8, remove 3 to reach 200, then remove the remaining 5: 195.

Record both parts of the split. For 68 + 7, 2 and 5 still make all seven.

Watch for this: After reaching the benchmark, remember the remaining part.

Try it: Work out 397 + 8 and 302 − 6 by bridging.

Check the answer and explanation

397 + 3 + 5 = 405; 302 − 2 − 4 = 296.

4. Use doubles and nearby doubles

A known double can anchor a nearby fact. Decide which addend changed and by how much.

Worked example: 48 + 49 is one more than 48 + 48. Double 48 is 96, so the sum is 97. Alternatively, 49 + 49 − 1 gives 97.

Extend the relationship: 160 + 170 is 160 + 160 + 10 = 330. Explain why the correction is ten, not one.

Watch for this: The correction must match the actual difference between the addends.

Try it: Use a double to find 75 + 76.

Check the answer and explanation

75 + 75 = 150; one more gives 151.

5. Compensate and preserve a relationship

Use a round number temporarily, then correct for the amount you changed.

Worked example: 298 + 46 = 300 + 46 − 2 = 344. For 364 − 99, subtract 100 and restore one: 265.

A sum stays the same if one addend rises by two and the other falls by two: 298 + 46 = 300 + 44. A difference stays the same when both numbers change by the same amount: 364 − 99 = 365 − 100.

Watch for this: If too much was subtracted, add the excess back.

Try it: Solve 247 + 98 and 503 − 198 mentally.

Check the answer and explanation

247 + 100 − 2 = 345. For subtraction, 503 − 200 + 2 = 305.

6. Choose, compare and explain an efficient route

Different correct strategies may suit different people and numbers. Explain why a particular route is convenient.

Worked example: 27 + 46 + 73 can be reordered as (27 + 73) + 46 = 146. The friendly pair makes 100.

For 502 − 497, counting up five is shorter than a long written calculation. For 276 + 143, partitioning may be clearer. Use mental reasoning for this skill; small written jottings can record your route.

Watch for this: Addition can be reordered; subtraction cannot generally be reordered the same way.

Try it: Choose a mental route for 39 + 24 + 61 and explain it.

Check the answer and explanation

Pair 39 + 61 = 100, then add 24: 124. Other correct explained mental routes are valid.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • State whether the boxes are labelled before claiming how many arrangements there are.
  • Fifteen tens is 150, not 15.
  • After reaching the benchmark, remember the remaining part.
  • The correction must match the actual difference between the addends.
  • If too much was subtracted, add the excess back.
  • Addition can be reordered; subtraction cannot generally be reordered the same way.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Extend 8 + 6 = 14 to 80 + 60.
  2. Calculate 602 − 9 mentally.
  3. Find 36 + 57 + 64 efficiently.
Answers and next step
  1. 140: fourteen tens.
  2. 602 − 2 − 7 = 593.
  3. (36 + 64) + 57 = 157.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use ten-frames for the small fact, then rename the counters as tens. Offer a number line for each bridge.

Extend within this topic: Compare two mental methods for 398 + 197. Explain the corrections carefully, and check that both give 595.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
