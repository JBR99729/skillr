Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Use suitable instruments with labelled markings. Check zero, understand the spaces on the scale, record the unit and compare measured quantities.

Before you start: Recognise cm, m, g, kg, mL and L; read counting steps on a number line.

Useful words: instrument: a measuring tool; interval: the amount between neighbouring marks; zero: the starting reference; capacity: the amount a container can hold.

1. Prepare a suitable instrument

Choose a tool for the attribute and the size of the measurement. Look at its range and labelled scale before using it.

Worked examples: use a ruler for a short rigid object, a flexible measuring tape for the girth of a tree, scales for mass and a graduated jug for liquid.

Check that scales read zero when empty. Put a jug on a level surface. For a measuring tape, keep the tape against the surface without stretching it.

Watch for this: The tool must measure the intended attribute and have a suitable range.

Try it: Which tool would measure the mass of flour? What must you check before adding it?

Check the answer and explanation

Scales. Check zero and, if a container is used, tare it or subtract its separately measured mass.

2. Measure length from zero

A ruler’s marks bound the centimetre spaces. Align the object with zero, then read its other endpoint.

Worked example: an object begins at 0 cm and ends at 13 cm. Its length is 13 cm. If it begins at 2 cm and ends at 15 cm, its length is also 15 − 2 = 13 cm.

A centimetre-grid strip can become a measuring tape if its equal units and zero are labelled. An endpoint halfway between two centimetre marks gives a half-centimetre reading where the instrument supports it.

Watch for this: Use the zero mark, which may be inside the physical edge of the ruler.

Try it: A ribbon runs from the 3 cm mark to the 17 cm mark. How long is it?

Check the answer and explanation

14 cm: 17 − 3. The endpoint number alone is not its length.

3. Read mass scales and separate contents

Work out each interval before reading a pointer. Record whether the measurement includes the container.

Worked example: a scale is labelled 0, 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 g with equal intervals. A pointer at the fourth step after zero reads 400 g.

A bowl and flour together read 650 g; the empty bowl is 150 g. The flour has mass 500 g. Taring the empty bowl would let the scale show the flour directly.

Watch for this: A 100 g interval does not become one gram because it is one space.

Try it: A bag and empty container read 850 g together. The container is 200 g. Find the contents’ mass.

Check the answer and explanation

650 g. Subtract the container mass, or tare first when measuring.

4. Read liquid markings and capacity

Read the supplied unit and equal interval size. Keep the jug level and view the liquid surface at eye level.

Worked example: equal marks are labelled 0, 200, 400, 600, 800 and 1 000 mL. Liquid at the third interval above zero is 600 mL.

A two-litre container with 600 mL inside has capacity 2 L and current contents 600 mL. To compare capacities directly, fill to the stated capacity mark or measure the maximum safely with suitable equipment.

Measure it: with suitable supervision, use two stable plastic measuring containers and room-temperature water. Read their labelled intervals, measure out 200 mL in one and 300 mL in the other, and check the levels at eye height. Record the unit and compare: the second contains 100 mL more. Compare maximum capacities separately if required; do not confuse these poured amounts with capacity.

Watch for this: Current liquid level and maximum capacity are different quantities.

Try it: A scale goes up by 100 mL per space. What is the reading four spaces above 200 mL?

Check the answer and explanation

600 mL: 200 + 4 × 100.

5. Compare using common units

Compare the same attribute and use compatible units. Equal numbers with different units may mean different quantities.

Worked examples: a 36 cm strip is 8 cm longer than a 28 cm strip. A 1 L container holds more than a 750 mL container because 1 L = 1 000 mL.

For masses 450 g, 700 g and 450 g, order 450 g = 450 g < 700 g. Include ties instead of inventing a difference.

Watch for this: Compare the measurements, not the apparent sizes of drawings.

Try it: Which has greater capacity, 900 mL or 1 L, and by how much?

Check the answer and explanation

1 L has 100 mL more capacity.

6. Diagnose, correct and remeasure

An answer is only as reliable as the measuring procedure. Name the actual error and choose a correction.

Worked example: a pencil starts at 4 cm and ends at 16 cm. Calling it 16 cm ignores the start: the measured length is 12 cm. Reposition at zero and remeasure to check.

Try one safe measurement of each attribute with an adult if needed: measure a strip, weigh a sealed object and compare two container capacities. Record the reading and unit yourself.

Watch for this: Repeating the same faulty procedure does not repair its error.

Try it: A scale already shows 120 g with an empty bowl. What should you do before measuring ingredients?

Check the answer and explanation

Tare the bowl to zero, or record its mass and subtract it from the combined reading. Check the scale is stable.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • The tool must measure the intended attribute and have a suitable range.
  • Use the zero mark, which may be inside the physical edge of the ruler.
  • A 100 g interval does not become one gram because it is one space.
  • Current liquid level and maximum capacity are different quantities.
  • Compare the measurements, not the apparent sizes of drawings.
  • Repeating the same faulty procedure does not repair its error.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Find the length from 5 cm to 18 cm.
  2. On a scale with 50 g steps, read six steps above zero.
  3. Explain why a half-full 1 L bottle does not have capacity 500 mL.
Answers and next step
  1. 13 cm.
  2. 300 g.
  3. Its capacity is still 1 L; approximately 500 mL describes its current contents.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use a ruler with clear whole-centimetre marks and a jug with large labelled intervals. Count spaces aloud before reading.

Extend within this topic: Make an accurate paper tape with labelled centimetres. Use it to compare two curved boundaries, explaining how you avoided gaps, overlaps and stretching.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
