Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Look at an object’s features, build and compare models, and explain how a feature helps with a particular use.

Before you start: Recognise familiar 2D shapes and common 3D objects; count small collections.

Useful words: face: a flat surface; curved surface: a rounded surface; edge: where faces meet; vertex: a corner point; classify: group using a stated rule.

1. Identify features of 3D objects

Turn an object to inspect surfaces that a single drawing may hide. Describe flat faces, curved surfaces, edges and vertices consistently.

Worked examples: a cube has six square flat faces, twelve straight edges and eight vertices. A sphere has one continuous curved surface and no flat faces. A cylinder has two circular flat faces and a curved surface.

Use actual objects alongside diagrams. Do not count only the faces visible in one view.

Watch for this: An object’s full set of features may include hidden faces.

Try it: How does a cylinder differ from a sphere in its flat faces?

Check the answer and explanation

A cylinder has two circular flat faces; a sphere has none.

2. Compare and classify with a rule

State the feature used to make a group, then check every object against it. The same objects can be sorted in several valid ways.

Worked example: for the rule “has a flat face”, cubes, rectangular prisms and cylinders belong; a sphere does not. For “has only flat faces”, the cylinder moves out.

Compare prisms and pyramids using their faces and vertices. A prism has two matching, parallel end faces with straight sides, joined by flat side faces. A pyramid has a base and triangular side faces meeting at an apex.

Watch for this: A group name needs a feature rule, not just “these look alike”.

Try it: Can a cube belong in both “only flat faces” and “six faces”? Explain.

Check the answer and explanation

Yes. Different rules can overlap: a cube satisfies both.

3. Make and compare block models

Count all unit cubes, including hidden ones. Compare how many cubes models use and how their base and height differ.

Worked example: a rectangular block model has a base of three cubes by two cubes and is two cubes high. Each layer has 6 cubes; two layers use 12 cubes. A two-by-two base with the same height uses 8 cubes.

Build both models and separate layers to verify hidden cubes. This compares space occupied using unit cubes; a formal volume formula is not required.

Watch for this: State when a layer is complete before multiplying its cube count.

Try it: A rectangular model has two layers of five cubes each. How many cubes? What would three identical layers use?

Check the answer and explanation

10 cubes, then 15. Count every full layer, not just the visible front row.

4. Connect solid, skeleton and digital models

Different models show different features. A solid model shows faces; a skeleton highlights edges and vertices.

Worked example: a cube skeleton uses twelve equal straight straws for its edges and eight joins for its vertices. It has six square face openings; the straws themselves are not the faces.

Make a safe skeleton with suitable connectors and adult help if needed. If geometry software is available, rotate a digital object and compare what each view reveals. A picture of a model is not evidence that the learner built it.

Watch for this: Do not confuse the pieces of a representation with the features represented.

Try it: What feature does a straw edge show? What is missing from an open skeleton?

Check the answer and explanation

A straw shows an edge. The face surfaces are not filled in, although their boundaries can be identified.

5. Choose features for a practical use

Connect an observed feature to the intended job. Test a claim when stability, rolling or strength depends on conditions.

Worked examples: a box with flat faces can stack. A sphere can roll in many directions. A cylinder can stand on a flat end or roll on its curved side.

For a tower, compare a narrow base with a wider base using the same safe materials. For a model bridge, compare braced and unbraced shapes under the same small test load. Record what happened instead of assuming every taller or triangular model is always strongest.

Watch for this: Suitability depends on the object, material and use, not the shape name alone.

Try it: Why might a rectangular prism be useful for a storage box?

Check the answer and explanation

Its flat faces allow it to sit and stack, and its shape can enclose usable space. Explain in relation to the actual storage need.

6. Explain and improve a design

Describe the features, give evidence for their use and change a feature to meet a stated need.

Worked example: a tall block tower topples under a gentle, consistent test. Widen its base, repeat the same test and compare stability. Report the actual improvement or lack of improvement.

Identify familiar objects on Country/Place by geometric features. When investigating First Nations dwellings and their orientation to climate, use a named, authorised local source and discuss the documented design; do not generalise one community’s knowledge to all peoples.

Watch for this: A practical claim needs a specified test and evidence, rather than an attractive drawing alone.

Try it: A display model needs to remain upright. What could you test and change?

Check the answer and explanation

For example, compare base widths while holding height and material steady. Report observations and justify any design change.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • An object’s full set of features may include hidden faces.
  • A group name needs a feature rule, not just “these look alike”.
  • State when a layer is complete before multiplying its cube count.
  • Do not confuse the pieces of a representation with the features represented.
  • Suitability depends on the object, material and use, not the shape name alone.
  • A practical claim needs a specified test and evidence, rather than an attractive drawing alone.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Name a feature shared by a cube and a rectangular prism.
  2. Explain how a cylinder can both stand and roll.
  3. Two complete layers contain six cubes each. How many cubes altogether?
Answers and next step
  1. Both have only flat faces; both have twelve straight edges and eight vertices.
  2. It can stand on a circular flat end and roll on its curved side.
  3. 12 cubes, including any hidden ones.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Handle a cube, cylinder and sphere. Use one clear sorting rule, then build a small complete block model.

Extend within this topic: Design two models for the same purpose. State the comparison rule, keep the test fair and explain which features helped.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
