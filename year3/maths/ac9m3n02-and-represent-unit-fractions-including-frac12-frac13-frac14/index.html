Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Fractions name equal parts of a stated whole. Count halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and tenths, then combine parts of the same size to make one whole.

Before you start: Share objects equally; recognise one whole and count equal groups.

Useful words: unit fraction: one equal part; denominator: how many equal parts make the whole; numerator: how many of those parts are selected.

1. Find the whole and equal parts

Name the whole before naming its fractions. A unit fraction has one selected part: 1/3 is one of three equal parts of that whole.

Worked example: split one strip into five equal sections. One section is 1/5. Split an equally long strip into ten equal sections: one section is 1/10. More equal parts make each part smaller when the wholes match.

Fold or draw strips for 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5 and 1/10. Check equal size, rather than just counting the pieces.

Watch for this: Four pieces are not automatically four quarters.

Try it: A whole is cut into four pieces, but one piece is larger than the others. Can every piece be called one-quarter?

Check the answer and explanation

No. Quarters must be four equal parts of the same whole.

2. Count multiples of one part

Keep the size of each part fixed and count how many are selected. Three lots of one-quarter make three-quarters.

Worked example: the strip has four equal parts and three are shaded: 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. The denominator remains 4 because the part size has not changed.

In words, say “three quarters”. The same fraction can be shown with a folded strip, an area model or a matching point on a number line.

Watch for this: The numerator counts selected parts; it does not count all the parts.

Try it: Draw one whole split into ten equal parts and select seven. Name the fraction.

Check the answer and explanation

7/10: seven tenths of the stated whole.

3. Show fractions as lengths and positions

On a number line, the distance from 0 to 1 is one whole. Divide that distance into equal spaces.

Worked example: divide 0 to 1 into five equal spaces. Starting at 0, count 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, 5/5. The third step lands at 3/5; the fifth lands at 1.

Use a strip of the same whole length under the line to connect a selected length with its endpoint.

Watch for this: Count the intervals from zero; zero is not the first fraction step.

Try it: Where is 2/3 on a line with three equal spaces from 0 to 1?

Check the answer and explanation

At the second mark after 0. Three steps of one-third reach 1.

4. Find one equal fraction of a collection

For a collection, the whole is the complete set. Share it into the number of equal groups named by the denominator.

Worked example: find 1/4 of 20 counters. Share all 20 into four equal groups: each has five counters. One of those groups is one-quarter of the collection, so the answer is 5 counters.

Check that all counters are used and every group has the same number.

Watch for this: One-quarter names a relationship to the whole; it does not always mean one object.

Try it: Find 1/3 of 18 buttons. Explain how you grouped them.

Check the answer and explanation

6 buttons. Make three equal groups of six; one group is one-third.

5. Find several equal fractions of a collection

First find one unit fraction, then take the required number of those equal groups.

Worked example: find 3/5 of 20. One-fifth is 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Three such groups contain 3 × 4 = 12.

Draw five boxes with four counters each; select three boxes. The unselected two boxes contain eight counters, and 12 + 8 checks the whole of 20.

Watch for this: Divide by the denominator before counting the selected equal groups.

Try it: Find 2/3 of 18 counters. How many are left?

Check the answer and explanation

One-third is 6. Two-thirds is 12; 6 counters are left.

6. Complete one whole

Parts with the same denominator have the same size when they use the same whole. Count how many are still needed.

Worked example: 2/5 needs 3/5 more: 2/5 + 3/5 = 5/5 = 1. The two selected parts and the three remaining parts are all fifths.

Another example is 6/10 + 4/10 = 1. Cover the unshaded part with matching pieces and explain why no gap or overlap remains.

Watch for this: Do not add denominators: 2/5 + 3/5 is five fifths, not five tenths.

Try it: Complete 3/4 + __ = 1. Then write two different pairs of tenths that make one.

Check the answer and explanation

1/4. Examples: 1/10 + 9/10 = 1 and 3/10 + 7/10 = 1. Other pairs totalling ten tenths work.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Four pieces are not automatically four quarters.
  • The numerator counts selected parts; it does not count all the parts.
  • Count the intervals from zero; zero is not the first fraction step.
  • One-quarter names a relationship to the whole; it does not always mean one object.
  • Divide by the denominator before counting the selected equal groups.
  • Do not add denominators: 2/5 + 3/5 is five fifths, not five tenths.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Show 1/3 of a strip.
  2. Find 2/5 of 15 objects.
  3. What completes 7/10 to make one whole?
Answers and next step
  1. Divide the whole strip into three equal parts and select one.
  2. One-fifth is 3; two-fifths is 6 objects.
  3. 3/10; seven tenths and three tenths make ten tenths.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use identical paper strips and real counters. Say the whole aloud before sharing; begin with one unit fraction.

Extend within this topic: Make three different same-denominator fraction pairs that complete one whole. Explain why a pair with unlike part sizes cannot be checked by simply adding the numerators.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
