Repeated Chance Experiments

Repeated chance experiments can produce different results even when the conditions stay the same. Recording every trial makes variation visible.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

Learning intention: conduct repeated chance experiments; identify and describe possible outcomes, record the results, recognise and discuss the variation.

Students keep conditions consistent, tally each trial immediately and compare frequencies across repeated sets.

Concept boundary: focus on observed frequencies and variation, not formal theoretical probability.

Key vocabulary and representations

Chance experiment — a repeatable action with an uncertain result. Trial — one repeat. Frequency — how often an outcome occurs. Variation — differences between repeated sets of results.

Teaching progression and worked examples
  1. List possible outcomes.
  2. Repeat the experiment under the same conditions.
  3. Tally each result.
  4. Compare two result sets and discuss variation.

Example: 20 spinner trials produce 12 red and 8 blue; another 20 produce 9 red and 11 blue. Different frequencies are possible even with the same spinner.

Curriculum elaborations and how they are taught

Use coins, spinners, dice and other simple devices to conduct repeated trials, record frequencies in tables and compare separate runs without expecting perfect equality.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Expecting equal counts: compare multiple small samples.
  • Changing conditions: keep the device and procedure consistent.
  • Recording from memory: tally each trial immediately.
Support, Core and Extend
  • Support: use 10 trials and a prepared tally table.
  • Core: conduct 20 trials and describe frequencies.
  • Extend: compare two groups' results and explain variation.
Quick check and mastery evidence

Students identify all possible outcomes, record a short run accurately and explain why another run may differ. That explanation is the key mastery evidence.
