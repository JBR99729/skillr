10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Compare two sets of coin tosses

Toss a coin ten times and tally heads and tails. Repeat with a fresh tally. Check that each tally totals ten, then compare the results.

Adjust the challenge: Start with five tosses per set. Extend by comparing your two sets with someone else’s.

FAQ

Must both sets of ten tosses give the same result?

No. The possible outcomes stay the same, but the counts can vary.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: List possible outcomes, run repeated trials and record every result. Compare sets of results and explain why chance can produce variation.

Before you start: Use chance words, make tally groups of five and count frequencies.

Useful words: trial: one performance of an experiment; frequency: how often an outcome occurred; variation: differences between results; replacement: putting an item back before the next draw.

1. Define an experiment and its outcomes

State the action and the outcome you will record. Keep the procedure consistent so different trial sets can be compared.

Worked example: toss a coin and record heads or tails. Decide beforehand to repeat a toss if the coin lands on its edge or leaves the agreed area.

For a bag experiment, state whether the counter is returned and mixed before each new draw. With replacement, the same contents remain available; without it, later conditions change.

Watch for this: Distinguish a recorded outcome category from a physical face or object.

Try it: Plan a die experiment recording whether the top face is a 6 or not a 6. What are the two outcome categories?

Check the answer and explanation

6 and not 6. Both categories must be defined before recording; “not 6” includes 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5.

2. Predict without promising an exact count

Use the experiment conditions to make a prediction, while allowing ordinary chance variation.

Worked example: for ten tosses of a fair coin, predict that both heads and tails may appear. Five of each is possible, but not guaranteed. Even a longer run need not have exactly equal counts.

Write the prediction before seeing results. Keep the actual results unchanged, even if they differ from the prediction.

Watch for this: A fair process can produce uneven counts in a short run.

Try it: Must a fair coin give one head and one tail in two tosses? Explain.

Check the answer and explanation

No. Two heads, two tails, or one of each are possible. Fairness does not force short-run equality.

3. Conduct trials and record once

Complete the agreed trial, record its outcome once and reset the apparatus as planned. Group tally marks in fives.

Worked example data
OutcomeFrequency
Heads6
Tails4
Total10

Worked example: ten recorded coin tosses are H, T, T, H, H, T, H, T, H, H. Count six heads and four tails. A frequency table totals 6 + 4 = 10, matching all ten trials.

Now carry out a short safe coin or spinner experiment, with an adult if needed. The learner should make the record; supplied results alone do not show that an experiment was performed.

Watch for this: Do not count a heading, a key or a prediction as a trial result.

Try it: Count the outcomes in H, T, H, T, T, H. Check the total.

Check the answer and explanation

3 heads and 3 tails; total 6 trials.

4. Read and check result records

A frequency describes what occurred in that set. Check each row against the raw record, as well as the overall total.

Worked example: a 20-trial spinner record has 12 red and 8 blue. The total is 20. If only 7 blue results were recorded in the table, one blue result has been lost.

A correct total alone does not guarantee correct categories: changing one red to blue would preserve the total but alter both frequencies.

Watch for this: A total check detects some errors; checking the categories detects others.

Try it: Fifteen trials gave 9 red outcomes. Every other outcome was blue. How many blue results were there?

Check the answer and explanation

6 blue: 15 − 9. Verify 9 + 6 = 15.

5. Compare repeated sets and describe variation

Compare like experiments and state the size of each set. Describe the observed difference without declaring one run the only correct result.

SetHeadsTailsTotal
A6410
B4610

Worked example: set A has 6 heads and 4 tails; set B has 4 heads and 6 tails. Both used ten tosses. A has two more heads than B, yet both are possible results of a fair process.

Combining gives 10 heads and 10 tails across 20 tosses in this example. That exact equality happened here; it is not a rule for every combined set.

Watch for this: Do not compare raw counts as if sets of different sizes were the same experiment size.

Try it: One ten-trial set has 7 red results and another has 5. Describe the variation.

Check the answer and explanation

The first set has two more red results. Different counts can occur under the same chance procedure.

6. Discuss the method and evidence limits

Repeat the same procedure, retain honest results and distinguish observations from guarantees.

Worked example: blue appeared on four of ten draws. The record supports “blue occurred four times in this set”. It does not establish “blue must occur next” or “all future sets will have four blue”.

Repeated testing also helps people study how computer recommendation systems behave. Real user behaviour is more complex than a coin toss; this is a comparison about collecting repeated evidence, not a claim that a coin model explains every system.

Watch for this: A previous run does not make an independent outcome “due”.

Try it: A coin has shown heads three times. Is tails now certain? What should you record next?

Check the answer and explanation

Tails is not certain on the next independent toss. Conduct it normally and record whichever result actually occurs.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Distinguish a recorded outcome category from a physical face or object.
  • A fair process can produce uneven counts in a short run.
  • Do not count a heading, a key or a prediction as a trial result.
  • A total check detects some errors; checking the categories detects others.
  • Do not compare raw counts as if sets of different sizes were the same experiment size.
  • A previous run does not make an independent outcome “due”.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Why list outcomes before running trials?
  2. A set has 8 red and 12 blue results. How many trials?
  3. Two fair-coin sets differ. Does that alone prove a recording error?
Answers and next step
  1. To define consistent categories and avoid losing or changing results.
  2. 20.
  3. No. Check the procedure and raw record, but ordinary chance variation can explain different sets.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use one two-outcome experiment and a small prepared table. Record each trial immediately before the next.

Extend within this topic: Carry out two equally sized sets using the same apparatus. Compare each category, explain variation and identify any procedure change that would make the comparison weaker.
