Addition and subtraction connections

Addition joins parts; subtraction finds a missing part or undoes a change. Use these connections to partition numbers, find unknown values and check number sentences.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Addition joins parts; subtraction finds a missing part or undoes a change. Use these connections to partition numbers, find unknown values and check number sentences.

Before you start: Know addition and subtraction facts and represent a number as a whole with parts.

Useful words: inverse operations: operations that undo each other; whole: the complete amount; part: one amount within the whole; equal: the same value on both sides.

1. Build a whole and its parts

A part–part–whole model keeps the whole distinct from its two parts. You can partition the same whole in different ways.

Worked example: a whole of 62 has parts 37 and 25: 37 + 25 = 62. Removing 25 leaves 37; removing 37 leaves 25.

Another partition is 40 and 22. Move counters between the two parts, keeping the whole of 62 unchanged, and record the new addition and subtraction facts.

Watch for this: Find the whole before choosing which subtraction to write.

Try it: Show two different partitions of 84. Write one related subtraction for each.

Check the answer and explanation

Examples: 50 + 34 = 84, so 84 − 50 = 34; 60 + 24 = 84, so 84 − 24 = 60. Accept other valid partitions.

2. Explain a fact family

Two unequal parts and their whole give two addition facts and two subtraction facts. Explain each as joining or separating the same parts.

Worked example: 16 + 8 = 24; 8 + 16 = 24; 24 − 8 = 16; 24 − 16 = 8. Addition allows the parts to swap; subtraction starts with the whole.

If both parts are 12, the turn-around facts repeat: 12 + 12 = 24 and 24 − 12 = 12. There are fewer distinct sentences, not a missing new number.

Watch for this: 29 − 65 = 36 is not a related whole-number subtraction.

Try it: Write the four related facts for 29, 36 and 65.

Check the answer and explanation

29 + 36 = 65; 36 + 29 = 65; 65 − 29 = 36; 65 − 36 = 29.

3. Find an unknown addend

An unknown addend is a missing part. Find it by subtracting the known part from the whole or by counting up.

Worked example: 29 + □ = 84. Count 29 → 30 (+1), → 80 (+50), → 84 (+4). The missing part is 55. Check 29 + 55 = 84.

A calculator can evaluate 84 − 29 after the inverse relationship is identified. Explain why that calculation finds the missing part.

Watch for this: The missing number is a part, not the total.

Try it: Find □ in 47 + □ = 93. Check by substitution.

Check the answer and explanation

46. Substituting gives 47 + 46 = 93.

4. Find an unknown start or removed amount

The location of the unknown changes the calculation. Ask whether it names the starting whole or the part removed.

Worked examples: □ − 28 = 47 has an unknown start: rebuild the whole, 47 + 28 = 75. In 75 − □ = 47, the missing removed part is 75 − 47 = 28.

Draw the same whole and parts for both sentences, then put the box over a different quantity. Check the complete original sentence.

Watch for this: Do not use subtraction automatically just because the sentence contains a minus sign.

Try it: Solve □ − 36 = 58, then solve 94 − □ = 58.

Check the answer and explanation

94, then 36. Both use the same whole and parts: 58 + 36 = 94.

5. Treat equality as a balance

The equal sign says both sides have the same value. It does not simply mean “write an answer next”.

Worked example: 28 + □ = 19 + 24. The right side is 43, so the left needs 43 − 28 = 15. Check 28 + 15 = 19 + 24 = 43.

The whole can also be written first: 84 = 29 + 55 is true. Read the relationship in either direction.

Watch for this: Calculate or explain both sides; the box is not always the total.

Try it: Find □ in 72 − □ = 30 + 18.

Check the answer and explanation

24. The right side is 48, and 72 − 24 = 48.

6. Connect a story, model and inverse check

Identify what each quantity means in the story, then choose a matching representation.

Worked example: Ravi spends $245 and has $138 left. His starting money is unknown: □ − 245 = 138. Rebuild it with 245 + 138 = $383. Check $383 − $245 = $138.

A context can also come from a locally authorised First Nations story or dance illustrating joining and separating. Use its actual description and permissions, identify the mathematical action and write a matching sentence; do not invent cultural meanings.

Watch for this: Use the inverse to check the original relationship, not an unrelated calculation.

Try it: A collection has 96 shells after 27 were added. How many were there first?

Check the answer and explanation

69 shells. □ + 27 = 96; 96 − 27 = 69; check 69 + 27 = 96.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Find the whole before choosing which subtraction to write.
  • 29 − 65 = 36 is not a related whole-number subtraction.
  • The missing number is a part, not the total.
  • Do not use subtraction automatically just because the sentence contains a minus sign.
  • Calculate or explain both sides; the box is not always the total.
  • Use the inverse to check the original relationship, not an unrelated calculation.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Solve 38 + □ = 91.
  2. Solve □ − 47 = 26.
  3. Solve 15 + □ = 28 + 9.
Answers and next step
  1. 53; 38 + 53 = 91.
  2. 73; 73 − 47 = 26.
  3. 22; each side then equals 37.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use a bar or counters with a cover over one part. Move the cover between the start, change and result to compare unknowns.

Extend within this topic: Make a true equation with addition on one side and subtraction on the other. Hide one value and explain two ways to recover it.
