Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: An estimate uses a known reference or convenient numbers. Use it to judge whether a quantity or calculation makes sense, and explain when an exact check is needed.

Before you start: Count groups of ten and understand addition, subtraction and simple equal groups.

Useful words: estimate: a reasoned approximate amount; benchmark: a known reference; reasonable: consistent with the information and approximate size.

1. Use a known small collection

Compare an unknown collection with a group you know. An estimate is supported by a reference, even when its exact count is not yet known.

Worked example: a labelled reference shows ten counters. The larger pictured collection contains three similar-sized clusters. An initial estimate is about 30 counters. Check the actual collection afterwards and revise your benchmark if needed.

For a physical collection, point out the ten-object reference first. Large objects do not automatically mean more objects.

Watch for this: Do not call an exact count an estimate.

Try it: How could a group of ten help you estimate a jar of buttons without counting them all?

Check the answer and explanation

Compare the reference with the visible quantity, estimate how many similar groups fit, and multiply by ten. Explain visibility and packing limits; a defensible estimate may be a range.

2. Scale a representative part

Use one part of a collection to estimate the whole only when that part is reasonably representative.

Worked example: a tray has four equal-sized regions with similar bead density. A counted sample region has 12 beads. Estimate 4 × 12 = 48 beads in the tray.

If the sample is the crowded corner and the rest is sparse, 48 may be too high. Compare several regions or choose a more typical region before scaling.

Watch for this: Equal-sized regions need not contain equal numbers of objects.

Try it: Six similar rows each hold about eight shells. Estimate the total. Why might the estimate fail?

Check the answer and explanation

About 48 shells. It may fail if row lengths or gaps differ greatly.

3. Choose useful nearby numbers

Choose an approximation that fits the purpose. Nearby tens give more detail; nearby hundreds may make a rough check easier.

Worked example: 247 is close to 250 when working with tens, and close to 200 when working with hundreds. On a line from 200 to 300, it is 47 from 200 and 53 from 300.

For checking a classroom total, nearby tens may be useful. For making sure there are enough seats, a nearest-number estimate alone can be unsafe: compare the actual need with the available seats.

Watch for this: Say what precision you used; two different estimates can both be useful.

Try it: Which nearby ten would you use for 368? Which nearby hundred?

Check the answer and explanation

370 and 400. Explain the place used and distances to the alternatives.

4. Estimate a sum

Choose nearby or compatible addends, then add those approximate amounts. Keep an approximate answer separate from an exact answer.

Worked example: estimate 198 + 306 using hundreds: 200 + 300 = about 500. The exact total is 504, which is close.

For 47 + 52, compatible numbers 50 + 50 give about 100. Explain what changed rather than silently replacing the numbers.

Watch for this: Keep the addition operation when replacing the numbers.

Try it: Estimate 284 + 127 using nearby tens.

Check the answer and explanation

280 + 130 = about 410. The exact sum is 411, so this is a useful check.

5. Estimate a difference

Keep the subtraction relationship and approximate both quantities sensibly. Moving both numbers can change the size of the gap.

Worked example: 603 − 287 is about 600 − 300 = 300. The exact difference is 316. The estimate suggests a few hundred, so an answer of 3 160 is implausible.

For close values, rough hundreds may erase a useful difference. An estimate for 398 − 392 is better made with tens or by noticing that the numbers are only a few apart.

Watch for this: A rounded difference may be too large or too small; check the actual changes.

Try it: Estimate 517 − 193 using nearby hundreds.

Check the answer and explanation

500 − 200 = about 300. The exact difference is 324.

6. Judge a claim and know when to check exactly

An estimate can reject an implausible answer. Being near the estimate does not prove an exact answer is correct.

Worked example: six packs cost $9 each. Estimate the cost as about 6 × $10 = $60. A proposed total of $540 is far too large. The exact cost is 6 × $9 = $54.

If a rounded total is $20 and the budget is $20, calculate more precisely before deciding. A small rounding difference could change whether the purchase fits.

Watch for this: An estimate checks size and context; exact equality needs an exact check.

Try it: A student says 198 + 306 = 509 because it is close to 500. Is that enough evidence?

Check the answer and explanation

No. It is plausible in size, but the exact sum is 504. Use an exact calculation or inverse check.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Do not call an exact count an estimate.
  • Equal-sized regions need not contain equal numbers of objects.
  • Say what precision you used; two different estimates can both be useful.
  • Keep the addition operation when replacing the numbers.
  • A rounded difference may be too large or too small; check the actual changes.
  • An estimate checks size and context; exact equality needs an exact check.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Estimate 392 + 208 using hundreds.
  2. Four similar sample sections each have about 15 items. Estimate the whole.
  3. Does an estimate of $30 prove a $30 budget is enough?
Answers and next step
  1. 400 + 200 = about 600.
  2. About 60 items, if the sections are representative.
  3. No. Refine or calculate the total exactly when the estimate is at the budget boundary.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Count a reference group of ten, then use two or three comparable groups. State “about” and accept explained ranges.

Extend within this topic: Find a pair of numbers where rounding each to the nearest hundred is a poor way to estimate their small difference. Explain why.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
