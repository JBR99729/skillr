Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Build multiplication facts from equal groups and patterns, retrieve them accurately and use them to find related division facts.

Before you start: Make equal groups, skip-count and recall doubles and twos as helpful prerequisites.

Useful words: multiple: a total from whole-number groups; related facts: equations using the same groups and total; recall: retrieve a known fact.

1. Give a fact meaning with groups and arrays

Say what each factor counts. Objects, equal rows and repeated addition all show why a multiplication fact works.

Worked example: three rows of six counters make 3 × 6 = 18. The same array can be read as six columns of three: 6 × 3 = 18.

Build arrays for the 3, 4, 5 and 10 facts. Explain rows and columns before gradually removing the picture.

Watch for this: Unequal groups cannot be described by one repeated equal-group fact.

Try it: Draw four equal rows of five. Write two matching multiplication facts.

Check the answer and explanation

4 × 5 = 20 and 5 × 4 = 20. The complete array contains twenty counters.

2. Find and explain patterns

Moving to the next fact adds one more group. Link the list to the group size, not just the order of the answers.

Worked example: for groups of 5, record 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. The units digit alternates 0 and 5. A repeated-addition algorithm starts at 0, adds 5 and records each total.

A calculator or spreadsheet can generate more totals after the rule is understood. Check the first few by hand and relate each new total to one extra group.

Watch for this: The starting zero is not one group.

Try it: Complete the four-group totals: 0, 4, 8, __, __, __. What changed each time?

Check the answer and explanation

12, 16, 20. Each step adds another group of four.

3. Build a forgotten fact from a known fact

Use a known neighbouring group, double or split array to derive the fact.

Worked example: 4 × 7 is double 2 × 7: double 14 is 28. For 3 × 8, use 2 × 8 + 1 × 8 = 16 + 8 = 24.

To find 5 × 6, halve 10 × 6 = 60: half is 30. Explain which number of equal groups has been halved.

Watch for this: One fewer group means subtract the group size, not subtract one.

Try it: Use 5 × 8 = 40 to find 4 × 8.

Check the answer and explanation

Remove one group of eight: 40 − 8 = 32.

4. Recall and check mixed facts

Practise the 3, 4, 5 and 10 facts in mixed order, with models available when a fact is uncertain.

Worked examples: 3 × 9 = 27; 4 × 6 = 24; 5 × 7 = 35; 10 × 8 = 80.

Check a recalled fact with a related known fact. For 4 × 6, double 2 × 6 = 12 to get 24. Explain boundary facts too: 0 × 5 = 0 and 1 × 5 = 5.

Watch for this: Build accuracy and flexible recall; speed alone does not show understanding.

Try it: Find 3 × 8, 4 × 9, 5 × 6 and 10 × 7. Explain one useful check.

Check the answer and explanation

24, 36, 30, 70. For example, 4 × 9 is double 2 × 9 = 18.

5. Use multiplication to divide

A known product connects to two division facts. Use a drawing to distinguish sharing from finding how many groups.

Worked example: 4 × 7 = 28 gives 28 ÷ 4 = 7 and 28 ÷ 7 = 4. Sharing 28 among four gives seven each; grouping 28 in sevens gives four groups.

For 45 ÷ 5, ask “five times what makes 45?” Nine groups of five rebuild the total.

Watch for this: Do not swap the dividend and divisor and expect the same answer.

Try it: Use 3 × 8 = 24 to write two division facts.

Check the answer and explanation

24 ÷ 3 = 8 and 24 ÷ 8 = 3.

6. Choose a fact in a practical problem

Identify equal groups and the unknown, then retrieve or derive the appropriate fact.

Worked example: 35 seedlings go into rows of five. The group size is known and the number of rows is missing: 35 ÷ 5 = 7 rows. Check 7 × 5 = 35.

For four trays of nine seedlings, find the total: 4 × 9 = 36 seedlings. Explain why this story asks for multiplication.

Watch for this: Include the answer unit and explain which quantity was missing.

Try it: Thirty-six counters are shared among four players. How many does each get?

Check the answer and explanation

9 counters each. Use 4 × 9 = 36 and check the equal shares.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Unequal groups cannot be described by one repeated equal-group fact.
  • The starting zero is not one group.
  • One fewer group means subtract the group size, not subtract one.
  • Build accuracy and flexible recall; speed alone does not show understanding.
  • Do not swap the dividend and divisor and expect the same answer.
  • Include the answer unit and explain which quantity was missing.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Find 4 × 8 using a known fact.
  2. Write a related division fact for 5 × 9 = 45.
  3. Ten packs contain three stickers each. How many stickers?
Answers and next step
  1. 32; double 2 × 8 = 16.
  2. 45 ÷ 5 = 9 or 45 ÷ 9 = 5.
  3. 30 stickers; 10 × 3 = 30.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Build arrays, label their groups and practise a small mixed set accurately. Revisit a model after an uncertain fact.

Extend within this topic: Explain two different ways to derive 4 × 9, then use the result to solve a missing-factor problem.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
