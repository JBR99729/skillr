AC9M3M03: Units of Time and Duration

Recognise and use the relationship between days, hours, minutes and seconds to estimate and compare the duration of events.

Topic guideTeacher deckWorksheetPracticeTest

What students learn in AC9M3M03

This unit helps students build a clear, usable understanding of recognising and using the relationship between formal units of time including days, hours, minutes and seconds to estimate and compare the duration of events. The goal is not to memorise one answer pattern. Students should be able to explain the idea, recognise it in a new example and apply it in a short practice or worksheet task.

  • Start with the main idea: recognise and use relationships between formal units of time to estimate and compare duration.
  • Use concrete examples, pictures, oral explanation and short written responses before independent practice.
  • Estimate how long it takes to read a passage and share the estimate using a sensible formal unit.
  • Plan a sequence of events, such as a class party, by estimating how long each activity will take.
  • Finish with mixed activities so students must choose a strategy rather than copy the last example.

Choosing a sensible unit

Strong time understanding starts with choosing a unit that matches the event. A blink is better described in seconds, eating lunch in minutes, a school day in hours, a camp in days and a holiday in days or weeks.

  • Seconds: blink, clap, short sprint, countdown.
  • Minutes: brushing teeth, reading, recess, a short game.
  • Hours: school day, movie, sports practice, road trip.
  • Days and weeks: camps, holidays, library loans and longer plans.

Ask students, “Could another unit work?” and then, “Which unit communicates the duration most clearly?” This develops reasoning rather than rule-following.

Estimate, measure and compare

Have students estimate first, then use a sand timer, stopwatch or digital timer to measure the event. Useful investigations include reading a short passage, completing 10 star jumps, packing a school bag, tidying a desk or completing a short puzzle.

  1. Choose the most sensible unit.
  2. Make an estimate.
  3. Measure the actual duration.
  4. Compare estimate and measurement.
  5. Explain whether the estimate was reasonable.

Real-life scenarios for Year 3

  • School timetable: Maths runs from 9:10 AM to 10:00 AM, so the duration is 50 minutes.
  • Sport: Training begins at 4:30 PM, with a 15-minute warm-up, 30-minute skills session and 10-minute cool-down, finishing at 5:25 PM.
  • Cooking: Toast is measured in minutes, baking in tens of minutes and slow cooking in hours.
  • Travel: A nearby walk may take minutes, a drive to another city hours and a holiday days or weeks.
  • Reading: Estimate how many minutes it takes to read a set passage, then measure and compare.
  • Class party: Estimate the duration of games and activities and decide whether the plan fits the available time.

Simple conversion strategy

  • Hours → minutes: multiply by 60. Example: 2 hours = 2 × 60 = 120 minutes.
  • Minutes → seconds: multiply by 60. Example: 3 minutes = 180 seconds.
  • Weeks → days: multiply by 7. Example: 3 weeks = 21 days.
  • For simple reverse conversions, divide by the matching relationship. Example: 180 minutes ÷ 60 = 3 hours.

Always attach the unit to the answer and, when comparing two durations, convert them to the same unit first.

Elapsed time: count on

For simple everyday duration problems, a number-line or timeline method is clearer than subtracting clock digits.

  1. Find the start time.
  2. Count forward to a friendly hour.
  3. Count the remaining minutes.
  4. Add the jumps.

Example: 2:15 PM to 4:45 PM = 45 minutes to 3:00 + 1 hour to 4:00 + 45 minutes = 2 hours 30 minutes.

AM, PM and calendar enrichment

Students often meet AM and PM in real-life schedules. AM covers midnight through the morning before noon; PM covers noon through the afternoon and evening before midnight. Remember: 12 noon is 12:00 PM and 12 midnight is 12:00 AM.

For enrichment, connect longer units: 12 months = 1 year; a common year has 365 days and a leap year has 366 days. A decade is 10 years and a century is 100 years. These ideas support broad time vocabulary but should not replace the AC9M3M03 focus on estimating and comparing event duration.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: recognise and use the relationship between formal units of time including days, hours, minutes and seconds to estimate and compare the duration of events

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Thinking 1 hour = 100 minutes instead of 60 minutes.
  • Writing 75 minutes as “1:75” instead of regrouping to 1 hour 15 minutes.
  • Comparing numbers without first converting durations to the same unit.
  • Choosing an awkward unit, such as describing a two-hour trip as 7,200 seconds.
  • Mixing up AM and PM, especially noon and midnight.
  • Blindly subtracting clock digits instead of counting forward on a timeline.
  • Using the correct answer once but not being able to explain why it works.
  • Mixing up similar vocabulary such as before/after and longer/shorter.

Student FAQ

What are the main units of time?

Seconds, minutes, hours, days, weeks, months and years.

How do I convert hours to minutes?

Multiply the number of hours by 60.

How do I convert minutes to seconds?

Multiply the number of minutes by 60.

How do I compare two different time units?

Convert both durations to the same unit first, then compare.

What is elapsed time?

The amount of time that passes between a start time and a finish time.

What is a leap year?

A leap year has 366 days, with 29 days in February. This is useful enrichment when discussing years and calendars.
