Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Duration is how long an event takes. Use seconds, minutes, hours and days to estimate, time, compare and plan events, explaining how the units relate.

Before you start: Sequence events, read whole-number counts and recognise a clock or timer.

Useful words: duration: how long; estimate: an approximate time based on experience; stopwatch: counts elapsed time; countdown: shows time remaining.

1. Connect formal time units

Use 60 seconds = 1 minute, 60 minutes = 1 hour and 24 hours = 1 day. Choose a unit that suits the event.

60 s = 1 min60 min = 1 h24 h = 1 day

Worked examples: a short sprint is conveniently timed in seconds; a lesson in minutes; a long journey in hours; a multi-day camp in days.

Natural cycles also help people describe time. Explore a locally authorised First Nations account involving the sun, moon or stars, with its named source and appropriate guidance. Compare the described cycle with the event duration without inventing a universal cultural calendar.

Watch for this: An hour has 60 minutes, not 100.

Try it: Which units would be useful for a blink, reading a short passage and a three-day camp?

Check the answer and explanation

Seconds; minutes; days. These are useful scales, rather than fixed exact durations for every person.

2. Rename the same duration

Regroup time using its actual unit relationships. State what each number measures.

Worked example: 2 minutes 15 seconds = 120 seconds + 15 seconds = 135 seconds. In reverse, 90 seconds = 60 seconds + 30 seconds = 1 minute 30 seconds.

For hours, 1 hour 20 minutes = 60 + 20 = 80 minutes. A new representation does not change how long the event lasts.

Watch for this: Do not use base-100 money rules for time.

Try it: Rename 150 seconds in minutes and seconds. Rename 2 hours in minutes.

Check the answer and explanation

2 minutes 30 seconds; 120 minutes.

3. Estimate an event before measuring

Use a similar known event as a benchmark. State the activity and conditions; estimates need not be identical for everyone.

Worked example: a learner usually reads a similar short passage in about two minutes. They estimate about two minutes for a new passage, explaining that it is a similar length and difficulty.

A one-minute sand timer can calibrate what one minute feels like. Estimate the duration of drawing, tidying or reading before checking with a timer.

Watch for this: An estimate is not a required exact time for all children.

Try it: Choose a safe short task. Predict its duration, name your unit and give a reason.

Check the answer and explanation

Answers vary. Accept a sensible unit and benchmark-linked estimate, then keep it separate from the measured result.

4. Read timers and check estimates

Read the labels on the display. A stopwatch counts elapsed time; a countdown usually shows time remaining.

Worked example: on a timer labelled minutes:seconds, 1:30 means 1 minute 30 seconds. A countdown set to 3:00 later reads 2:35; 25 seconds have elapsed.

If an estimate was 40 seconds and the measured duration is 52 seconds, the estimate was 12 seconds too short. Set and read a safe one-, three- or five-minute task with a suitable timer.

Watch for this: A display needs its labels; 1:30 must not be treated as 1 minute 3 seconds.

Try it: A minutes:seconds stopwatch reads 0:48 after an estimated 1-minute task. Compare the estimate with the measurement.

Check the answer and explanation

The task took 48 seconds. The 60-second estimate was 12 seconds too long.

5. Compare and order durations

Convert to a common unit if needed, then compare duration, not just the printed number.

Worked example: 75 seconds lasts longer than 1 minute because 1 minute is 60 seconds. The difference is 15 seconds.

Order 45 seconds, 2 minutes and 90 seconds: 45 seconds, 90 seconds, 2 minutes. Write units beside every result and explain the conversion.

Watch for this: Two minutes is longer than 90 seconds even though 2 is the smaller numeral.

Try it: Which is longer, 130 minutes or 2 hours? By how much?

Check the answer and explanation

130 minutes is longer by 10 minutes because 2 hours = 120 minutes.

6. Plan a sequence within a time allowance

Estimate each activity, combine the durations and check the total against the allowance. Include transitions when they matter.

Worked example: a 45-minute party has a 10-minute game, 8-minute relay, 12-minute snack and 15-minute dance. Total 45 minutes: it fits exactly if transitions are included in those estimates.

If the estimates instead total 52 minutes, remove or shorten at least 7 minutes. Then recheck the revised plan.

Watch for this: A plan that fits on paper may need extra transition time in practice.

Try it: A 30-minute plan has activities of 8, 12 and 7 minutes. How much time remains for transitions?

Check the answer and explanation

3 minutes. The activities total 27 minutes, and 30 − 27 = 3.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • An hour has 60 minutes, not 100.
  • Do not use base-100 money rules for time.
  • An estimate is not a required exact time for all children.
  • A display needs its labels; 1:30 must not be treated as 1 minute 3 seconds.
  • Two minutes is longer than 90 seconds even though 2 is the smaller numeral.
  • A plan that fits on paper may need extra transition time in practice.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Convert 2 minutes 20 seconds to seconds.
  2. An estimate is 35 s; the measured time is 42 s. Describe the error.
  3. Do 12-, 9- and 15-minute activities fit into 40 minutes?
Answers and next step
  1. 140 seconds.
  2. The estimate was 7 seconds too short.
  3. Yes, they total 36 minutes, leaving 4 minutes for other needs.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Calibrate one minute with a labelled timer. Compare two short safe tasks before introducing mixed units.

Extend within this topic: Create two different activity plans for 45 minutes. State your estimates and transition allowance, then explain what to change if one activity runs five minutes late.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show reasoning with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation where appropriate. For an actual measuring, creating or data-collection task, the learner still performs and records the stated skill; provide suitable supervision.
