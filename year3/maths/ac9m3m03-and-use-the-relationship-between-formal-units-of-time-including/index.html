Units of Time, Estimation and Duration

Recognise and use days, hours, minutes and seconds to estimate, measure and compare how long events take.

Topic guideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest

What students learn in AC9M3M03

The central idea is duration: how long an event takes. Students use formal units to make sensible estimates, compare events, plan sequences and check estimates with timers. Unit relationships support this thinking; they are not the whole topic.

Big idea: choose a sensible unit → estimate how long → measure when possible → compare → explain whether the estimate was reasonable.
  • Recognise seconds, minutes, hours and days.
  • Choose a sensible unit for a real event.
  • Estimate realistic durations and justify estimates.
  • Compare events as shorter, longer or about the same.
  • Plan activities to fit a time limit.
  • Read and set digital countdown timers.
  • Use sand timers, stopwatches and digital timers to check estimates.
  • Explore cultural accounts of natural cycles involving the sun, moon and stars.

Curriculum coverage: E1–E5

  • E1: estimate how long it takes to read a set passage and communicate the duration.
  • E2: plan a sequence of events from estimated durations, such as a class party.
  • E3: read or set digital devices to the minute or second.
  • E4: use sand and digital timers to check estimates such as 1, 3 and 5 minutes.
  • E5: explore First Nations Australian cultural accounts of cycles of time involving the sun, moon and stars.

Formal units of time

Seconds

Very short: a blink, jump or short sprint.

Minutes

Short: brushing teeth, reading a passage or a game.

Hours

Longer: a movie, sports practice or school day.

Days

Whole-day events: camp, weekend or holiday.

60 seconds = 1 minute • 60 minutes = 1 hour • 24 hours = 1 day • 7 days = 1 week.

Watch for: 1 hour is 60 minutes, not 100.

Estimating realistic durations

An estimate is a sensible approximation based on experience. It does not need to equal the measured result.

  • Sharpen a pencil: about 10 seconds is plausible; 10 minutes is not.
  • Read a short passage: about 1–3 minutes may be reasonable.
  • Walk to the library: a few minutes is more sensible than seconds or hours.
  • Sports practice: about an hour may be reasonable.

Ask: What unit fits? What similar event do I know? Is my number too small or too large? Why?

Estimate → measure → compare

  1. Choose an event and sensible unit.
  2. Estimate before starting.
  3. Measure with a stopwatch, digital timer or sand timer.
  4. Compare estimate and measurement.
  5. Explain what you learned for your next estimate.

Example: estimate 40 seconds; timer says 52 seconds. The estimate was 12 seconds too short.

Comparing durations

  • Reading a chapter normally takes longer than reading one page.
  • A 7-minute walk takes 4 minutes longer than a 3-minute walk.
  • 45 seconds is shorter than 1 minute.
  • 2 minutes and 120 seconds are equal.

Convert to the same unit when that makes a numerical comparison clearer.

Planning events

45-minute party: game 10 min + relay 8 min + snack 12 min + dance 15 min = 45 min. It fits exactly.

If a plan totals 52 minutes but only 45 are available, students need to shorten or remove at least 7 minutes. Encourage students to create and revise their own schedules.

Digital timers

  • 0:45 = 45 seconds.
  • 1:30 = 1 minute 30 seconds.
  • 3:00 = 3 minutes.
  • From 3:00 to 2:35, 25 seconds have passed.

Students should practise setting a timer, predicting what it will show and interpreting a countdown.

Timer investigations

Use safe tasks such as clapping, drawing, reading or tidying. Students estimate 1, 3 or 5 minutes, measure the duration and record the difference. A stopwatch or digital timer is useful for precise seconds; a sand timer makes a fixed interval visible.

Natural and cultural cycles of time

E5 asks students to explore First Nations Australian cultural accounts involving the sun, moon and stars. Use appropriate local community or authoritative First Nations resources for specific cultural knowledge. Students can also notice repeating natural patterns such as day/night, moon phases and seasonal sky changes.

Real-life scenarios

  • Reading: estimate and time a set passage.
  • Class party: choose activities that fit 30 or 45 minutes.
  • Sport: estimate a race in seconds and practice in minutes or hours.
  • Cooking: choose minutes or hours depending on the task.
  • Travel: compare a short walk, drive and multi-day trip.
  • School: compare recess, a lesson and the whole day.

Useful connected skills retained

The existing guide’s good conversion, elapsed-time and AM/PM ideas remain useful when solving real situations.

  • Hours → minutes: ×60; 2 h = 120 min.
  • Minutes → seconds: ×60; 3 min = 180 s.
  • Elapsed time is the duration between a start and finish.
  • AM/PM helps place events in the day.
  • Detailed analogue clock reading continues in AC9M3M04.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the code mainly as unit conversion.
  • Choosing unrealistic units or estimates.
  • Thinking an estimate must be exact.
  • Using 100 minutes per hour.
  • Reading 1:30 on a countdown as 1 minute 3 seconds.
  • Planning without checking the total time.
  • Giving a number without explaining whether it is reasonable.
