10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Survey and report a household preference

Ask everyone at home to choose one of three fruits. Record each answer once, make a column graph and write a sentence that answers the question using the counts.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two choices. Extend by explaining what the survey can tell you about your household.

FAQ

Can your household survey tell you the favourite fruit of every family?

No. It describes only the people who answered your survey.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Ask a focused question, plan and collect suitable data, choose a display and use the evidence to answer the original question. Explain the method and its limits.

Before you start: Collect and record categories or counts; read frequency tables and simple graphs.

Useful words: investigation: a connected question-to-conclusion process; variable: what is recorded; evidence: the data supporting a claim; conclusion: an answer tied to the question.

1. Focus the question and variable

Name the group, purpose and what you will record. A useful statistical question allows a set of responses that may vary.

Worked example: replace “Which game does everyone like?” with “Which of these three break-time activities does each learner in our class prefer today?” The group and occasion are bounded, and the variable is a category.

For a count investigation, ask how many books each class member borrowed this week. State one record per learner and retain valid zero counts.

Watch for this: Do not claim a class survey answers for every child.

Try it: Improve “What is the favourite breakfast cereal?” for an investigation in one class.

Check the answer and explanation

For example, “Which breakfast cereal do learners in our class report preferring today?” Define the response options or a clear open-answer recording rule.

2. Plan a trustworthy collection

Decide who or what to include, how to collect data and how to keep the rule consistent.

Worked example: for a class activity choice, give each learner one response, include everyone available, use the same options and note absences. Do not ask the same learner twice.

For a parent open-day question, obtain responses through the agreed adult-supported school process. A survey of children’s favourite weekdays is not evidence of when their parents can attend.

Watch for this: Choose a group and collection method that actually match the question.

Try it: Why would surveying only the children already playing one particular game weaken a class preference investigation?

Check the answer and explanation

That group may differ from the rest of the class. Use the intended class group and record who did or did not respond.

3. Collect and preserve a checkable record

Carry out the plan with appropriate guidance. Keep raw responses, then organise them without losing cases.

Worked example: fourteen recorded activity choices give games 6, drawing 5 and reading 3. Check the frequency total: 6 + 5 + 3 = 14 responses. Check each category against its actual raw record too.

Count-data example: to investigate “How many library books did each of six learners borrow this week?”, the supplied raw counts are 0, 1, 1, 2, 0, 2. Record one count per learner. Zero means no books borrowed and must be retained.

For a class or household investigation, collect a small safe set with permission and keep the learner’s own recording. A supplied example teaches the method but does not substitute for the learner collecting data.

Watch for this: A plausible-looking total is not enough; records need to be traceable.

Try it: An agreed single-choice survey has 12 respondents but a table totals 13. What should you do?

Check the answer and explanation

Reconcile the raw record and table: look for a duplicate, miscount or recording error. Do not remove a result arbitrarily.

4. Choose and make a relevant display

Represent the recorded dataset faithfully. Choose a title, labels, key or scale that serves the investigation question.

Worked example data
ActivityNumber of learners
Games6
Drawing5
Reading3

Worked example: for the fourteen activity choices, a frequency table or column graph shows games 6, drawing 5 and reading 3. A simple unit scale keeps the difference between 6 and 5 visible.

For the six library-book counts 0, 1, 1, 2, 0, 2, make a count–frequency table: 0 books→2 learners; 1 book→2 learners; 2 books→2 learners. A graph can use the ordered values 0, 1, 2 along the category axis and bar heights of 2 learners each. Keep the two meanings of the numbers distinct.

For count data, retain the numerical value order and valid zero values. If software is available, create the graph and check it against the raw record rather than trusting its default labels.

Watch for this: A correct grand total cannot justify changing the dataset.

Try it: Could the activity graph use values 6, 6 and 2 because the total still equals 14?

Check the answer and explanation

No. It changes the category frequencies. A faithful representation must preserve each recorded value.

5. Answer with evidence and a boundary

Return to the original question. Support a conclusion with values and specify the observed group.

Worked example: “Games received the most responses in our fourteen-response survey: 6, compared with 5 for drawing and 3 for reading.” This supports choosing games as the largest single group.

Six is less than half of fourteen, so do not call it a majority. The responses also do not prove games are best for every learner.

For the six library-book respondents, two borrowed no books, two borrowed one and two borrowed two. No recorded count was more common than the others. The frequencies total six learners; the books total 0 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 0 + 2 = 6 books. Equal totals here are a coincidence, not a rule. Limit the conclusion to those six learners and that week.

Watch for this: Separate what the data shows from a cause, prediction or value judgement.

Try it: Using the supplied activity results, how many more chose games than reading? State the evidence limit.

Check the answer and explanation

3 more: 6 − 3. This describes the stated survey, not all children or all future choices.

6. Communicate and review the complete investigation

Link the question, method, data, display and conclusion in a short report or flowchart. Review a genuine limitation and useful next step.

Question and planCollect and representInterpret and review

Worked example: report the class activity question, one response per learner, fourteen responses, the labelled table and the 6–5–3 conclusion. If two learners were absent, note that boundary and decide whether to seek their views before a class decision.

A locally guided environmental investigation may use an authorised First Nations seasonal resource. Agree an observable local indicator and recording period, collect actual observations and make a one-to-one display; do not invent cultural indicators or generalise a local calendar.

Watch for this: A finished graph alone is not a complete investigation.

Try it: Plan and carry out a small guided investigation. What should the final report include?

Check the answer and explanation

The focused question, intended group, consistent method, actual raw records, an accurate display, evidence-based answer, limitation and next step. Accept oral, written or visual reporting that preserves these links.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Do not claim a class survey answers for every child.
  • Choose a group and collection method that actually match the question.
  • A plausible-looking total is not enough; records need to be traceable.
  • A correct grand total cannot justify changing the dataset.
  • Separate what the data shows from a cause, prediction or value judgement.
  • A finished graph alone is not a complete investigation.
Check understanding and choose the next step
  1. Why does an investigation need a focused group?
  2. What check links a table to collected data?
  3. What belongs beside a conclusion besides the largest category name?
Answers and next step
  1. So the collection and claim refer to a defined group rather than everyone.
  2. Reconcile each raw response with its category and frequency, then check the total.
  3. Numerical evidence, the question answered and the group or occasion to which the evidence applies.

If an answer is uncertain, reopen the matching focus, use its model and explain a fresh example before the test.

Support: Use one class or household question, a short list of clear options and a prepared recording frame. The learner still records and explains the evidence.

Extend within this topic: Compare two sensible collection plans for the same question. Explain their limitations and carry out the chosen guided plan, keeping the whole evidence trail.
