CODE: AC9S1U01
DESCRIPTOR: Identify the basic needs of plants and animals, including air, water, food or shelter, and describe how the places they live meet those needs.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Plant needs and related Year 1 Australian Curriculum science mapping.
- The reviewed repository bank contains 24 Practice and 16 separate Test questions.
- Evidence spans food, water, air, light, shelter, room/space, habitat suitability, shared versus different needs, and habitats meeting several needs at once.
- Questions progress from recognising one need to evaluating a habitat card, identifying a missing resource, and explaining why one resource cannot replace another.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Compare needs of plants, animals and humans without teaching that every organism needs the same food or shelter.
- Connect habitat features to needs: pond water, food organisms, tree hollows, cover, suitable light and growing room.
- Include care for pets, plants and local living things.
- Treat a habitat as a system providing several resources and conditions, not simply "an animal's home."
- Use local, community-approved knowledge when addressing First Nations Caring for Country; identify the specific Nation/community where known and avoid pan-Indigenous generalisations.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Habitat photographs/diagrams, need–feature matching, before/after habitat changes, plant setup cards and simple dioramas.
- Examples across gardens, ponds, bush areas, homes and managed animal environments.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Plants get food from soil or "eat" sunlight.
- Water replaces every other need.
- All animals eat the same food.
- Shelter is only a human-made house.
- A place meeting one need is automatically a suitable habitat.
- More of one resource compensates for a missing different resource.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Retain need → habitat feature → evidence reasoning.
- Include multi-need decisions, comparisons between organisms, habitat-change effects and care choices.
- Avoid decorative animal trivia unrelated to needs.
- Topic page must explicitly teach shared needs, different ways of meeting them, and why all required conditions matter.

CODE: AC9S1U02
DESCRIPTOR: Describe daily and seasonal changes in the environment and explore how these changes affect everyday life.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Weather patterns.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence includes day/night, weather changing within/between days, wind/rain/temperature observations, tree and flower changes across seasons, daylight/shade patterns and practical responses.
- The bank deliberately distinguishes a usual seasonal pattern from a guarantee about every day.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Daily changes: light/dark, weather, temperature, shade position and short-term effects on places and activities.
- Seasonal changes: longer-term patterns in weather, daylight, plants and animal behaviour.
- Effects on daily life: clothing, shelter, activity timing, safe routes, garden care and resource availability.
- Observation records must preserve time order and distinguish direct observation from inference.
- Australian seasonal patterns vary by location. First Nations seasonal calendars must be locally grounded, attributed and taught through approved sources.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired morning/afternoon scenes, short weather records, dated plant photographs, shade/daylight cards, local seasonal calendars and activity-choice scenarios.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- One cold day means the season changed.
- Every day in a season has identical weather.
- Weather and season are synonyms.
- All Australian places have the same four-season pattern.
- Dark clouds prove rain occurred.
- A pattern guarantees the next observation exactly.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Balance daily and seasonal examples.
- Ask what changed, what evidence supports it, the time scale involved and how people/plants/animals may respond.
- Do not drift into formal astronomy or climate statistics.
- Teach "common pattern" versus "always."

CODE: AC9S1U03
DESCRIPTOR: Describe pushes and pulls in terms of strength and direction and predict the effect of these forces on objects' motion and shape.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Investigate pushes and pulls.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence covers push versus pull, direction arrows, start/stop/speed/turn effects, stronger versus gentler forces, barriers, and shape changes in soft materials.
- Existing visual cards make arrow direction and travel outcomes necessary evidence rather than decoration.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Contact pushes and pulls can start or stop motion, change speed or direction, or change shape.
- Strength and direction must be described relative to a clearly identified object and starting state.
- Predictions are conditional: the same object and setup should be compared when reasoning about stronger/gentler force.
- Everyday contexts include doors, drawers, trolleys, balls, playdough, playground equipment, toys and suitable sporting equipment.
- First Nations instructive toys/games may be used only with accurate, culturally appropriate source context.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Before/after motion cards, force arrows, path diagrams, equal-setup comparisons and soft-material shape changes.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A force must keep acting for an object ever to move.
- A push always makes something faster.
- Stronger always means farther regardless of surface/barrier/setup.
- Direction belongs to the object rather than the applied force.
- Shape change means the material became a new substance.
- A visible arrow is the object's path when it actually represents the force.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach force action → strength/direction → predicted motion/shape effect.
- Include fair comparisons and cases where a barrier or opposing force changes the outcome.
- Avoid formal force units, calculations and unnecessary friction terminology.

CODE: AC9S1H01
DESCRIPTOR: Describe how people use science in their daily lives, including using patterns to make scientific predictions.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL comparison evidence: Design a race car track; the curriculum mapping also links plant needs.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Existing questions connect observations and repeated results to practical choices, then require relevant evidence, changed-condition awareness and supported predictions.
- IXL is a related task-design reference rather than complete descriptor coverage.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- People use observations, tests and patterns when caring for living things, responding to weather, designing toys/equipment, cooking, building and solving everyday problems.
- A scientific prediction uses an observed or experienced pattern and remains testable; it is not a guess based only on preference.
- Science can inform a choice without guaranteeing an outcome.
- The code must show use, not merely ask students to name a scientist or laboratory object.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Short observation records, repeated-result tables/picture sequences, everyday problem/solution cards and "evidence → prediction → action" chains.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Science happens only in laboratories.
- Any future statement is a scientific prediction.
- One observation always establishes a reliable pattern.
- A prediction must be correct to be scientific.
- A practical design succeeds because it looks attractive.
- Changed conditions can be ignored.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Use authentic Year 1 contexts and ask which evidence supports a practical prediction or solution.
- Include plant/animal care, weather planning and pushes/pulls without duplicating the understanding-code questions.
- Topic teaching should model observation → pattern → prediction → everyday decision.

CODE: AC9S1I01
DESCRIPTOR: Pose questions and make predictions to explore observed simple patterns or relationships.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL references: Investigate pushes and pulls and Investigate sunlight and shade.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence progresses from identifying a prediction to matching a question to an observed relationship, selecting relevant prior experience and interpreting repeated comparisons.
- Evaluation of completed investigations is kept mainly for AC9S1I05.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Begin with an observation, identify what could be compared, pose an answerable question and make a reasoned prediction.
- Questions should focus on observable outcomes, not opinions such as "nicest."
- Predictions may use personal experience or a simple observed pattern and should state a reason.
- A prediction is made before checking and need not be correct.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired observations, small repeated-result sequences, question/prediction cards and simple comparative setups involving habitats, weather, shade or motion.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A question and prediction are the same thing.
- A preference question is automatically investigable.
- A prediction reports what already happened.
- Cautious words such as "may" make any prediction evidence-based.
- The prediction is wrong if the investigation has not happened yet.
- An unrelated experience supports the prediction.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach the sequence: notice → ask → predict with a reason → investigate later.
- Include questions with measurable/observable outcomes and distractors that are plausible but investigate a different variable.
- Avoid drifting into full procedural design, formal variables or judging fairness.
