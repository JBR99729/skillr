CODE: AC9S1U01
DESCRIPTOR: Identify the basic needs of plants and animals, including air, water, food or shelter, and describe how the places they live meet those needs.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Plant needs and related Year 1 Australian Curriculum science mapping.
- The reviewed repository bank contains 24 Practice and 16 separate Test questions.
- Evidence spans food, water, air, light, shelter, room/space, habitat suitability, shared versus different needs, and habitats meeting several needs at once.
- Questions progress from recognising one need to evaluating a habitat card, identifying a missing resource, and explaining why one resource cannot replace another.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Compare needs of plants, animals and humans without teaching that every organism needs the same food or shelter.
- Connect habitat features to needs: pond water, food organisms, tree hollows, cover, suitable light and growing room.
- Include care for pets, plants and local living things.
- Treat a habitat as a system providing several resources and conditions, not simply "an animal's home."
- Use local, community-approved knowledge when addressing First Nations Caring for Country; identify the specific Nation/community where known and avoid pan-Indigenous generalisations.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Habitat photographs/diagrams, need–feature matching, before/after habitat changes, plant setup cards and simple dioramas.
- Examples across gardens, ponds, bush areas, homes and managed animal environments.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Plants get food from soil or "eat" sunlight.
- Water replaces every other need.
- All animals eat the same food.
- Shelter is only a human-made house.
- A place meeting one need is automatically a suitable habitat.
- More of one resource compensates for a missing different resource.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Retain need → habitat feature → evidence reasoning.
- Include multi-need decisions, comparisons between organisms, habitat-change effects and care choices.
- Avoid decorative animal trivia unrelated to needs.
- Topic page must explicitly teach shared needs, different ways of meeting them, and why all required conditions matter.

CODE: AC9S1U02
DESCRIPTOR: Describe daily and seasonal changes in the environment and explore how these changes affect everyday life.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Weather patterns.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence includes day/night, weather changing within/between days, wind/rain/temperature observations, tree and flower changes across seasons, daylight/shade patterns and practical responses.
- The bank deliberately distinguishes a usual seasonal pattern from a guarantee about every day.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Daily changes: light/dark, weather, temperature, shade position and short-term effects on places and activities.
- Seasonal changes: longer-term patterns in weather, daylight, plants and animal behaviour.
- Effects on daily life: clothing, shelter, activity timing, safe routes, garden care and resource availability.
- Observation records must preserve time order and distinguish direct observation from inference.
- Australian seasonal patterns vary by location. First Nations seasonal calendars must be locally grounded, attributed and taught through approved sources.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired morning/afternoon scenes, short weather records, dated plant photographs, shade/daylight cards, local seasonal calendars and activity-choice scenarios.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- One cold day means the season changed.
- Every day in a season has identical weather.
- Weather and season are synonyms.
- All Australian places have the same four-season pattern.
- Dark clouds prove rain occurred.
- A pattern guarantees the next observation exactly.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Balance daily and seasonal examples.
- Ask what changed, what evidence supports it, the time scale involved and how people/plants/animals may respond.
- Do not drift into formal astronomy or climate statistics.
- Teach "common pattern" versus "always."

CODE: AC9S1U03
DESCRIPTOR: Describe pushes and pulls in terms of strength and direction and predict the effect of these forces on objects' motion and shape.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Mapped IXL skill: Investigate pushes and pulls.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence covers push versus pull, direction arrows, start/stop/speed/turn effects, stronger versus gentler forces, barriers, and shape changes in soft materials.
- Existing visual cards make arrow direction and travel outcomes necessary evidence rather than decoration.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Contact pushes and pulls can start or stop motion, change speed or direction, or change shape.
- Strength and direction must be described relative to a clearly identified object and starting state.
- Predictions are conditional: the same object and setup should be compared when reasoning about stronger/gentler force.
- Everyday contexts include doors, drawers, trolleys, balls, playdough, playground equipment, toys and suitable sporting equipment.
- First Nations instructive toys/games may be used only with accurate, culturally appropriate source context.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Before/after motion cards, force arrows, path diagrams, equal-setup comparisons and soft-material shape changes.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A force must keep acting for an object ever to move.
- A push always makes something faster.
- Stronger always means farther regardless of surface/barrier/setup.
- Direction belongs to the object rather than the applied force.
- Shape change means the material became a new substance.
- A visible arrow is the object's path when it actually represents the force.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach force action → strength/direction → predicted motion/shape effect.
- Include fair comparisons and cases where a barrier or opposing force changes the outcome.
- Avoid formal force units, calculations and unnecessary friction terminology.

CODE: AC9S1H01
DESCRIPTOR: Describe how people use science in their daily lives, including using patterns to make scientific predictions.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL comparison evidence: Design a race car track; the curriculum mapping also links plant needs.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Existing questions connect observations and repeated results to practical choices, then require relevant evidence, changed-condition awareness and supported predictions.
- IXL is a related task-design reference rather than complete descriptor coverage.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- People use observations, tests and patterns when caring for living things, responding to weather, designing toys/equipment, cooking, building and solving everyday problems.
- A scientific prediction uses an observed or experienced pattern and remains testable; it is not a guess based only on preference.
- Science can inform a choice without guaranteeing an outcome.
- The code must show use, not merely ask students to name a scientist or laboratory object.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Short observation records, repeated-result tables/picture sequences, everyday problem/solution cards and "evidence → prediction → action" chains.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Science happens only in laboratories.
- Any future statement is a scientific prediction.
- One observation always establishes a reliable pattern.
- A prediction must be correct to be scientific.
- A practical design succeeds because it looks attractive.
- Changed conditions can be ignored.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Use authentic Year 1 contexts and ask which evidence supports a practical prediction or solution.
- Include plant/animal care, weather planning and pushes/pulls without duplicating the understanding-code questions.
- Topic teaching should model observation → pattern → prediction → everyday decision.

CODE: AC9S1I01
DESCRIPTOR: Pose questions and make predictions to explore observed simple patterns or relationships.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL references: Investigate pushes and pulls and Investigate sunlight and shade.
- Reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test.
- Evidence progresses from identifying a prediction to matching a question to an observed relationship, selecting relevant prior experience and interpreting repeated comparisons.
- Evaluation of completed investigations is kept mainly for AC9S1I05.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Begin with an observation, identify what could be compared, pose an answerable question and make a reasoned prediction.
- Questions should focus on observable outcomes, not opinions such as "nicest."
- Predictions may use personal experience or a simple observed pattern and should state a reason.
- A prediction is made before checking and need not be correct.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired observations, small repeated-result sequences, question/prediction cards and simple comparative setups involving habitats, weather, shade or motion.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A question and prediction are the same thing.
- A preference question is automatically investigable.
- A prediction reports what already happened.
- Cautious words such as "may" make any prediction evidence-based.
- The prediction is wrong if the investigation has not happened yet.
- An unrelated experience supports the prediction.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach the sequence: notice → ask → predict with a reason → investigate later.
- Include questions with measurable/observable outcomes and distractors that are plausible but investigate a different variable.
- Avoid drifting into full procedural design, formal variables or judging fairness.

CODE: AC9S1I02
DESCRIPTOR: Suggest and follow safe procedures to investigate questions and test predictions.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- No direct IXL skill is listed for this code in the Year 1 Australian Curriculum mapping.
- IXL's race-track design sequencing provides related procedural evidence only; no direct equivalence is claimed.
- Existing reviewed bank contains 24 Practice + 16 separate Test questions and was runtime-validated across complete bank rotation.
- Questions cover prepare → act → observe → finish, missing-step selection, procedure repair and safe responses to changed setups.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Students suggest and follow short, coherent procedures linked to a question or prediction.
- Safety is contextual: clear workspace, suitable materials, gentle actions, hand hygiene, adult help where needed, stop/report hazards and protect living things.
- A procedure should make the intended observation possible and preserve the relevant comparison.
- Year 1 students use guided investigations; formal risk matrices and variable terminology are out of scope.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Ordered picture steps, partially completed procedures, safe/unsafe setup comparisons and simple investigation plans involving plants, weather, shade, objects or pushes/pulls.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A generic safety rule makes any procedure scientifically useful.
- The steps can be followed in any order.
- Observing before setting up records the intended change.
- More force/material/time always improves a test.
- Safety equipment replaces careful behaviour.
- An unsafe step is acceptable if it produces a result quickly.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Assess both safety and fitness-for-purpose.
- Include choosing a missing step, sequencing, repairing a flawed plan, and adapting safely when conditions change.
- Practical/Classroom View tasks must allow students to suggest and carry out procedures; quizzes can only recognise/select them.

CODE: AC9S1I03
DESCRIPTOR: Make and record observations, including informal measurements, using digital tools as appropriate.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Direct IXL reference: What objects can a magnet pull? Learners observe an animation, select the observed action and identify the responding part.
- Existing reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test questions with observation cards and informal-length diagrams.
- Coverage includes observable features, counts, dates, order, equal informal units, useful photographs/labels and distinguishing observation from added explanation.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Record what was actually sensed or measured, with enough detail to be checked later.
- Informal measurements require a named, consistent unit and count.
- Records may use drawings, photographs, symbols, words, numbers, tables or appropriate digital tools.
- Separate observation ("the car stopped at 6 blocks") from inference ("the rough surface slowed it").

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Before/after images, labelled drawings, dated photo sequences, equal-unit length models, simple tables and competing record choices.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A guess or explanation is an observation.
- Decorative detail makes a record more useful.
- Units can be omitted or changed.
- A photograph needs no date, label or context.
- Counts can be estimated when the task requires recording observed items.
- Digital automatically means more accurate.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Include choosing, completing and correcting records.
- Require time/place/feature/unit labels where they affect meaning.
- Practical and worksheet evidence must include students independently making at least one observation record.

CODE: AC9S1I04
DESCRIPTOR: Sort and order data and information and represent patterns, including with provided tables and visual or physical models.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- Direct IXL reference: Identify plants and animals, sorting pictured and labelled living things into named groups.
- Weather-pattern evidence supports ordering observations over time.
- Existing reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test questions with one-to-one displays and sorting models.
- Content removes unnecessary arithmetic and multi-unit graph keys.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Sort by one stated observable feature, preserve time/order where relevant and represent a simple pattern faithfully.
- Students use provided templates, tables, visual models or physical models with guidance.
- The representation must preserve every observation once and make the relevant pattern visible.
- Scientific sorting uses evidence relevant to the question, not preference.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Sorting hoops/bins, chronological cards, one-to-one picture displays, simple tables, physical models and repeated observation sequences.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Changing the sorting rule midway.
- Placing one item twice or omitting it.
- Ordering by size when the required order is time.
- Treating a decorative arrangement as a scientific pattern.
- Assuming categories can never overlap when the rule allows it.
- Altering data to make the pattern neater.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach collect/observe → choose or follow rule → sort/order → represent → describe pattern.
- Include rule inference, correction of misplaced items and checking representation against source data.
- Avoid turning this into a standalone Year 1 mathematics graphing unit.

CODE: AC9S1I05
DESCRIPTOR: Compare observations with predictions and others' observations, consider if investigations are fair and identify further questions with guidance.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL mapping links design and weather investigations; the race-track task provides related goal/procedure evidence.
- Fairness and further-question coverage is anchored to the official descriptor, not falsely attributed to an unseen IXL stage.
- Existing reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test questions.
- Coverage includes prediction/result comparison, comparing observers, recognising confounded comparisons in everyday language, retaining mixed evidence and selecting a related next question.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- A mismatch between prediction and observation is useful evidence, not failure.
- Compare reports by checking whether observers examined the same event, time, object and feature.
- At this level, fairness means keeping relevant conditions the same and changing the intended factor; formal control-variable language is unnecessary.
- A further question should arise from the actual result or uncertainty.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Prediction/result pairs, two-observer records, paired investigation setups, mixed-result tables and next-question choices.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Change the observation to match the prediction.
- A correct prediction proves the procedure was fair.
- Different observers must have one "wrong" answer even when conditions/times differ.
- Changing several things gives stronger evidence.
- Repeat until the preferred result appears.
- Any science question is a useful follow-up.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Include supported, unsupported and partly supported predictions.
- Use age-appropriate fair-comparison language.
- Require learners to identify what changed, what stayed the same and which further question follows.
- Practical assessment should include discussion of an actual class investigation.

CODE: AC9S1I06
DESCRIPTOR: Write and create texts to communicate observations, findings and ideas using everyday and scientific vocabulary.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

EXISTING IXL / REPOSITORY EVIDENCE
- IXL mapping links magnet and sunlight/shade investigations.
- Revisited magnet example asks learners to connect an observed movement with accurate force language.
- Existing reviewed bank: 24 Practice + 16 separate Test, six evidence SVGs and full runtime validation.
- Coverage includes captions, labels, texture/force/absorption vocabulary, time order, measurement units, explicit comparisons, record correction, observation versus possible explanation and evidence-supported design suggestions.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Communication may be written, drawn, labelled, diagrammatic or multimodal.
- Everyday vocabulary supports accessibility; scientific vocabulary adds precision when it is understood.
- A useful text identifies what was observed/found and preserves relevant evidence, sequence and units.
- Claims should not go beyond the evidence.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Labelled drawings, captions, simple reports, flow charts, before/after records, tables with accompanying sentences and audience-appropriate multimodal texts.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Scientific words make an unsupported claim correct.
- A title alone communicates the finding.
- Labels can contradict the diagram.
- Predictions and results can be swapped.
- Units, order or comparison details are optional.
- Longer writing is automatically better science communication.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Progress from selecting an accurate caption to improving precision and constructing an independent short scientific text.
- Include matching text to evidence, correcting overclaims and choosing vocabulary suited to the observation and audience.
- Critical boundary: selected responses assess recognition/improvement only. Topic, worksheet and Classroom View must require the child to independently write, draw, label or orally communicate.

PURPOSE
Record agreed requirements for a later Skillr practice-framework task while Year 1 Science research continues. These notes are proposed product requirements, not evidence that the current runtime already supports them.

MATCHING PUZZLES
- Add a reusable, accessible matching-puzzle interaction for Year 1 Science Practice.
- Prefer simple tap-to-select and tap-to-match controls suitable for young learners; do not require drag-and-drop as the only input method.
- Use matching where it measures a genuine curriculum relationship, such as organism → need, habitat feature → need, observation → category, procedure step → position, or scientific word → evidence.
- Auto-mark the completed set and clearly show which pairs need another attempt.
- Keep language short, visual load controlled and every relationship unambiguous.
- Use primarily in Practice; do not allow interaction novelty to distort Test comparability or first-attempt benchmarking.

COLLECT DATA INTERACTION
- Add a lightweight, reusable Practice interaction in which students record provided observations, sort them into named categories, order them when relevant, and/or build a one-to-one display.
- Pilot curriculum fit: AC9S1I03 (make and record observations) and AC9S1I04 (sort, order and represent data).
- Students should work with on-screen observations or teacher-provided/classroom observations. No photo, audio or file uploads are required.
- Suitable actions include tapping an observation card, choosing a category, placing one observation once, preserving chronological order and checking a display against the source observations.
- Auto-mark objective parts. Where independent observation or communication cannot be validly measured on-screen, direct the evidence to the worksheet/Classroom View or a parent/teacher completion tick.
- Avoid unnecessary arithmetic, multi-unit graph keys, long typing and high-storage data collection.

MASTERY, RETAKES AND REPORTING
- Practice may be repeated without limit.
- Incorrect Practice questions return only after the learner completes the full current round.
- Practice is complete and receives its tick only when every question in that strand's Practice bank has been answered correctly: 100% mastery.
- A learner may retake and eventually achieve 100%.
- For grades and benchmarking, record only the learner's first Test-attempt result.
- Later Test retakes are learning opportunities and must not overwrite, replace or improve the recorded first-attempt grade/benchmark.
- The interface must distinguish clearly between Practice mastery, the recorded first Test result and later retake performance.

RESEARCH QUESTIONS BEFORE IMPLEMENTATION
- Compare how IXL, Khan Academy and relevant free primary-science resources structure matching, observation recording, sorting and one-to-one data displays for early learners.
- Record which interactions are directly observed, which are inferred from public descriptions and which have no source match.
- Check accessibility for keyboard, touch and screen readers; include a non-drag alternative.
- Check whether feedback is immediate or round-based, how incomplete matches are handled, and whether retakes alter reported progress.
- Map each proposed interaction to the exact Year 1 Science descriptor component before authoring.
- Confirm that the existing Skillr runtime and reporting model can support the requirements without changing current content during the research phase.

IMPLEMENTATION HOLD
- Do not modify the quiz runtime, question banks, grading, benchmarking or completion-tick behaviour during this research phase.
- Begin implementation only after the research findings are reviewed and the owner explicitly approves the build task.
