Explore linear graphs with digital tools (AC9M8A04)

Vary one parameter at a time, test predictions and describe changes in linear graphs.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Worked example

using digital tools to investigate integer solutions to equations such as 2x+3y\;=\;48

Learn the skill

1. Identify the intercept

The vertical intercept is the value of y when x is zero.

Try it · Where does y = 2x − 4 cross the vertical axis?

(0, −4).

2. Test the slope

The coefficient of x describes the change in y for each unit increase in x.

Try it · How does y change per unit of x for y = −2x + 1?

It decreases by 2.

3. Generalise what stays fixed

Compare several graphs before stating a general rule.

Try it · What stays the same when slope varies but the constant term is 2?

All the lines cross the vertical axis at (0, 2).

Common mistakes

Changing both parameters and attributing both effects to one. Hold one parameter fixed while testing the other.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Experiment with slope and intercept

Graph y = x + 2, y = 3x + 2 and y = x − 1. Compare the crossing points on the vertical axis and how quickly each line rises.

Adjust the challenge: Change one number at a time. Extend by testing a negative slope.

FAQ

Which line rises fastest as x increases?

y = 3x + 2. Its y-value increases by 3 for every 1 increase in x.

Learning goals
  • Vary parameters systematically in linear functions and relations.
  • Predict and test how gradient, intercept and inequality sign affect representations.
  • Use tables and graphs as evidence for or against a conjecture.
  • Generalise patterns only after testing enough relevant cases.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall straight-line graphs, coordinates, gradient as rate of change, intercepts, tables of values, equations and simple inequality regions.

Key concept

A useful digital experiment controls variables. In y=mx+b, hold b fixed while changing m to isolate the effect of gradient; hold m fixed while changing b to isolate the intercept effect.

The conjecture must be stated before or during testing, then challenged with positive, zero and negative cases where relevant. One attractive graph is not proof of a general rule.

Digital tools accelerate variation and comparison, but reasoning remains mathematical: identify what changed, what stayed invariant and whether the evidence supports the claimed generalisation.

Victoria numbering note: this national descriptor AC9M8A04 aligns to Victorian VC2M8A05. Victorian VC2M8A04 is a separate algorithms/testing-and-error-correction descriptor, so matching by number alone would be incorrect.
Worked examples

Hold gradient fixed

y=3x−4, y=3x and y=3x+5 all have gradient 3, so their graphs are parallel. Only the y-intercept changes.

Hold intercept fixed

Compare y=0.5x+2, y=2x+2 and y=−2x+2. They share intercept 2; changing m changes steepness and direction.

Test inequality regions

Use y=2x as the boundary. Testing (0,1) shows it satisfies y>2x; the opposite half-plane satisfies y<2x.

Find integer solutions

For 2x+3y=12, integer points include (0,4), (3,2) and (6,0). A table and graph help detect the pattern.

Common misconceptions
  • One graph proves a general rule. Test multiple strategically different cases.
  • Changing b changes gradient. In y=mx+b, b shifts the line vertically but does not change m.
  • Digital tools replace explanation. They provide evidence; the conjecture and generalisation require reasoning.
  • A visual intersection is automatically exact. Readings may be approximate; verify algebraically when exactness matters.
Guided practice
  1. Graph y=2x+b for b=−3,0,4 and state what remains invariant.
  2. Graph y=mx+1 for m=−2,0,3 and describe the effect of m.
  3. Test which side of y=x+2 satisfies y>x+2.
  4. List three integer solutions to x+2y=8.
Independent practice
  1. Predict the effect of increasing b in y=4x+b, then test it.
  2. Predict the effect of changing m from 2 to −2 with b fixed.
  3. Decide whether y=5x−1 and y=5x+8 are parallel and justify.
  4. Compare y=−x and y>−x on a graph.
  5. Find four integer solutions to 2x+y=10.
  6. Design a digital experiment to test “all lines with the same gradient are parallel”.
  7. State what evidence would disprove your conjecture.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A student compares y=2x+1 with y=5x+7 and concludes “increasing m makes a line steeper.” Explain why the experiment is confounded, redesign it so only one parameter changes, and state the evidence needed for a reliable generalisation.

Questions and answers
  1. Why vary one parameter at a time? To isolate its effect rather than mixing causes.
  2. What happens when b changes with m fixed? The line shifts vertically and remains parallel.
  3. What does negative m do? The line decreases from left to right.
  4. What makes a conjecture convincing? It survives systematic tests, including relevant boundary or contrasting cases.
Practice and review
  1. Investigate y=mx+3 for m=−3,−1,0,2,4 and generalise the effect of m.
    Keep b fixed and compare direction and steepness.
  2. Test the claim “lines y=4x+b are parallel for all b” with digital evidence and mathematical reasoning.
    Use several b-values and identify the invariant gradient.
  3. Compare y=2x−1, y>2x−1 and y<2x−1, explaining boundary and solution regions.
    Test a point not on the line to identify each half-plane.
Check understanding
  • I change one parameter at a time.
  • I make a testable conjecture.
  • I use graphs/tables as evidence.
  • I distinguish evidence from generalisation.

Exit ticket: Why is changing both m and b at once a weak test of what m controls?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Run controlled parameter sweeps rather than random graphing. Require students to state conjecture, controlled variable, changed variable, evidence and generalisation.

Parent/carer

Ask what changes and what stays the same when one number in y=mx+b is altered. Encourage a prediction before checking digitally.
