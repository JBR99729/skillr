Modelling Rational Numbers and Percentages — AC9M8N05

Turn practical percentage and rational-number situations into mathematical models, choose efficient calculations, interpret the result in context, and review whether the assumptions and model are sensible.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPractice SheetPracticeTest
1. Learning goals
  • Formulate practical problems using rational numbers and percentages.
  • Use percentage multipliers for increases, decreases, discounts and mark-ups.
  • Interpret financial and other contextual results with suitable units and rounding.
  • Review assumptions, limitations and whether a chosen model is appropriate.

Australian Curriculum focus: use mathematical modelling to solve practical problems involving rational numbers and percentages, including financial contexts; formulate problems, choose efficient calculation strategies; interpret, communicate and review the model

2. Prerequisite knowledge

Recall percentages of quantities, fractions and decimals, integer operations, order of operations and financial vocabulary such as discount, mark-up, profit and loss.

3. Concept teaching

Mathematical modelling is a cycle: understand the situation, choose quantities and assumptions, perform mathematics, interpret the result, then review whether the model represents the situation well enough. A numerical answer alone is not the full model.

Percentage change can be represented with multipliers. Increasing by 15% means multiply by 1.15; decreasing by 20% means multiply by 0.80. The current quantity is the percentage base, so equal percentage increase and decrease do not generally undo each other.

In financial contexts, state assumptions explicitly. Prices may or may not include GST, simplified tax tables may be used, repeated changes use new bases, and money should normally be rounded to cents only after the main calculation.

4. Worked/modelled examples

Percentage increase

A $240 item is marked up by 15%: 240×1.15=$276. The multiplier combines the original 100% and the extra 15%.

Increase then decrease

$500 increased by 20% gives $600. A 20% decrease then gives 600×0.8=$480, not $500, because the second percentage uses a different base.

Model and review

A population estimate of 18,000 grows by 3% in one year: 18,000×1.03=18,540. The model assumes a uniform 3% change and ignores migration and seasonal structure, so the interpretation should state those limits.

5. Common misconceptions and corrections
  • 15% increase means add 0.15. Correction: add 15% of the original, or multiply by 1.15.
  • A 20% decrease reverses a 20% increase. Correction: the second percentage is taken from a different base quantity.
  • Rounding early has no effect. Correction: repeated calculations can accumulate rounding error, so round at the requested final stage.
  • A model is correct if the arithmetic is correct. Correction: a model also depends on assumptions, inputs and whether it represents the situation adequately.
6. Guided practice
  1. Increase $80 by 12% using a multiplier and explain the 1.12.
  2. Reduce $450 by 18% and state the final price.
  3. Compare a 10% discount followed by 10% GST with simply returning to the original price.
  4. List two assumptions in a simple model of yearly population growth.
7. Independent practice
  1. A $320 jacket is discounted by 25%. Find the sale price.
  2. A price of $150 is marked up by 30%. Find the new price.
  3. A quantity decreases from 800 to 680. Calculate the percentage decrease.
  4. A $1,200 budget is split 35%, 25% and the remainder. Find each amount.
  5. Model two consecutive 5% increases on $2,000.
  6. Create a simple financial percentage model, state one assumption, solve it and review whether the model is adequate.
8. Reasoning/problem-solving

Two shops advertise the same $400 item. Shop A offers 30% off. Shop B offers 20% off and then a further 12.5% off the reduced price. Determine which is cheaper and explain why adding the two Shop B percentages would be invalid.

9. Important questions and answers
  1. What are the main stages of a mathematical model? Formulate the situation, choose assumptions and representations, calculate, interpret and review.
  2. What multiplier represents a 17% increase? 1.17.
  3. Why do equal percentage increase and decrease not cancel? They are calculated from different base quantities.
  4. What should a model review discuss? Whether assumptions, data, calculations and representation are appropriate for the situation.
10. Assessment-style questions + review hints
  1. A price rises by 8% from $275. Find the new price and explain the multiplier.
    Use 1.08 and interpret the result in dollars.
  2. A store discounts an item by 20% and then applies a further 10% discount. Find the overall percentage reduction.
    Multiply successive factors; do not add the advertised percentages.
  3. A model assumes a town population grows by exactly 2% each year. Give one mathematical strength and two contextual limitations.
    Separate the useful consistent multiplier from real-world assumptions.
11. Exit ticket/mastery check
  • I formulate a practical percentage model.
  • I use increase and decrease multipliers correctly.
  • I interpret and round in context.
  • I review assumptions and limitations instead of stopping at arithmetic.

Exit ticket: Why does multiplying by 1.2 and then by 0.8 produce 0.96 of the original rather than the original amount?

12. Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Require students to write a one-line assumption and a one-line interpretation for every modelling task. Use competing discount offers to force reasoning about changing bases.

Parent/carer

Use sale prices, bills or simple budgets. Ask what the percentage is “of” before calculating and ask the student to explain any assumption made.
