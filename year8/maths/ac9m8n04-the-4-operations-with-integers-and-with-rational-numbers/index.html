Operations with Integers and Rational Numbers — AC9M8N04

Choose efficient strategies for positive and negative integers, fractions and decimals; track signs and operation order; and verify whether the size and sign of an answer are reasonable.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPractice SheetPracticeTest
1. Learning goals
  • Apply the four operations to integers and rational numbers.
  • Explain integer sign rules using patterns rather than slogans alone.
  • Choose efficient mental, written or digital strategies for a calculation.
  • Check answers using estimation, inverse operations and sign/magnitude reasoning.

Australian Curriculum focus: use the 4 operations with integers and with rational numbers, choosing and using efficient strategies and digital tools where appropriate

2. Prerequisite knowledge

Recall integer ordering, equivalent fractions, decimal place value, common denominators, order of operations and basic distributive and associative properties.

3. Concept teaching

Integer operations can be understood through movement, opposites and repeated patterns. Subtracting a negative adds its opposite: −6−(−9)=−6+9=3.

For multiplication and division, sign rules follow stable patterns. A positive result comes from factors with the same sign; a negative result comes from different signs. Find magnitude using the corresponding positive numbers.

Efficient rational-number work depends on representation. Fractions may need common denominators; decimals use place value; mixed calculations often benefit from exact fractions until the end. Digital tools can check a result but should not replace interpretable reasoning.

4. Worked/modelled examples

Subtracting a negative

−6−(−9)=−6+9=3. The number being subtracted is −9, so adding its opposite moves 9 units right.

Fraction calculation

3/4−5/6=9/12−10/12=−1/12. The common denominator makes the two quantities directly comparable.

Order and sign

−3²=−(3²)=−9, while (−3)²=9. Brackets decide whether the negative sign is part of the base.

5. Common misconceptions and corrections
  • Two negatives always make a positive. Correction: that shortcut applies only in specific multiplication/division or subtraction-of-a-negative structures.
  • A larger-looking digit means the negative number is larger. Correction: −8<−3 because −8 lies further left on the number line.
  • Fractions should always be converted to decimals first. Correction: exact fraction arithmetic is often more efficient and avoids premature rounding.
  • −3² and (−3)² are the same. Correction: order of operations makes them −9 and 9 respectively.
6. Guided practice
  1. Calculate −7+12 and represent the change on a number line.
  2. Calculate −4×6 and explain the sign.
  3. Find 5/6−3/4 exactly.
  4. Evaluate −2³+10 and check whether the sign and magnitude are reasonable.
7. Independent practice
  1. Calculate −15−(−8).
  2. Calculate (−7)(−4).
  3. Calculate −36÷9.
  4. Find 7/10+5/12 exactly.
  5. Evaluate 2.4−3.75+1.1.
  6. Compare two strategies for 25×(−16) and identify the more efficient one.
8. Reasoning/problem-solving

A student evaluates −8−(−3) as −11 because “two negatives make a positive” was applied without interpreting the subtraction. Reconstruct the expression step by step and explain a reliable way to avoid this error.

9. Important questions and answers
  1. What does subtracting a negative mean? Add the opposite of the negative number, so subtracting −a is equivalent to adding a.
  2. When is a product negative? When the factors have different signs.
  3. Why keep some fraction answers exact? Exact fractions avoid unnecessary rounding and can make later operations simpler.
  4. How can you check a rational-number calculation? Use estimation, an inverse operation, another representation, or a digital calculation after the reasoning.
10. Assessment-style questions + review hints
  1. Evaluate −5+3(−4)−(−6).
    Use order of operations first, then combine signed terms.
  2. Calculate 5/8÷(−15/16) exactly.
    Multiply by the reciprocal and track the sign separately.
  3. A student claims (−2)⁴=−16. Explain the error.
    The base is −2 and four negative factors multiply to a positive result.
11. Exit ticket/mastery check
  • I calculate accurately with signed numbers.
  • I can justify sign rules.
  • I choose efficient fraction and decimal strategies.
  • I check operation order, sign and magnitude.

Exit ticket: Why are −4² and (−4)² different?

12. Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Replace rule chanting with short pattern sequences and number-line interpretations. Deliberately contrast expressions where brackets change the base.

Parent/carer

Ask for a rough estimate before the exact answer. With negative numbers, also ask whether the final sign makes sense in the context.
