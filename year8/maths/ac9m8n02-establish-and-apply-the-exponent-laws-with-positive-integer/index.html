Exponent Laws — AC9M8N02

Build the exponent laws from repeated multiplication, then apply product, quotient, power-of-a-power and zero-exponent rules only when their conditions are satisfied.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPractice SheetPracticeTest
1. Learning goals
  • Explain exponent notation as repeated multiplication.
  • Establish product, quotient and power-of-a-power laws from expanded form.
  • Explain why a⁰=1 for non-zero a.
  • Apply exponent laws accurately, including multi-step expressions.

Australian Curriculum focus: establish and apply the exponent laws with positive integer exponents and the zero-exponent, using exponent notation with numbers

2. Prerequisite knowledge

Recall factors, powers, square and cube numbers, multiplication/division facts and order of operations. Be able to expand 2⁴ as 2×2×2×2.

3. Concept teaching

Exponent laws are shortcuts for counting repeated factors. They are not arbitrary rules. If the same base is multiplied, the total number of factors adds: aᵐ×aⁿ=aᵐ⁺ⁿ.

When equal-base powers are divided, common factors cancel, so aᵐ÷aⁿ=aᵐ⁻ⁿ when a≠0. A power raised to a power repeats the whole factor group, giving (aᵐ)ⁿ=aᵐⁿ.

The zero exponent follows from consistency: a³÷a³=1 but the quotient law gives a³⁻³=a⁰, so a⁰=1 for a≠0. Always check the base is the same before combining exponents.

4. Worked/modelled examples

Product of powers

2³×2⁴=(2×2×2)(2×2×2×2)=2⁷. The exponents add because there are 7 factors of 2.

Quotient and zero exponent

5⁶÷5²=5⁴. Also 7⁴÷7⁴=7⁰=1. The rule requires a non-zero base for division.

Power of a power

(3²)⁴ contains four groups of two factors of 3, so (3²)⁴=3⁸. Do not add 2+4.

5. Common misconceptions and corrections
  • Add the exponents whenever powers are added. Correction: Exponent laws here apply to multiplication/division of equal bases, not ordinary addition such as 2³+2⁴.
  • Multiply the exponents in a product of powers. Correction: For aᵐ×aⁿ, add exponents; multiplication of exponents belongs to (aᵐ)ⁿ.
  • Any number to power 0 is 0. Correction: For non-zero a, a⁰=1.
  • Different bases can be combined by adding exponents. Correction: 2³×3² cannot be rewritten as 6⁵.
6. Guided practice
  1. Expand 3²×3⁴ and use the expansion to justify the product law.
  2. Simplify 7⁹÷7⁴ and explain the cancellation.
  3. Simplify (5³)² and state which exponent law applies.
  4. Use 4⁵÷4⁵ to explain why 4⁰=1.
7. Independent practice
  1. Simplify 2⁵×2³.
  2. Simplify 10⁷÷10².
  3. Simplify (3⁴)³.
  4. Evaluate 9⁰ and state the condition on the base.
  5. Simplify 5²×5⁶÷5³.
  6. Explain why 2³+2⁴ cannot be simplified to 2⁷.
8. Reasoning/problem-solving

A student simplifies (2³×2⁴)² as 2⁹ because 3+4+2=9. Diagnose the error, give two correct solution methods, and show that both produce the same result.

9. Important questions and answers
  1. Why do exponents add when equal bases are multiplied? Because the exponent counts factors of the same base, so the factor counts combine.
  2. Why do exponents subtract in a quotient? Common equal-base factors cancel, leaving the difference in factor counts.
  3. Why is a⁰=1? For non-zero a, aⁿ÷aⁿ=1 and the quotient law gives a⁰.
  4. Can exponent laws combine different bases? Not by the equal-base product/quotient rules unless the expression is first rewritten with a common base.
10. Assessment-style questions + review hints
  1. Simplify 3⁴×3⁷÷3⁵ and justify each exponent change.
    Work through multiplication/division of the common base rather than manipulating exponents without explanation.
  2. Evaluate (2³)⁴÷2⁵.
    Use power-of-a-power first, then the quotient law.
  3. Explain why 6⁰=1 but the reasoning ‘remove all factors so nothing remains’ is incomplete.
    Use the quotient-law identity 6³÷6³=1=6⁰.
11. Exit ticket/mastery check
  • I can derive exponent laws from expanded form.
  • I check that bases match before applying laws.
  • I distinguish product, quotient and power-of-a-power rules.
  • I can justify the zero exponent for non-zero bases.

Exit ticket: Why is (a³)² equal to a⁶ rather than a⁵?

12. Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Make students expand at least one example before using each shortcut. Contrast near-miss expressions such as 2³×2⁴, 2³+2⁴ and (2³)⁴.

Parent/carer

Ask your child to explain what the exponent counts. If they can expand a power and reconstruct the shortcut, the rule is less likely to be memorised incorrectly.
