Irrational Numbers in Context

Some lengths and circle measures cannot be written as a fraction; their decimals continue without terminating or repeating.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What students learn

Some lengths and circle measures cannot be written as a fraction; their decimals continue without terminating or repeating.

Learning intention: Some lengths and circle measures cannot be written as a fraction; their decimals continue without terminating or repeating.

Success criteria

  • Explain where does π live? using the approved model.
  • Apply measure π with any circle to a new case.
  • Verify a conclusion with a second representation, estimate or test case.
Concept boundary and prerequisites

Must teach

  • recognise irrational numbers in applied contexts, including square roots and π

Prerequisites

  • Relevant Year 7 number representations and vocabulary.

May support informally

  • Digital tools may test, visualise and verify after the mathematical structure is explicit.

Do not overteach

  • Do not introduce later-year formalism that is unnecessary for this content description.
Teaching progression

This progression makes the Year 8 number relationship visible before independent application.

  1. 1. Where does π live?
    Reveal 3.141 and 3.142 first, then place π between them. Zoom to the next decimal place.
    Students: Places π between successive decimal bounds and explains that the bounds are approximations.
  2. 2. Where does √2 appear?
    Trace the two one-unit sides, then draw the diagonal. Fold the paper model to show the same long-side:short-side ratio.
    Students: Labels the right triangle, applies Pythagoras and connects the exact length √2 to its decimal approximation.
  3. 3. Two famous irrational ratios
    Compare the golden rectangle ratio with the two historical π estimates. Keep exact and approximate notation visible.
    Students: Compares the estimates, identifies the notation and explains why none is exactly π.
  4. 4. Measure π with any circle
    Wrap string around two circular objects, straighten it, and compare each circumference with its diameter.
    Students: Measures, calculates C÷d and compares results across circles.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Where does π live?

Curriculum: recognising that the real number system includes irrational numbers which can be approximately located on the real number line; for example, the value of π lies somewhere between 3.141 and 3.142 that is, 3.141 < π < 3.142

Irrational numbers have exact positions even though their decimals never end or repeat.

Teacher: Reveal 3.141 and 3.142 first, then place π between them. Zoom to the next decimal place.

Ask: How can π have one exact location if its decimal never finishes?

Look for: Uses inequality notation correctly and distinguishes the exact number π from a rounded decimal.

E2: Where does √2 appear?

Curriculum: using digital tools to systematically explore contexts or situations that use irrational numbers, such as finding the length of the hypotenuse in a right-angled triangle with the other 2 sides having lengths of one metre or 2 metres and one metre; or given the area of a square, finding the length of the side where the result is irrational; or finding ratios involved with the side lengths of paper sizes A0, A1, A2, A3 and A4

A one-unit square creates √2 on its diagonal, and A-series paper preserves a 1:√2 side ratio when folded.

Teacher: Trace the two one-unit sides, then draw the diagonal. Fold the paper model to show the same long-side:short-side ratio.

Ask: Which equation connects the two sides and the diagonal?

Look for: States d²=2 and d=√2, with the positive root justified as a length.

E3: Two famous irrational ratios

Curriculum: investigating the golden ratio in art and design, and historical approximations to π in different societies

The golden ratio appears in design, while societies have developed useful approximations for π throughout history.

Teacher: Compare the golden rectangle ratio with the two historical π estimates. Keep exact and approximate notation visible.

Ask: What makes an approximation useful even when it is not exact?

Look for: Recognises φ and π as irrational and interprets ≈ and < correctly.

E4: Measure π with any circle

Curriculum: connecting the ratio between the circumference and diameter of any circle to the irrational value of π using circular objects and string or dynamic drawing software

For every circle, circumference divided by diameter gives the same irrational ratio: π.

Teacher: Wrap string around two circular objects, straighten it, and compare each circumference with its diameter.

Ask: Which measurements change, and which ratio remains invariant?

Look for: Explains that circumference and diameter scale together and that experimental variation comes from measurement error.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Exact and approximate values are treated as interchangeable — Keep exact notation until the requested approximation step.
  • The irrational numbers in context model is copied without interpretation — Connect every label in ‘A decimal bound locates π; it does not replace π.’ to its mathematical role.
  • The result is not verified — Use a second representation, boundary case, inverse operation or simulation check.
Support, core and extend

Support: Reduce numerical or representational load while retaining the same decision and explanation.

Core: Connect at least two representations, justify the method and verify independently.

Extend: Test a boundary case, counterexample or model limitation without introducing later-year procedures.

Quick check and mastery evidence

Prompt: If we zoom in again, which two decimals could bound π more closely?

Expected idea: For example, 3.1415 < π < 3.1416. Both endpoints are rational approximations; π is the exact irrational value between them.

Students should explain the relationship in their own words, apply it in a new case and verify the result using another representation, estimate or check.
