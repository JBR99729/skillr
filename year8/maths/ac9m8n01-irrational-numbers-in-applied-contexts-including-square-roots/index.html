Irrational Numbers in Context — AC9M8N01

Some exact lengths and ratios cannot be written as fractions. Learn how square roots and π fit into the real number system, how to locate them approximately, and when to keep an answer exact.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPractice SheetPracticeTest
1. Learning goals
  • Recognise irrational numbers as real numbers that cannot be written as a ratio of integers.
  • Distinguish an exact value such as √2 or π from a decimal approximation.
  • Locate irrational numbers between rational bounds on a number line.
  • Connect irrational numbers to square diagonals, circles and other applied contexts.

Australian Curriculum focus: recognise irrational numbers in applied contexts, including square roots and π

2. Prerequisite knowledge

Recall fractions, decimals, square numbers, square roots and the real number line. Know that a terminating or recurring decimal is rational.

3. Concept teaching

Rational numbers can be written as a fraction of integers. Irrational numbers cannot. Their decimal expansions do not terminate and do not eventually repeat, but each irrational number still has one exact position on the number line.

Keep exact notation when it carries mathematical information. For example, the diagonal of a unit square is exactly √2. Writing 1.414 is useful for measurement, but it is only an approximation.

Bounds locate an irrational number without pretending the decimal has ended: 1.4² < 2 < 1.5², so 1.4 < √2 < 1.5. Similarly, 3.141 < π < 3.142.

4. Worked/modelled examples

Unit-square diagonal

A square has side length 1 m. By Pythagoras, d²=1²+1²=2, so d=√2 m. Because length is positive, use the positive root. The exact answer is √2 m; 1.414 m is an approximation.

Bounding √5

2.2²=4.84 and 2.3²=5.29. Therefore 2.2<√5<2.3. A calculator can refine the bound, but the exact value remains √5.

Measuring π

For a circle with diameter 12 cm, C=12π cm exactly. If a decimal measurement is needed, C≈37.70 cm. The ratio C÷d remains π for every circle.

5. Common misconceptions and corrections
  • An irrational number has no exact value. Correction: Irrational numbers are exact; only their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-recurring.
  • Every non-terminating decimal is irrational. Correction: Recurring decimals are rational. Irrational decimals are non-terminating and non-recurring.
  • √9 is irrational because it uses a square-root symbol. Correction: √9=3, so it is rational. The symbol alone does not decide the number type.
  • A calculator display is the exact irrational number. Correction: A finite calculator display is a rounded approximation.
6. Guided practice
  1. Classify 3/7, 0.125, 0.272727…, √3 and π as rational or irrational.
  2. Use 1.7² and 1.8² to bound √3.
  3. Find the exact diagonal of a square with side 4 cm, then give a 2-decimal approximation.
  4. Explain why 22/7 is useful but is not equal to π.
7. Independent practice
  1. State whether √16, √7, 0.625, 0.1010010001… and 5/12 are rational or irrational.
  2. Find two consecutive tenths between which √10 lies.
  3. A square has area 18 m². Give its side length exactly and to 2 decimal places.
  4. Explain the difference between x=π and x≈3.14.
  5. Order √2, 1.45 and 3/2 from least to greatest.
  6. Give an applied situation in which keeping √2 or π exact is better than rounding immediately.
8. Reasoning/problem-solving

A student says, “If π is between 3.141 and 3.142, then π must be a rational number somewhere in that interval.” Explain precisely why the conclusion does not follow. Use the number line and decimal expansion ideas in your argument.

9. Important questions and answers
  1. What makes a number irrational? It cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers; its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-recurring.
  2. Why is √2 irrational but √4 rational? √2 cannot be written as a fraction of integers, while √4=2.
  3. Can an irrational number be located on a number line? Yes. It has an exact location and can be trapped between increasingly close rational bounds.
  4. When should an answer stay in terms of π or a square root? Keep the exact form unless the context asks for a measurement or stated decimal accuracy.
10. Assessment-style questions + review hints
  1. A square has area 50 cm². Find its side length exactly and to 1 decimal place.
    Use √50 as the exact length, then round only at the end.
  2. Show that √7 lies between 2.6 and 2.7 without using a calculator square-root key.
    Compare 2.6² and 2.7² with 7.
  3. A learner writes π=3.1416. Critique the statement and rewrite it correctly.
    Distinguish equality from approximation and use ≈.
11. Exit ticket/mastery check
  • I distinguish rational and irrational numbers.
  • I preserve exact values until approximation is needed.
  • I can bound a square root between decimals.
  • I connect irrational numbers to measurements and geometry.

Exit ticket: Why can √2 have an exact position on the number line even though its decimal never ends?

12. Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Keep exact and approximate notation side by side. Ask students to justify classifications rather than memorise a list, and use square/circle contexts before more abstract decimal discussion.

Parent/carer

Ask your child whether an answer is exact or approximate and why. A useful home example is measuring the diagonal of a square and comparing it with the exact √2 relationship.
