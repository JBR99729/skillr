AC9M8N01 • Year 8 Maths

Irrational numbers in context

Recognise irrational numbers in applied contexts, including square roots and π.

Topic Guide Teacher Slides Practice Sheet Practice Test
What students learn

Irrational numbers are real numbers that cannot be written exactly as a fraction of two integers. Their decimal representations continue forever without terminating or repeating. Students connect this idea to familiar quantities such as π and √2.

Learning intention: understand that irrational numbers have exact values and exact positions even when their decimal representations never finish.

  • Locate an irrational number approximately on a number line.
  • Distinguish an exact value such as π or √2 from a decimal approximation.
  • Recognise irrational values in geometric and measurement contexts.
  • Use a second representation or estimate to verify a conclusion.
Key concepts and vocabulary

Rational number: a number that can be written as a fraction a/b where a and b are integers and b is not zero.

Irrational number: a real number that cannot be expressed as such a fraction. Its decimal does not terminate or repeat.

Exact value: notation such as π or √2 that names the number without rounding it.

Approximation: a nearby value such as π ≈ 3.142 or √2 ≈ 1.414.

Important distinction: 3.141 < π < 3.142 locates π between two rational decimals; neither decimal is equal to π.

Teaching progression and worked examples

1. Where does π live?

Place 3.141 and 3.142 on a number line, then locate π between them. Zooming further gives a tighter bound such as 3.1415 < π < 3.1416. The bounds become more precise, but π remains the exact value.

2. Where does √2 appear?

For a square with side length 1, the diagonal forms a right triangle. By Pythagoras, d² = 1² + 1² = 2, so d = √2 ≈ 1.414. The exact diagonal is √2; 1.4 or 1.414 are approximations.

3. Useful approximations are not exact equalities

Historical approximations such as 22/7 are useful for π, but they are rational numbers and therefore cannot equal the irrational number π exactly. Use ≈ or inequalities rather than =.

4. Measure π with a circle

Measure the circumference C and diameter d of several circular objects. Although C and d change with the size of the circle, the ratio C ÷ d stays close to 3.14159… . Experimental differences arise from measurement error; the exact ratio is π.

Australian Curriculum elaborations
  • E1: recognise that irrational numbers belong to the real number system and can be approximately located on a number line, for example 3.141 < π < 3.142.
  • E2: explore contexts involving irrational numbers, such as right-triangle hypotenuses, square side lengths and A-series paper ratios.
  • E3: investigate the golden ratio in art and design and historical approximations to π.
  • E4: connect circumference divided by diameter for any circle with the irrational value π.
Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • “π equals 3.14.” Correct this to π ≈ 3.14. π is exact; 3.14 is rounded.
  • “Every square root is irrational.” Counterexample: √9 = 3 is rational. Square roots of non-square positive integers such as √2 are irrational.
  • “A very accurate fraction becomes π.” A fraction remains rational however accurate the approximation is.
  • “The decimal goes on, so the number has no exact location.” Use nested decimal bounds on a number line to show one exact location.
Support, core and extend

Support: compare exact symbols with rounded decimals and use a labelled number line before calculation.

Core: connect number-line, geometric and measurement representations and justify why each value is exact or approximate.

Extend: create tighter rational bounds for an irrational number or compare competing approximations without introducing later-year formal proofs.

Quick check
  1. Why is 3.1416 not exactly π?
  2. A unit square has diagonal √2. Why is 1.4 not the exact diagonal length?
  3. What ratio should remain approximately constant when different circles are measured?

Expected ideas: the decimals are rounded rational approximations; √2 is the exact positive diagonal; circumference ÷ diameter is π.
