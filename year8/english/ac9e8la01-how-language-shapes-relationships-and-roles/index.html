How language shapes relationships and roles

Students learn that word choice, address terms, group vocabulary and register can build solidarity, mark roles and show relationships between people and communities.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

We are learning to explain how language choices signal group identity, relationships, roles and levels of social distance.

Australian Curriculum target: recognise how language shapes relationships and roles

Core concepts

  • Language choices are social signals: they can show closeness, shared identity, respect, expertise, authority or distance.
  • Group identities are formed and reinforced through shared vocabulary, acronyms, slang, specialist terminology and terms of address.
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander language names can inform relationships to Country/Place when handled respectfully and accurately.

Success criteria

  • Identify language choices that build solidarity or show authority.
  • Explain how a term of address, acronym, specialist term or register choice positions speakers and listeners.
  • Use respectful evidence when discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander language names and relationships to Country/Place.

Useful prior knowledge

  • Students can recognise audience, purpose and context in a short text.
  • Students can compare formal and informal word choices.
  • Students understand that language varies across communities and situations.
4-Step Instructional Sequence

4. 4-Step Instructional Sequence

Step 1: Explicit Teaching & Modelling

Display three versions of the same request and ask students what relationship each version suggests.

Students: Students identify words that sound friendly, respectful, expert or distant.

Step 2: Guided Analysis

Use the identity web to connect shared vocabulary with values, behaviours and belonging.

Students: Students explain why a term works inside one group but may exclude outsiders.

Step 3: Collaborative / Independent Practice

Model how a language name can be evidence of relationship to Country/Place without simplifying cultural knowledge.

Students: Students describe what can and cannot be inferred from a map reference.

Step 4: Formative Assessment

Give a short text and ask students to annotate language choices and their social effects.

Students: Students write a two-sentence explanation using evidence.

Check: Choose one language choice from a short text and explain how it shapes a relationship, role or group identity.

Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Explaining a language choice

Teacher model: Name the choice, name the relationship or role, then explain the effect on the audience.

  1. Spot: The phrase 'our chat' assumes the listener belongs to the same group.
  2. Connect: That shared context creates closeness and solidarity.
  3. Explain: It would suit a peer, but not a first email to a principal because the role relationship is different.

E1: Group language builds belonging

A group can sound like a group because members share words, shortcuts and address terms that carry shared values and behaviours.

Teacher move: Point to each identity-web node and connect it to belonging, expertise or role.

Ask: Which word would make someone sound like an insider here? Which word might confuse an outsider?

E2: Language names can show relationship to Country/Place

A language name on an authoritative map can help students recognise that language, identity and Country/Place are connected, but it must be discussed carefully and respectfully.

Teacher move: Display the source as a reference, explain that mapped language names are not simple borders, and model cautious wording.

Ask: What can this source help us notice? What should we avoid assuming from the map alone?

Three Ways To Ask

Students rewrite one request for a close friend, a teacher and an official form. They underline the words that changed.

Share: Pairs explain how one changed word altered role, distance or respect.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. Elaborations are useful examples and contexts, not a disconnected checklist.

  • E1: understanding that group identities are formed through language that reflects shared values, beliefs and behaviours, and through language choices that engender solidarity such as specialist terminology, acronyms and terms of address; for example, teenage groups and sportspeople have adopted particular words and ways of communicating
  • E2: exploring the Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies Map of Indigenous Australia and identifying language names that inform relationships to Country/Place
Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Formal language is always better.: Replace better with better fit. Ask: Who is speaking, to whom, and for what purpose?
  • Slang or acronyms are just lazy language.: Show how specialist terms and acronyms can efficiently signal group expertise or shared identity.
  • A map label tells the whole story of Country/Place.: Use cautious source language and explain that cultural knowledge needs respectful, authoritative context.

Do not overteach

  • Do not turn this into a full sociolinguistics theory lesson.
  • Do not require students to memorise Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander language groups or treat the AIATSIS map as a simple boundary map.
  • Do not judge one variety of English as better; focus on fit for relationship, role, audience and context.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Choose one language choice from a short text and explain how it shapes a relationship, role or group identity.

What a strong response should show: A complete answer quotes or names the language choice, identifies the relationship/role/group identity, and explains the effect on the audience.

Students should transfer the idea to a fresh text or composition rather than reproduce the worked example.

Support, Core and Extend

Support, Core and Extend

Support

Give two versions only and provide sentence stems: This word suggests ... because ...

Keep the concept: Still explain relationship or role, not just formal/informal.

Core

Annotate three short texts for group language, role and audience effect.

Keep the concept: Stay within text evidence and social effect.

Extend

Compare a group insider version with a public-facing version and explain how the writer changes solidarity and authority.

Keep the concept: Deepen explanation without requiring sociolinguistic theory.
