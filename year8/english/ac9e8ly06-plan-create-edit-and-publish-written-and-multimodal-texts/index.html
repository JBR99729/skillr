Learning goals

From idea to published text

AC9E8LY06 is a composing outcome. Students plan, create, revise, edit and publish written or multimodal texts. They make choices about ideas, structure, language, literary devices and visual features because those choices suit a particular purpose and audience.

What students should be able to do

  • Plan: define purpose and audience, gather and organise ideas, and choose a suitable form.
  • Create: draft a coherent text and expand important ideas rather than listing disconnected points.
  • Revise: change the order, emphasis, structure, vocabulary or multimodal design when those choices do not yet serve the purpose.
  • Edit: improve sentence control, grammar, spelling, punctuation and consistency after the larger meaning decisions are sound.
  • Publish: present the final text in a form that is readable, purposeful and appropriate for its audience.

Success criteria

  • I can explain who my audience is and what I want the text to achieve.
  • I can show how my structure helps the reader follow or respond to my ideas.
  • I can justify at least one language, literary or visual choice.
  • I can point to a meaningful revision I made between draft and final version.
  • I can edit accurately without confusing proofreading with revision.
4-Step Instructional Sequence

4. 4-Step Instructional Sequence

Step 1: Explicit Teaching & Modelling

Highlight one phrase, structure or image.

Students: Name the exact choice and describe what it makes the reader notice.

Step 2: Guided Analysis

Place two versions side by side.

Students: Identify what changes and explain why the change matters.

Step 3: Collaborative / Independent Practice

Model choice → context → effect.

Students: Build a complete analytical sentence.

Step 4: Formative Assessment

Students: Apply the frame independently.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. Elaborations are useful examples and contexts, not a disconnected checklist.

  • E3: editing for accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, and for meaning by experimenting with different order of ideas, a range of sentence structures, literary devices and vocabulary to clarify meaning for academic texts where appropriate
  • E4: using conceptual maps or journals to plan and reflect on each stage of creating a written or multimodal text
Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • A feature name is enough to prove an interpretation: Use the frame: ‘The choice ___ suggests/positions ___ because ___.’
  • Effect means whether the student personally likes the text: Replace preference with an audience effect supported by the exact wording or structure.

Do not overteach

  • Do not require university-level terminology or theory.
  • Do not treat one interpretation as valid without evidence.
  • Do not replace close reading with plot summary or personal preference.

Support, Core and Extend

Use two short versions, colour-code choice and effect, and provide the sentence frame ‘The choice ___ makes the audience ___ because ___.’

Core

Writer's workshop for AC9E8LY06

Make decisions a real editor would make

1. Start with audience and purpose

A student council speech, a museum caption and a science-expo explanation may all communicate accurate information, but they should not sound or look the same. Before drafting, write one sentence for audience and one for purpose. Those two decisions guide structure, vocabulary, tone and visual choices.

2. Revise meaning before proofreading

Draft: “Plastic pollution is bad and lots of animals are affected by it. People should do something.”

Revision question: What exactly should the audience understand or do?

Revised version: “Single-use plastics remain in the environment long after their brief use. When fragments enter waterways, animals can ingest them or become entangled. Reducing avoidable single-use items therefore targets one source of the problem.”

The improvement is not mainly spelling. The revision adds a causal chain, selects more precise vocabulary and creates a clearer persuasive direction.

3. Treat multimodal features as meaning, not decoration

If a science-expo text includes a diagram, ask what the diagram explains more efficiently than prose. If a community-news article includes a photograph, caption or data graphic, check whether it supplies evidence, context or emphasis. A visual that does not help the audience understand the message may be unnecessary.

4. Keep a visible revision trail

For one paragraph, save the first draft and final version. Underline one structural change, circle one vocabulary change and note one correction made during editing. This makes the composing process visible and gives stronger evidence of achievement than a polished final copy alone.

Common misconception

Editing is not the same as spellcheck. A writer can produce a grammatically correct paragraph that is still poorly organised or unsuited to its audience. Revise the meaning and organisation first; proofread accuracy after that.
