AC9S8I01 • Year 8 Science

Ask Questions Science Can Test

Good investigations begin with a measurable question. Scientists use patterns and models to form reasoned predictions and hypotheses, then test them with evidence.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

Observe a pattern or use a model → ask an investigable question → identify variables → make a reasoned prediction or hypothesis → test with evidence.

The key skill is not guessing. A strong prediction or hypothesis is based on scientific reasoning and can be tested.

Success criteria

  • Distinguish correlation from causation.
  • Write questions with measurable variables.
  • Use scientific models to develop questions and predictions.
  • Write hypotheses that link an independent variable, dependent variable and scientific reason.
  • Identify when uncontrolled variables weaken a causal claim.
1. Correlation is not automatically causation

A correlation means two variables change together. Causation means changing one variable produces a change in another.

Example

Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases both rise in summer. This does not mean ice-cream causes sunburn. A third factor — stronger sunlight and hotter weather — can influence both.

To support a causal claim, scientists try to control other variables and compare evidence from a suitable investigation.

2. Building an investigable question

A strong school-science question usually identifies:

  • Independent variable: the factor changed.
  • Dependent variable: the factor measured.
  • Conditions: useful details such as time, amount or measurement method.

Weak: “Why do plants hate salt?”

Better: “How does salt concentration affect the mass of potato tissue after 30 minutes?”

Questions such as “Should we…?” or “Is this good?” involve values and cannot be answered by experimental measurements alone.

3. Exploring scientific models

A scientific model represents key features or relationships. It may be a diagram, physical model, mathematical relationship or simulation.

Good model questions connect the model to observable evidence. For example: “How do different plate-boundary types affect the pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes?”

A model is useful even when simplified, but it should be evaluated by comparing its predictions with evidence.

4. Reasoned predictions

A prediction states what you expect to happen when conditions change. A reasoned prediction also connects the expected outcome to scientific understanding.

Change: reactant concentration increases.

Prediction: reaction rate will increase.

Reason: there are more reacting particles in a given volume, increasing collision frequency.

Avoid absolute wording such as “always” unless the relationship truly applies without relevant limits.

5. Writing a hypothesis

A useful hypothesis is testable, directional and scientifically reasoned.

A helpful structure is:

If [independent variable changes], then [dependent variable will change], because [scientific reason].

Example: If light exposure increases, plant growth will increase within suitable limits because greater light availability can increase the rate of photosynthesis.

A hypothesis is not proven before an investigation. It is a proposed explanation or relationship to test.

6. Variables and fair causal tests

When testing a cause-and-effect relationship, other important variables should be kept constant where possible. These are controlled variables.

If a plant experiment changes both light colour and water amount, water becomes a confounding variable. A difference in plant growth could then be caused by either factor.

Controlling variables does not make an investigation perfect, but it strengthens the causal reasoning.

Common misconceptions
  • “Two variables change together, so one must cause the other.” A third factor may explain both.
  • “Any question is investigable.” Scientific investigations need measurable evidence.
  • “A hypothesis is just a guess.” It should be based on a pattern, model or scientific explanation.
  • “A prediction and hypothesis are identical.” A prediction states an expected outcome; a hypothesis usually proposes a testable relationship with reasoning.
  • “Models must be exact copies of reality.” Models simplify reality to highlight useful relationships.
How to answer AC9S8I01 questions
  1. Identify the variables. What changes and what is measured?
  2. Check measurability. Could evidence answer the question?
  3. Check causation. Are there alternative explanations or uncontrolled variables?
  4. Use the model or pattern. What scientific relationship supports the reasoning?
  5. Qualify the claim. Avoid conclusions stronger than the evidence.
