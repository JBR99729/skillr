YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Ask whose questions shape the investigation AC9S8H02

Learning intention: Explain how locality, priorities, participation and recognition influence scientific questions and methods while respecting knowledge authority.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A public case of co-production

CSIRO describes the Gulumoerrgin/Larrakia seasonal-calendar project in the Darwin region as co-production with Larrakia knowledge holders. Public project information names Lorraine Williams, Judith Williams, Maureen Ogden, Keith Risk and Anne Risk, with CSIRO researcher Emma Woodward. This case shows how locally meaningful observations, priorities and knowledge-holder leadership shape what is documented and why. We are examining public project roles and methods here, not reproducing its seasonal knowledge or calendar.

Public case source: CSIRO Gulumoerrgin calendar project. This lesson does not supply calendar content, artwork, consultation or permission for reuse.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Connect purpose to questions

An investigation reflects decisions about what matters. A local land-management question may draw on long-term ecological relationships and place-specific categories. A school instrument-comparison question may focus on how two thermometers behave. Different purposes can produce different questions and records without making one method appropriate for every purpose.

Task P: document locally meaningful ecological relationships for local management. Task Q: compare two thermometers at the same place and time.
Try it · Why might P and Q collect different records?

Their purposes differ. P needs relevant ecological relationships and local authority; Q needs comparable instrument readings and controlled conditions. A thermometer comparison is not a replacement for an entire local knowledge system.

2. Examine participation and credit

Who can participate, whose observations are recorded, and who receives credit can affect the development of knowledge. Ask whether a project description names contributors accurately and describes their roles. A description that makes outside researchers the only creators can erase knowledge-holder contributions.

Two draft credits for the public case: A says “an outside team invented the community’s knowledge”; B acknowledges knowledge-holder leadership and documented co-production roles.
Try it · Which credit better matches the supplied case, and why?

B. It recognises that the knowledge was held by community members and that documentation was collaborative. A falsely attributes invention of that knowledge to outsiders.

3. Respect authority and intended use

Reading public project information is different from reproducing a knowledge representation. Establish who has authority, what use is proposed and what permissions or restrictions apply. Attribution is necessary but does not automatically authorise adaptation, publication or commercial use.

A student finds a publicly viewable calendar and proposes copying and changing it for a product sold online. No permission for this use is supplied.
Try it · What should happen before that copying or adaptation?

Do not copy or adapt it on the assumption that public access permits the use. Check the knowledge holders’ authority and relevant conditions, seek the required permission through appropriate channels, or choose an authorised alternative.

Common mistakes

“One calendar represents every community.” Knowledge and representations can be specific to Country/Place, community and purpose.

“Credit alone grants permission.” Acknowledgement and permission are different responsibilities.

“Perspectives are just personal guesses.” Perspectives can shape observations, categories, purposes and participation; claims still require appropriate evidence and authority.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare two inquiry briefs

Write a question for comparing school thermometers and a different question about how a documented co-production project decides what to record. Identify purpose, contributors, evidence and limitations. Use the supplied public metadata; do not invent local cultural knowledge or season names.

Adjust the challenge Support: use purpose → question → evidence prompts. Core: explain how participation changes a project. Extend: identify missing attribution or permission information in a draft plan.

No cultural artwork, local terminology or seasonal knowledge is to be copied or invented. This activity does not claim community consultation or endorsement.

Three tricky questions

1. Is scientific quality established simply by who holds a view?

No. Relevant evidence, methods and context matter. Respecting knowledge authority also determines who may share particular knowledge and under which conditions.

2. Does a published case from the Darwin region authorise use of the same categories everywhere?

No. Local categories and authority are place-specific; the case cannot be treated as a universal seasonal template.

3. Can the class claim it co-designed with a community after reading a website?

No. Reading public information is not collaboration, consultation, consent or endorsement.

FAQ

Can we discuss a public project without copying its calendar?

Yes. Describe public roles, purposes and research methods accurately, with attribution and within the source’s conditions.

Why does credit matter to knowledge development?

Credit affects whose contributions are recognised, whose questions are heard and whether future collaboration is fair and accurate.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Explain how cultural perspectives can shape scientific questions, priorities and participation.
  • Distinguish cultural influence on inquiry from evidence used to support a claim.
  • Analyse recognition, collaboration and knowledge-sharing examples.
  • Apply respectful evidence-based reasoning to unfamiliar contexts.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that science is conducted by people and communities, while scientific claims are tested using evidence. Distinguish choosing a question from deciding whether an answer is supported.

Key concept

Cultural perspectives can influence questions, priorities, participation, interpretation and recognition. This does not make evidence optional.

The Matilda effect shows how historical assumptions about gender could reduce recognition of women’s scientific contributions. Correcting attribution improves the history of how knowledge developed.

Long-term practice can identify useful questions and designs. Flexible bamboo buildings can suggest earthquake-resistant principles that engineers then test for mechanisms and limits.

Respectful work with First Nations knowledge requires appropriate knowledge holders, consent, attribution, cultural protocols and attention to Cultural and Intellectual Property.

Worked examples

First Nations knowledge

Place-based knowledge of season, wind, plants and Country can shape research questions and interpretation. Collaboration should begin with permissions and appropriate knowledge holders.

Bamboo engineering

Generations of bamboo building practice can identify flexible designs worth testing. Engineering experiments examine mechanisms and transferability.

Anti-doping technology

A social value—fair competition—creates demand for anti-doping tests. Accuracy is then judged through evidence such as sensitivity, false positives and independent validation.

Vivekananda and Tesla

Conversations between Vivekananda and Tesla show how different intellectual traditions can stimulate questions; discussion alone does not establish a physical law.

Common misconceptions
Culture only causes bias in science. Correction: perspectives can also generate questions and support innovation.
All perspectives are equally supported. Correction: scientific claims still require evidence.
Traditional knowledge can be copied unchanged anywhere. Correction: knowledge can be place-based and requires permission and interpretation.
Consultation after publication is enough. Correction: meaningful collaboration begins before questions and methods are fixed.
Guided practice
  1. Identify the perspective or value in a scenario.
  2. State which part of science it influences.
  3. Name the evidence needed to test the scientific claim.
  4. Explain one ethical collaboration requirement.
Independent practice
  1. Explain how the Matilda effect can change the historical record of science.
  2. Describe how bamboo-building knowledge could contribute to earthquake engineering.
  3. Explain why fair-play values can influence anti-doping research without determining test accuracy.
  4. Give two reasons First Nations knowledge should be treated as diverse and place-based.
  5. A medical study samples only adults from one city. Explain how this could limit the knowledge produced.
  6. Explain the difference between a cultural priority choosing a question and evidence supporting an answer.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A research team wants to enter a community knowledge category into a scientific database, but the translation removes distinctions important to knowledge holders. Propose a scientifically and culturally responsible response.

Questions and answers
  1. How can world views influence science? They can shape questions, priorities, participation, methods, interpretation and recognition.
  2. Do cultural perspectives replace evidence? No; claims still require evidence and reasoning.
  3. Why involve knowledge holders early? Early collaboration improves relevance, permissions, methods, attribution and interpretation.
  4. What is a key principle for First Nations knowledge? Respect diversity, place-based authority, cultural protocols, consent and attribution.
Practice and review
  1. Analyse how anti-doping research combines a social value with scientific evidence.
    Separate the fairness priority from evidence used to validate a test.
  2. Explain how the Matilda effect can influence scientific history.
    Discuss participation, recognition and re-examination of records.
  3. Evaluate publishing culturally restricted knowledge without community permission.
    Address consent, Cultural and Intellectual Property, attribution and limits on sharing.
Check understanding
  • I separate perspective from scientific evidence.
  • I explain how participation and recognition shape science.
  • I apply respectful collaboration principles.
  • I avoid treating all cultural knowledge as one uniform system.

Exit ticket: How can a perspective influence science without replacing evidence?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Use sources that distinguish knowledge contribution, scientific testing and attribution. Avoid tokenistic or pan-Indigenous examples.

Parent/carer

Ask the student to identify what a community perspective changes in a research process and what evidence is still needed.

Support: separate “perspective” and “evidence” in a two-column organiser.
Core: analyse collaboration and recognition scenarios.
Extend: evaluate cross-knowledge translation and authority without overgeneralising.
