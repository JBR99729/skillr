How World Views Influence Scientific Knowledge

Science is evidence-based, but it is carried out by people and communities. Cultural perspectives can influence which questions are asked, whose knowledge is recognised, which problems receive attention and how people collaborate.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

World view or cultural perspective → influences questions, priorities, participation or interpretation → scientific investigation and evidence → development of knowledge or technology.

The key distinction is that culture can influence how science develops; it does not make unsupported claims scientifically correct. Evidence, testing and reasoning are still required.

Success criteria

  • Explain at least two ways a cultural perspective can influence scientific work.
  • Distinguish influence on research priorities from evidence used to test a claim.
  • Analyse examples of recognition, collaboration, technology development and knowledge practices.
  • Explain why diverse perspectives can improve inquiry without making every perspective equally supported by evidence.
Five curriculum examples

E1 — The Matilda effect

Historical world views about gender sometimes restricted women's access to scientific institutions and reduced recognition of their contributions. The Matilda effect describes the under-recognition or misattribution of women's scientific work. Historians can use laboratory notes, correspondence and publication records to build a more accurate account of who contributed.

E2 — Cultural building knowledge and earthquakes

Building practices developed through long experience with local materials and hazards can provide valuable engineering knowledge. Flexible bamboo structures, for example, can suggest design principles worth testing for earthquake resistance. Cultural experience can guide the question; engineering tests determine how and where the principle works.

E3 — Different world views: Vivekananda and Tesla

Discussions between Swami Vivekananda and Nikola Tesla are an example of people from different intellectual traditions exploring ideas about matter and energy. The important science-as-a-human-endeavour concept is that different perspectives can stimulate questions and connections. Discussion alone does not prove a physical law; scientific ideas require testable evidence.

E4 — Fairness in sport and anti-doping technology

Fair competition is a social value. As sporting organisations identify banned performance-enhancing substances, scientists develop faster and more accurate detection methods. This shows how a cultural priority can create demand for scientific research and technological innovation. Test accuracy, false positives and false negatives matter because scientific results have consequences for athletes.

E5 — First Nations Australian material culture

First Nations Australian material culture can draw on connected knowledge of Country, materials, seasons, ecology, design and use. These knowledges are diverse and place-based; they should not be presented as one uniform system. Respectful scientific collaboration involves appropriate knowledge holders, permissions, attribution and cultural protocols.

Worked reasoning examples

Example 1 — Bamboo design

Observation: Flexible traditional buildings survive shaking better than some rigid structures.

Cultural contribution: Generations of building experience identified useful designs and materials.

Scientific development: Engineers can test flexibility, joints and loads to determine the mechanism and apply useful principles safely to new structures.

Limit: Survival in one setting does not prove that an unchanged design is safe at every scale or location.

Example 2 — Fairness in sport

Value: Athletes should compete under agreed fair conditions.

Problem: New performance-enhancing substances may be difficult to detect.

Scientific response: Researchers develop and validate new chemical tests.

Evidence: Detection rate, false-positive rate and independent validation determine whether a test performs reliably.

World views do not replace evidence

Students often confuse two different ideas:

  • Choosing what to investigate: values, needs and cultural perspectives can strongly influence this.
  • Deciding whether a scientific claim is supported: this requires appropriate evidence and reasoning.

For example, concern about clean drinking water may cause a community to prioritise contamination research. The concern determines the priority; measurements and testing determine whether the water contains a contaminant.

Common misconceptions
  • “Culture only causes bias in science.” Cultural perspectives can also generate questions, preserve useful observations, identify priorities and support innovation.
  • “If perspectives differ, every explanation is equally valid.” No. Scientific explanations still need evidence.
  • “Traditional knowledge needs no testing.” Knowledge can guide scientific inquiry; claims about mechanisms or transfer to new contexts should be investigated appropriately.
  • “All First Nations Australian knowledge is the same.” First Nations knowledges are diverse and connected to particular peoples, communities and Country.
  • “Correcting historical recognition changes the experimental result.” Correct attribution improves the history of how knowledge developed; it does not automatically alter the underlying data.
Assessment strategy

For AC9S8H02, strong questions should go beyond recalling definitions. Expect short sources, scenarios or data and ask:

  1. What perspective, value or assumption is present?
  2. What part of science does it influence — question, participation, method, interpretation, recognition or technology?
  3. What evidence would be needed to evaluate the scientific claim?
  4. What conclusion is justified without overgeneralising?
