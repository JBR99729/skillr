AC9S8I02 • Year 8 Science

Plan and Conduct Reproducible Investigations

A strong investigation is not only scientifically fair — it must also be repeatable, safe, ethical and respectful of Country/Place, heritage sites and artefacts where relevant.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

Question or hypothesis → variables and method → controls and assumptions → risk/ethics/heritage checks → repeatable procedure → evidence.

Reproducibility means another investigator can follow the recorded method under the same conditions and obtain comparable evidence.

Success criteria

  • Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Explain how uncontrolled variables weaken causal conclusions.
  • Recognise assumptions and limitations in analogous models.
  • Choose risk controls that reduce hazards without destroying the purpose of the investigation.
  • Apply ethical principles to biological samples, surveys and secondary data.
  • Plan respectful fieldwork around heritage sites and artefacts on Country/Place.
1. Variables, controls and reproducibility

Independent variable: the factor deliberately changed. Dependent variable: the factor measured. Controlled variables: relevant factors kept constant.

If more than one relevant factor changes, results may have more than one possible explanation. A reproducible method should also record quantities, equipment, timing, units and conditions precisely.

Example

Question: How does salt concentration affect mass change in potato cylinders?

Independent: salt concentration. Dependent: change in potato mass. Controls: potato variety, cylinder size, solution volume, temperature and exposure time.

Using different potato varieties would introduce an uncontrolled variable because varieties may differ in cell properties.

2. Assumptions in scientific models

Analogous models deliberately represent only selected features of a real system. Their assumptions should be stated so conclusions are not pushed beyond what the model can show.

  • Dialysis tubing can represent selective movement across a barrier but not every living membrane process.
  • A shake table can model some earthquake motion but not every complex ground movement.
  • A baking-soda volcano can model pressure release but not real magma chemistry or tectonic processes.

Key question: What feature is being represented, and what important feature is missing?

3. Risk management

A hazard is something capable of causing harm. Risk considers both the likelihood and possible consequence of that harm.

Good planning identifies hazards and selects appropriate controls. Examples include eye protection for splash risks, checking electrical leads, using safer concentrations, barriers, ventilation or changing a method where necessary.

The aim is not to remove every possible hazard at any cost; it is to reduce risk to an acceptable level while preserving the scientific purpose.

4. Ethical investigations and secondary data
  • Consent: participants should understand what is being collected and why.
  • Privacy: personal information should not be unnecessarily identifiable.
  • Voluntary participation: people should not be pressured into participating.
  • Data use: biological material, images and secondary data should be used consistently with the permission and purpose under which they were collected.

Publicly available data is not automatically free of ethical responsibilities. If sensitive health information can identify individuals, a de-identified or otherwise appropriate dataset should be used.

5. Heritage sites, artefacts and Country/Place

Scientific fieldwork may occur on Country/Place containing culturally significant sites, artefacts or materials. Investigators should seek guidance about permissions, cultural protocols, restricted areas and appropriate methods before collecting or disturbing material.

Where the scientific question can be answered without damage, non-destructive methods are preferable. Scientific purpose does not automatically override cultural heritage responsibilities.

Common misconceptions
  • “Reproducible means everyone gets exactly the same number.” Comparable results are expected, but normal measurement variation can remain.
  • “The independent variable should be controlled.” No — it is deliberately changed.
  • “A model is useless if it is not identical to reality.” Models are useful when their purpose and limitations are clear.
  • “Low probability means no safety control is needed.” Serious consequences can justify controls even when likelihood is low.
  • “Public data can always be used without privacy concerns.” Ethical use still matters.
  • “Small samples from heritage sites are harmless.” Removal or damage can still be culturally significant and may require permission.
How to answer AC9S8I02 questions
  1. Variables: What is changed, measured and controlled?
  2. Reproducibility: Could another group repeat the method precisely?
  3. Assumptions: What does the model represent, and what does it leave out?
  4. Risk: What could cause harm, and what control reduces that risk?
  5. Ethics: Are consent, privacy and appropriate data use addressed?
  6. Heritage: Are permissions, protocols and non-destructive options considered?
