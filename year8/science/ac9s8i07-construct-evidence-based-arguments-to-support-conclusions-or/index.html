AC9S8I07 • Year 8 Science

Build an Evidence-Based Argument

A scientific argument links a clear claim to relevant, credible evidence through explicit reasoning and responsible citation.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What students learn

We are learning to explain build an evidence-based argument using models and evidence.

Success criteria

  • Identify and connect claim, source quality, evidence.
  • Use a model to explain rather than only describe the concept.
  • Apply the central idea to a curriculum example and justify the connection.
  • Evaluate one model limit, misconception or evidence boundary.
Concept boundary and prerequisites

Must teach

  • Arguments distinguish claim, evidence and reasoning.
  • Evidence quality and competing explanations are evaluated.
  • Sources and culturally governed information are used with attribution and protocol.

Prerequisites

  • Read a labelled scientific diagram or data display.
  • Distinguish an observation from an explanation.
  • Use evidence to support a short scientific statement.

Do not overteach

  • Do not use restricted cultural information without permission.
  • Do not overgeneralise beyond the evidence population and conditions.
Teaching progression
  1. 1. Clarify the claim
    Use the shared model to foreground claim.
    Students: Annotate or explain the link involving claim.
  2. 2. Evaluate sources
    Use the shared model to foreground source quality.
    Students: Annotate or explain the link involving source quality.
  3. 3. Select evidence
    Use the shared model to foreground evidence.
    Students: Annotate or explain the link involving evidence.
  4. 4. Explain the link
    Use the shared model to foreground reasoning.
    Students: Annotate or explain the link involving reasoning.
Worked examples

Observe → connect → conclude

Claim: Battery A lasts longer under the tested load.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Evaluate: claim

evaluating the quality of evidence of primary and secondary sources used when constructing an argument to support a conclusion or claim

Use the quality of evidence of primary and secondary sources to make the role of claim visible and connect the evidence to the unit’s central explanation.

E2: Examine: source quality

examining competing ideas, differences in methods and sources of error when constructing an argument

Use competing ideas, differences in methods and sources of error to make the role of source quality visible and connect the evidence to the unit’s central explanation.

E3: Acknowledge: evidence

acknowledging and considering the ethical issues or cultural protocols when using or citing secondary data, such as acknowledging sources and respecting cultural protocols around sharing of particular information

Use considering the ethical issues or cultural protocols when using to make the role of evidence visible and connect the evidence to the unit’s central explanation.

E4: Analyse: reasoning

analysing what evidence would be necessary to support the conclusion that all buildings in an earthquake area should be made of bamboo

Use what evidence would be necessary to support the conclusion to make the role of reasoning visible and connect the evidence to the unit’s central explanation.

E5: Evaluate: ethical citation and conclusion

evaluating a claim that one brand of battery lasts longer than another brand of battery

Use a claim that one brand of battery lasts longer to make the role of ethical citation and conclusion visible and connect the evidence to the unit’s central explanation.

Common misconceptions
  • More sources automatically means stronger evidence.
  • A quotation is reasoning.
