Build an Evidence-Based Scientific Argument

A strong scientific argument does more than list facts. It makes a specific claim, selects relevant and credible evidence, explains the reasoning, evaluates competing evidence and uses secondary information responsibly.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core argument structure

Claim → Evidence → Reasoning → Limitations / competing evidence → Qualified conclusion.

The evidence must actually measure something relevant to the claim. A broad claim needs broader evidence than a narrow claim.

Success criteria

  • Distinguish strong evidence from anecdotes, advertising and unsupported claims.
  • Compare methods when sources disagree.
  • Match the amount and type of evidence to the claim being made.
  • Cite secondary information so the evidence is traceable.
  • Consider ethics, sensitive information and cultural protocols.
E1 — Evaluate evidence quality

Ask:

  • Is the evidence relevant to the claim?
  • Is there enough evidence to show a consistent pattern?
  • Was the evidence collected using a suitable method?
  • Is the source transparent and appropriately expert?
  • Could funding or another interest create bias?
  • Can the result be checked or replicated?

A peer-reviewed paper is not automatically perfect, and a manufacturer study is not automatically false. Evaluate the evidence and methods, then seek independent support where appropriate.

E2 — Compare competing ideas and methods

Two studies can disagree because they investigated different conditions. Before choosing one conclusion, compare:

  • independent and controlled variables
  • sample size and replication
  • instrument calibration and resolution
  • definitions of the measured outcome
  • environmental conditions
  • possible contamination or measurement error.

Battery example

Study A tests a battery under a constant 1.0 A load. Study B uses a variable lower load. Different lifetimes do not necessarily conflict: load changes discharge rate. A valid brand comparison should use matched conditions.

E3 — Ethics and cultural protocols

Secondary information has responsibilities attached to its use.

  • Attribute: acknowledge the people, community or publication that provided the knowledge.
  • Represent accurately: do not alter meaning to suit an argument.
  • Respect permissions: some cultural knowledge is restricted or governed by protocols about access and sharing.
  • Preserve context: do not remove information needed to understand knowledge appropriately.
  • Protect sensitive data: for example, exact locations of threatened species may require restricted publication.

First Nations Australian knowledges are diverse and place-based. Appropriate knowledge holders and community protocols should guide use and sharing where relevant.

E4 — What evidence does the claim require?

Start by identifying the exact outcome in the claim.

  • “Bamboo withstands shaking.” → structural test data.
  • “Bamboo is safer than concrete.” → comparable performance under matched conditions.
  • “Bamboo reduces injuries.” → building and injury data while considering other factors.
  • “Bamboo is more sustainable.” → lifecycle evidence about growth, processing, transport, durability and replacement.
  • “Bamboo is universally safe.” → evidence across many designs, sites and earthquake conditions; one test cannot justify this scope.
E5 — Evaluate a claim with a fair comparison

Brand A vs Brand B batteries

Weak argument: “Brand A feels stronger and lasted longer in my torch.”

Better method: use batteries of comparable age, identical electrical loads, the same temperature and a defined end point. Repeat each brand several times.

Better conclusion: “Under the tested load and conditions, Brand A had a greater mean discharge time than Brand B.”

This conclusion stays within the evidence instead of claiming Brand A is always better in every device.

Source credibility is not a simple ranking

Do not use rules such as “websites are bad” or “peer review proves truth”. Instead ask who produced the information, how the evidence was collected, whether methods are visible, whether other evidence agrees and whether there is a possible conflict of interest.

Triangulation means checking a claim against multiple relevant sources or forms of evidence rather than relying on one convenient source.

Common mistakes
  • Using one trial to support a broad claim.
  • Choosing evidence because it agrees with a preferred conclusion.
  • Treating popularity, confidence or professional presentation as evidence quality.
  • Comparing studies without checking whether their methods are equivalent.
  • Using a correlation as automatic proof of causation.
  • Citing a source without explaining how its evidence supports the claim.
  • Assuming publicly accessible cultural information is automatically unrestricted.
  • Publishing sensitive secondary data without considering possible harm.
Assessment strategy
  1. Claim: What exactly is being argued?
  2. Evidence: Is it relevant, sufficient and credible?
  3. Method: Were comparisons fair and variables controlled?
  4. Reasoning: Does the evidence logically support the claim?
  5. Alternatives: Is there competing evidence or another explanation?
  6. Responsibility: Are citation, ethics, sensitivity and cultural protocols handled appropriately?
