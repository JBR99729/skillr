YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Make the claim fit the evidence AC9S8I07

Learning intention: Construct reasoned arguments, evaluate the quality and limits of secondary evidence, and respect ethical and cultural conditions on its use.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Ten minutes longer in this tested set

This original hypothetical report tests three separate batteries of type A and three of type B, with the same size, chemistry, storage conditions and temperature. A regulated test draws 0.10 A from each battery until output reaches a 1.0 V cut-off; test order is randomised. A lasts 60, 63 and 57 minutes; B lasts 70, 71 and 69 minutes. Mean lifetimes are 60 and 70 minutes. B averaged 10 minutes longer in this tested set under these conditions. Six batteries do not establish that B always lasts longer in every device or that one particular chemical mechanism caused the difference.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Build claim, evidence, reasoning and limitation

State a precise claim, select relevant measurements and explain the link. The claim concerns lifetime to a defined cut-off at a fixed current, so those timed records directly bear on it. Matched conditions reduce alternatives. Add the small sample and conditions as limitations; numbers without an explanation are not a complete argument.

Claim draft: “B is the best battery for everyone.” The only supplied evidence is the six-unit test.
Try it · Write a defensible replacement argument.

“B averaged 70 minutes to the 1.0 V cut-off at 0.10 A, compared with A’s 60 minutes. Because current, cut-off and stated conditions matched, these times support longer mean lifetime for B in this tested set. More units and operating conditions are needed for broader claims.”

2. Evaluate sources and competing evidence

Trace a secondary report to its data and methods. Examine relevance, sample, units, transparency, expertise, funding and corroboration. A manufacturer’s funding is a reason to check possible bias, not automatic proof that measurements are false. Different loads, cut-offs or storage histories can make two accurate studies answer different questions.

Source X is an advert saying “B wins” with no methods. Source Y supplies the table and protocol above. A third report uses a different current and cut-off.
Try it · Which source best supports the narrow worked claim, and how should you treat the third report?

Y supplies relevant, traceable measurements for the stated conditions. X lacks evidence needed to judge its claim. Compare the third report’s methods and conditions; a different result under a different load need not contradict Y.

3. Use secondary information responsibly

Identify creator or organisation, title, date and stable source details, distinguish the source’s result from your inference and check reuse conditions. Citation does not grant permission to republish data, images or restricted knowledge. Protect privacy and follow relevant knowledge-holder guidance, context and cultural protocols for First Nations information.

A public webpage describes community knowledge but states that images and detailed records need knowledge-holder permission before republication.
Try it · Can a student copy the images into a public poster by adding a source link?

No. A link provides attribution, not the required permission. Seek guidance and permission through the teacher or use an authorised alternative; preserve context and any access or sharing restrictions.

Common mistakes

“One source proves a universal claim.” Check relevance, methods, sample and the breadth of evidence.

“A citation is the reasoning.” Explain why the source’s evidence bears on the exact claim.

“Online means unrestricted reuse.” Public access does not remove privacy, copyright or cultural conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Construct and challenge an argument

Write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph from the battery table with a limitation. A partner asks one challenge about methods or scope. Revise the paragraph, then add the information needed for a source citation and one reuse condition you would check.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the four argument headings. Core: answer a specific challenge with evidence. Extend: identify the additional lifetime, cost or lifecycle evidence needed for a broader “best battery” recommendation.

This is a paper evidence task, not instructions for battery testing, circuit construction, charging or handling damaged batteries.

Three tricky questions

1. Would testing the same depleted battery three times give three independent units?

No. These records specify separate batteries. Repeated use of one battery changes its state and does not sample three units.

2. If every B value exceeds every A value here, is “B always wins” justified?

No. That pattern holds in this small tested set. It does not cover every battery, device, current or environment.

3. Should a contrary source be ignored because its author is less famous?

No. Examine its relevant methods and evidence. Reputation alone neither resolves a conflict nor determines the strength of an argument.

FAQ

Can a qualified argument still be persuasive?

Yes. Accurate scope and honest limitations let readers assess the evidence and can make the reasoning stronger.

What should I do when permission is unclear?

Do not assume unrestricted reuse. Ask the teacher to help identify the relevant rights or knowledge holders and use an authorised alternative if needed.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Construct claim–evidence–reasoning arguments with limitations.
  • Evaluate relevance, sufficiency, transparency and possible bias.
  • Compare methods when sources disagree.
  • Use attribution, permissions and cultural protocols for secondary information.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall how method quality, sample size, controlled variables, conflicting evidence and conclusion limits affect a scientific claim.

Key concept

Use claim → evidence → reasoning → limitation or competing evidence → qualified conclusion. An anecdote or polished presentation is not a substitute for relevant measurements.

Evaluate sample size, method, transparency, expertise, replication and possible funding interests. Peer review is useful but not a guarantee; industry evidence is not automatically false.

When studies disagree, compare variables, instrument calibration, definitions, environment and sample characteristics. Triangulation checks a claim against multiple relevant sources or forms of evidence.

Secondary data use requires attribution, accurate representation, privacy and respect for permissions. First Nations knowledges are diverse, place-based and governed by appropriate knowledge holders and cultural protocols; sensitive data may require restricted publication.

Worked examples

Battery comparison

Compare brands using batteries of similar age, identical loads, the same temperature, a defined end point and repeated trials. Conclude only for the tested conditions.

Competing studies

A battery under a constant 1.0 A load can have a different lifetime from one under a variable lower load. The results may reflect method differences rather than a true contradiction.

Bamboo claim scope

“Bamboo withstands shaking” needs structural test data; “bamboo is more sustainable” needs lifecycle evidence about growth, processing, transport, durability and replacement.

Responsible secondary information

Attribute the community or publication, preserve context and permissions, and protect exact locations of threatened species or other sensitive data.

Common misconceptions
One trial supports a broad claim. Correction: Broader claims need broader, repeated evidence.
The source label decides truth. Correction: Methods, relevance, transparency and independent support matter.
Citing a source completes the reasoning. Correction: Explain how its evidence supports the claim.
Public cultural information is unrestricted. Correction: Access does not remove attribution, context or protocol responsibilities.
Guided practice
  1. Write the exact claim.
  2. Select evidence that measures the claimed outcome.
  3. Explain the scientific link between evidence and claim.
  4. Add one limitation, competing explanation or responsibility.
Independent practice
  1. Turn a battery anecdote into a fair comparison.
  2. Evaluate a manufacturer-funded study without dismissing it automatically.
  3. Explain why different electrical loads can produce different battery lifetimes.
  4. Match the two supplied bamboo claims—“withstands shaking” and “is more sustainable”—to the structural-test or lifecycle evidence each requires.
  5. Explain triangulation using three source types.
  6. Plan ethical citation of place-based First Nations knowledge or sensitive species data.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Two credible sources disagree about a material’s sustainability. Construct a plan to compare lifecycle boundaries, methods, funding, cultural considerations and independent evidence before reaching a qualified conclusion.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes evidence relevant? It measures an outcome that bears directly on the stated claim.
  2. What is triangulation? Checking a claim using multiple relevant sources or evidence types.
  3. How should competing evidence be handled? Compare methods and conditions, then qualify the conclusion.
  4. Why do cultural protocols matter? They govern respectful access, attribution, context and sharing of knowledge.
Practice and review
  1. Evaluate “Brand A is always better” from one torch trial.
    Identify anecdotal evidence, uncontrolled conditions, scope and a fair repeated test.
  2. Assess a bamboo sustainability claim.
    Require lifecycle evidence and identify the exact claim boundary.
  3. Use secondary cultural knowledge responsibly.
    Address knowledge holders, permission, attribution, context and restrictions.
Check understanding
  • I match evidence to claims.
  • I compare source methods.
  • I include limitations and alternatives.
  • I use secondary information responsibly.

Exit ticket: Why can a credible source still provide insufficient evidence for a claim?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Use competing source packets and require students to evaluate methods before choosing a conclusion.

Parent/carer

Ask: What is the exact claim, what evidence measures it, and what would count against it?

Support: use a claim–evidence–reasoning frame.
Core: qualify with limitations.
Extend: synthesise competing evidence and cultural responsibilities.
