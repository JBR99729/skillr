How New Evidence Changes Scientific Knowledge

Science is not a fixed list of facts. Explanations are strengthened, refined or replaced when reliable new evidence, better technologies or new perspectives reveal something the earlier explanation could not account for.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What students need to understand

The central idea is a simple evidence chain:

Earlier explanation → new evidence or perspective → mismatch or new question → testing → revised or stronger explanation.

Success criteria

  • Explain why scientific knowledge can change without saying that science is unreliable.
  • Identify how a new technology can produce evidence that was previously unavailable.
  • Explain why one new result is not automatically enough to overturn an accepted explanation.
  • Compare an older and newer explanation and identify what additional evidence the newer one accounts for.
  • Apply this reasoning to examples from biology, medicine, Earth science and chemistry.
Core concepts

Scientific knowledge is evidence-based

Scientific explanations are accepted because they account for available evidence and make useful, testable predictions. They remain open to revision if reliable evidence shows important limits.

Technology can extend observation

Microscopes, medical scanners and geophysical imaging do not make science automatically correct. They allow scientists to collect evidence that could not previously be observed.

New perspectives can expose assumptions

A different perspective may lead scientists to ask a new question, collect a different type of evidence or test an assumption that was previously taken for granted. The new idea still has to be judged against evidence.

Strong revisions explain more, not less

A useful revised model should account for reliable earlier evidence as well as the new evidence that challenged the earlier explanation.

Example 1 — Microscopes and medical imaging

Early microscopes revealed cells. Later microscopes with greater resolution revealed smaller structures inside cells. Medical technologies such as MRI and CT allowed scientists and doctors to examine internal organs in ways that were not previously possible.

Why knowledge changed: better tools produced new observations. Models of cells and organs became more detailed because there was more evidence to explain.

Key distinction: a newer instrument is not automatically trustworthy simply because it is newer. Calibration, repeat observations and comparison with other evidence still matter.

Example 2 — Repair and replacement of organs

Advances in materials science, electronics and engineering have enabled artificial joints, heart valves, pacemakers and cochlear implants.

These developments required evidence about how materials and devices behave inside the body. For example, a material for an artificial joint must have suitable strength and wear resistance, but it must also be biocompatible.

Scientific change: new technology did not only create devices; testing those devices also expanded knowledge about how body systems can be supported, repaired or replaced.

Example 3 — Sustainability and mining

Mining practices can change when environmental evidence and sustainability priorities become more important. Examples include water recycling, lower-energy processing, reduced waste, replacing topsoil, controlling erosion and revegetating disturbed land with suitable native species.

Important reasoning: sustainability usually involves trade-offs. A process that uses less water but more energy should be evaluated using several types of evidence rather than one measure alone.

Mine-site regeneration is therefore not simply “planting trees”. It involves rebuilding conditions that support stable soil, vegetation and ecosystem recovery.

Example 4 — Continental drift to plate tectonics

Alfred Wegener argued that continents had moved. He used evidence such as matching coastlines, fossils and rock patterns, but scientists did not yet have a convincing mechanism explaining how continents could move.

Later evidence included:

  • seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges
  • symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side of ridges
  • young ocean crust near ridges and older crust farther away
  • global patterns of earthquakes and volcanoes along plate boundaries.

This evidence helped produce the modern theory of plate tectonics, which explained both the movement and the mechanism more successfully than continental drift alone.

Exam idea: plate tectonics did not become accepted simply because it was newer. It became stronger because multiple independent lines of evidence supported it.

Example 5 — Imaging beneath Earth's surface

Scientists cannot directly see kilometres beneath sedimentary rock, so they use indirect evidence. Seismic and other geophysical techniques measure how waves or signals respond to different underground materials and structures.

These methods can help identify structures associated with mineral, energy or groundwater resources.

Limit: an image or wave pattern may suggest a resource, but it does not automatically prove the exact material or its quality. Additional surveys, sampling or drilling may be needed.

Example 6 — Newton and Einstein

Newton developed a mathematical model of motion and gravitation that explained a very wide range of observations. Centuries later, Einstein questioned assumptions about space, time, motion and gravity under conditions where Newton's framework had limits.

Einstein's theories did not make Newton's work useless. Newtonian mechanics remains extremely useful for many everyday situations, while relativity provides a broader explanation under conditions such as very high speeds or strong gravitational effects.

Big idea: scientific change can refine the range in which an earlier model is useful rather than simply declaring the earlier model “wrong”.

Example 7 — Mendeleev and the periodic table

Mendeleev organised elements by recurring patterns in their properties and used atomic mass as an important guide. He left gaps where the patterns suggested undiscovered elements should exist.

One reason his representation became influential was that later discoveries matched important properties he had predicted for missing elements.

Why this matters: a scientific representation becomes stronger when it organises existing evidence and successfully predicts new evidence.

How to answer AC9S8H01 questions
  1. Name the earlier idea. What did scientists previously think or what could they previously observe?
  2. Identify the new evidence, technology or perspective.
  3. Explain the mismatch or new information. What could the earlier explanation not account for?
  4. State how knowledge changed. Was the explanation refined, extended or replaced?
  5. Judge the evidence. Was it repeated, independent, predictive or supported by several sources?

Worked example

Question: Why did magnetic striping strengthen plate tectonic theory?

Answer: Symmetrical magnetic stripes on both sides of mid-ocean ridges showed that new ocean crust formed at the ridge and moved outward. This provided evidence for seafloor spreading, giving scientists a mechanism that Wegener's original continental drift explanation lacked.

Common misconceptions
  • “Science changes, so science cannot be trusted.” Scientific knowledge changes because it is tested against evidence; this process is a strength.
  • “One new result overturns a theory.” Scientists normally check reliability, repeat measurements and compare the result with other evidence.
  • “Newer means better.” New explanations are accepted when they account for evidence more successfully, not simply because they are recent.
  • “Different perspectives are all equally correct.” Perspectives may lead to useful questions, but scientific claims still require evidence.
  • “Einstein proved Newton wrong.” Einstein extended scientific understanding; Newton's equations remain useful within their appropriate range.
  • “Wegener had no evidence.” Wegener had evidence for continental movement, but lacked a convincing mechanism that later plate tectonic evidence supplied.
