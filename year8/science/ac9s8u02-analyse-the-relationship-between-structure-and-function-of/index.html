From Cells to Organ Systems

Specialised cells form tissues; tissues build organs; organs work together in systems. At every level, structure supports function and helps the organism survive.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

Multicellular organisms depend on organisation and specialisation. A specialised cell has features that suit a job; similar cells can form a tissue; tissues combine in organs; organs coordinate in organ systems. To explain AC9S8U02 well, do not just name a structure—link structure → function → effect on the whole organism.

Success criteria

  • Order and explain cell → tissue → organ → organ system.
  • Explain how a specialised structure helps a cell, tissue or organ perform its function.
  • Compare one plant transport system with one animal transport system.
  • Interpret a diagram or simple data set and make an evidence-based conclusion.
  • Predict how damage at one level can affect an organ system and survival.
  • Explain what an artificial organ can replace or support, and one limitation.
Biological organisation visual

Cell

A specialised living unit.

Example: cardiac muscle cell, root hair cell.

Tissue

Groups of cells working together.

Example: cardiac muscle tissue, xylem tissue.

Organ

Different tissues cooperating for a major function.

Example: heart, leaf, lung.

Organ system

Organs coordinating to keep the organism functioning.

Example: circulatory system, plant transport system.

Quick check: Put these in increasing organisation: heart, cardiac muscle cell, circulatory system, cardiac muscle tissue. Explain why the heart is an organ rather than a tissue.
Structure–function cheat sheet

Root hair cell

Structure: long projection.

Function: increases surface area for water and mineral uptake.

Xylem

Structure: hollow, strengthened vessels.

Function: transports water and helps support the plant.

Phloem

Structure: living transport tissue.

Function: moves dissolved sugars between sources and sinks.

Alveoli

Structure: many small sacs with thin moist walls and capillaries.

Function: large surface area and short diffusion distance for gas exchange.

Capillaries

Structure: very thin walls and narrow diameter.

Function: exchange of substances between blood and tissues.

Left ventricle

Structure: thick muscular wall.

Function: generates pressure to pump blood around the body.

Villi

Structure: many projections with blood vessels.

Function: increases surface area for nutrient absorption.

Trachea

Structure: cartilage support plus ciliated lining.

Function: stays open for airflow and helps clear trapped particles.

Worked examples: Observe → Connect → Conclude

1. Alveoli and gas exchange

Observe: Alveoli are numerous, have thin moist walls and are closely surrounded by capillaries.

Connect: Many alveoli create a large surface area; thin walls give a short diffusion distance; blood flow helps maintain concentration gradients.

Conclude: These structures increase the rate of gas exchange, allowing oxygen to enter the blood and carbon dioxide to leave efficiently.

High-scoring feature: the response links more than one structural feature to the process and then to the system function.

2. Villi and nutrient absorption

Observe: The small intestine has many villi with thin surfaces and a rich blood supply.

Connect: Villi greatly increase surface area and absorbed nutrients can be carried away by blood.

Conclude: The intestine can absorb digested nutrients efficiently and supply body cells with materials needed for respiration, growth and repair.

3. Xylem disruption

Observe: A plant has moist soil but blocked xylem vessels and its leaves wilt.

Connect: Water may enter roots but cannot be transported effectively to the leaves.

Conclude: Leaf cells lose water and turgor, showing that resource availability alone is not enough; the transport system must also function.

4. Hardening of arteries

Observe: An artery becomes narrower and less elastic.

Connect: The narrowed vessel resists blood flow and the heart must work harder to move blood through the system.

Conclude: Less effective circulation can reduce oxygen delivery to tissues and increase strain on the heart.

5. Dialysis as an artificial system

Observe: Dialysis removes selected wastes and excess water from blood.

Connect: This mimics part of the kidney's filtering and regulating role.

Conclude: Dialysis can support survival when kidneys fail, but it does not reproduce every kidney function continuously.

Common misconceptions: test yourself

“An organ works independently.”

False. Organs depend on other organs and systems. The heart, for example, depends on lungs for oxygen and vessels for transport.

“Every cell in an organ is the same.”

False. Organs contain multiple tissues and specialised cell types working together.

“Arteries always carry oxygenated blood.”

False. Arteries are defined by carrying blood away from the heart; pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood to the lungs.

“Xylem and phloem do the same job.”

False. Both are transport tissues, but xylem mainly carries water/mineral ions while phloem moves dissolved sugars.

“A larger organ is always more efficient.”

False. Efficiency depends on useful structural features—such as surface area, thin exchange surfaces, branching or specialised tissues—not size alone.

“An artificial organ is an exact copy.”

False. Artificial systems usually replace or support selected functions and have limitations.

10 important questions to solve
  1. Order cell, tissue, organ and organ system, and give one linked example.
  2. How does a root hair cell's shape improve absorption?
  3. Why are xylem vessels suited to water transport?
  4. How do alveoli support rapid gas exchange?
  5. Why does the left ventricle need a thicker wall than an atrium?
  6. Compare one transport feature in plants with one in animals.
  7. Predict what happens if most villi in the small intestine are damaged.
  8. Explain how hardening of arteries can affect the whole organism.
  9. What kidney function can dialysis partly replace?
  10. Why can both a 2D diagram and a 3D organ-system model be scientifically useful?
Solutions with explanations
  1. Cell → tissue → organ → organ system; e.g. cardiac muscle cell → cardiac muscle tissue → heart → circulatory system.
  2. The projection increases surface area in contact with soil, improving water and mineral uptake.
  3. Xylem forms continuous hollow, strengthened pathways that conduct water and add support.
  4. Large surface area, thin moist walls and nearby capillaries create efficient diffusion conditions.
  5. It must generate enough pressure to send blood through the systemic circulation.
  6. Plants use xylem/phloem; animals use blood in vessels driven by the heart. Both move resources, but their structures and driving mechanisms differ.
  7. Nutrient absorption falls because effective absorptive surface area is reduced.
  8. Narrow, stiff arteries impede flow, reduce effective delivery to tissues and increase cardiac workload.
  9. Removal of some wastes and excess water from blood.
  10. A 2D model may clarify pathways and labels, while a 3D model can show spatial relationships; each simplifies different aspects.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S8U02: analyse the relationship between structure and function of cells, tissues and organs in a plant and an animal organ system and explain how these systems enable survival of the individual.

  • E1 — 2D and 3D representations: compare models to understand the positions and relationships of organs.
  • E2 — analogous plant and animal systems: compare structure and function of systems that transport materials.
  • E3 — specialised cells and tissues: examine how specialised cells and tissues contribute to organ function.
  • E4 — organ contributions: describe structures of organs and relate each function to the system's overall role.
  • E5 — disorders: trace how a change in cells or tissues, such as arterial hardening, can disrupt organ and system function.
  • E6 — artificial organs: investigate how technologies such as dialysis support or mimic selected organ functions.
Teacher toolkit: 50-minute lesson sequence

0–5 min · Diagnostic entry

Students order four cards: cell, tissue, organ, system. Then answer: “What makes a structure specialised?”

Entry point: if students cannot order the levels, use one complete example before adding new systems.

5–15 min · Explicit model

Teach one chain such as cardiac muscle cell → tissue → heart → circulatory system. Model the sentence frame: feature → function → organism benefit.

15–27 min · Compare systems

Students compare xylem/phloem with arteries/capillaries using the table above or a teacher-provided diagram.

27–37 min · Model or case task

Build a simple organ-system model from clay, string, tubing or pipe cleaners. Students must label what each part represents and state one limitation.

37–45 min · Disruption challenge

Give one scenario: damaged villi, narrowed artery, blocked xylem or damaged alveoli. Students trace consequences from local structure to whole-system effect.

45–50 min · Exit ticket

“Name one structure, explain its function, then predict what would happen if it failed.” Use the rubric below for a fast check.

Differentiation

Enabler

Provide labelled diagrams, one completed structure→function example and sentence starters: “The ___ has ___, which helps it to ___.”

Core

Require students to connect structure to function and then to the system-level outcome.

Extension

Present an unfamiliar disruption or model. Require comparison, prediction and evaluation of limitations using evidence.

Formative assessment and mini-rubric

Mid-lesson checks

  • Can the student classify an example correctly as cell, tissue, organ or system?
  • Can the student explain why a structure suits its job, not just name the job?
  • Can the student trace a disruption beyond the damaged structure to a system consequence?

Hands-on and digital model activities

Physical model: transport pathway

Materials: coloured string/tubing, cards, modelling clay or reusable craft materials.

  1. Assign structures: exchange surface, transport pathway, pump/source, target tissue.
  2. Build the pathway.
  3. Label each structure and function.
  4. Remove or block one component and predict the system effect.
  5. Finish by stating one way the model differs from the real system.

Digital anatomy option

Use an interactive 3D anatomy platform to rotate, isolate and compare respiratory or cardiovascular structures. Students should record one spatial relationship that is clearer in 3D than in a flat diagram.

Ready-to-use case studies

Case 1: Narrowed arteries

Evidence: artery diameter decreases and the wall becomes less elastic.

Student task: identify the affected structure, predict the effect on flow, explain a likely consequence for tissues, and state why this can affect the whole circulatory system.

Case 2: Dialysis

Evidence: a machine removes selected wastes and excess water from blood at scheduled treatment times.

Student task: identify the kidney function being supported, explain how this helps internal conditions, and state one limitation compared with healthy kidneys.
