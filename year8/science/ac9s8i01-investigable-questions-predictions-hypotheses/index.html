YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Turn a pattern into a testable question AC9S8I01

Learning intention: Develop measurable questions, explanatory hypotheses and reasoned predictions to test models, patterns and relationships.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A model prediction is a result to test

An original hypothetical spring model gives extensions of 2, 4 and 6 mm for added masses of 10, 20 and 30 g. Extension means length minus the unloaded length. Assume this model proposes proportional extension within a stated 0–40 g elastic range. It predicts 8 mm at 40 g, but no 40 g result is supplied. A possible explanation is that greater weight stretches the elastic spring further until its restoring force balances the load. The prediction and explanation must be checked; these paper records do not establish a universal rule for springs.

Learn the skill

1. Ask a measurable, feasible question

Replace “Is this a good spring?” with “How does added mass from 10 to 40 g affect the extension of this spring after it settles?” Identify the changed quantity, measured response and conditions. Keep the same spring, unloaded reference, vertical support and measurement method. Feasibility includes suitable equipment, time, safety and ethics.

Proposed question: “Does a heavier load change the colour and extension of any spring anywhere?”
Try it · What makes the worked question more investigable?

It specifies one spring, a mass range and extension measured from a defined reference after settling. The broad question mixes outcomes and an unlimited population without a feasible method.

2. Separate hypothesis and prediction

A prediction states an expected observation. A hypothesis offers a testable explanation that generates predictions. Here, a greater load is expected to produce greater extension because an elastic spring must deform further to exert a greater balancing restoring force. The exact 8 mm prediction additionally depends on the supplied proportional model.

Statement A: “At 40 g the extension will be 8 mm.” Statement B: “Within the elastic range, a larger load stretches this spring further because a larger restoring force is needed to balance it.”
Try it · Which is the numerical prediction and which provides an explanatory hypothesis?

A is the prediction. B supplies an explanatory hypothesis. Neither is an observed result; the explanation of increasing stretch alone does not determine the exact numerical factor.

3. Test a pattern and its limits

Use a model to predict, collect suitable evidence, then compare. A 40 g reading different from 8 mm would prompt checks of the zero reference, settling and elastic-range assumption. It could require revising the model. In observational datasets, two quantities changing together may have another cause; a pattern alone does not isolate causation.

A separate observational record shows ice-cream sales and sunburn cases both rising during hot sunny weather.
Try it · Does that pattern show ice-cream causes sunburn?

No. Weather can influence both. It can generate questions, but a causal claim needs evidence that addresses alternative explanations.

Common mistakes

“A hypothesis is an unexplained guess.” Give a testable explanation and show what observations it predicts.

“8 mm is already a measured result.” It is a conditional prediction in the supplied model.

“Every pattern proves cause.” Check design, confounding factors and the scope of the data.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Design a model check on paper

Write the spring question, name the changed and measured variables and two controls, then give a reasoned 40 g prediction. State one observation that would challenge the proportional model and one check you would make before revising it.

Adjust the challenge Support: use question/prediction/hypothesis cards. Core: explain the prediction’s assumptions. Extend: write a useful observational question where changing the variable experimentally would be inappropriate.

This is a paper model task. Any real spring investigation needs teacher-approved loads, equipment and safe limits; no stretching or hanging loads is required here.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a useful hypothesis be unsupported by the results?

Yes. A careful test can reject or qualify an explanation and guide a better one; the investigation has still provided information.

2. Does doubling mass always double extension?

No. That is a feature of this stated proportional model within its assumed range, not a rule for every spring or every load.

3. Can scientists investigate without deliberately changing a variable?

Yes. Observational studies and model comparisons can test patterns where experiments are impractical or unethical, with appropriate limits on causal conclusions.

FAQ

Must a hypothesis use “if–then–because”?

No. That frame can help organise a testable explanation, but scientific meaning matters more than the sentence pattern.

Is a value question useless?

No. Values matter in decisions, but measurements alone cannot decide what a community should value.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Distinguish investigable questions from questions based mainly on values.
  • Identify independent, dependent, controlled and confounding variables.
  • Use patterns and models to make reasoned predictions.
  • Write a directional hypothesis with a scientific reason.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that observations describe what is detected, inferences interpret observations, and scientific models represent selected features or relationships.

Key concept

Use the chain pattern or model → investigable question → variables → reasoned prediction or hypothesis → evidence. An investigable question names measurable variables and conditions. Questions such as “Should we…?” or “Is this good?” involve values and cannot be answered by experimental measurements alone.

A correlation means variables change together; causation means changing one produces a change in the other. Controlled investigations help test causation, while a confounding variable provides another possible explanation.

A prediction states an expected outcome. A hypothesis proposes a testable, directional relationship and a reason: If [independent variable changes], then [dependent variable changes], because [scientific reason].

A model may be a diagram, physical object, mathematical relationship or simulation. It can generate questions and predictions even though it simplifies reality; its predictions must still be compared with evidence.

Worked examples

Correlation and causation

Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases both rise in summer, but ice-cream does not cause sunburn. Stronger sunlight and hotter weather influence both, so the pattern alone does not establish causation.

A measurable question

Replace “Why do plants hate salt?” with “How does salt concentration affect the mass of potato tissue after 30 minutes?” Salt concentration is the independent variable and mass change is the dependent variable.

Model-based question

A plate-boundary model can support the question “How does boundary type affect earthquake and volcano patterns?” The model predicts a relationship that mapped observations can test.

Reasoned prediction and hypothesis

In the supplied spring model, increasing added mass from 30 to 40 g predicts an extension increase from 6 to 8 mm. The prediction assumes the stated proportional model within 0–40 g; greater weight stretches the elastic spring further until its restoring force balances the load. If light exposure increases within suitable limits, plant growth should increase because light supports photosynthesis.

Common misconceptions
Two variables change together, so one causes the other. Correction: A third variable may explain both.
Any question is scientifically investigable. Correction: The question must be answerable with measurable evidence.
A hypothesis is just a guess. Correction: It is a testable relationship supported by scientific reasoning.
Models must copy reality exactly. Correction: Useful models simplify reality for a stated purpose.
Guided practice
  1. Underline the two measurable variables in a question.
  2. Name which variable is changed and which is measured.
  3. Identify one variable that should be controlled.
  4. Complete an if–then–because hypothesis using a model or known pattern.
Independent practice
  1. Improve the question “Is fertiliser good for plants?” so it is measurable.
  2. Explain why ice-cream sales and sunburn do not establish causation.
  3. Use the supplied spring model to predict extension at 40 g and state the model assumption and scientific reason.
  4. Identify the confounding variable if both light colour and water amount change.
  5. Develop an investigable question from a plate-boundary model.
  6. Explain why a hypothesis is tested rather than proved before an investigation.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Two proposed models match the supplied spring records up to 30 g. At 40 g, model A predicts continued proportional extension to 8 mm; model B predicts a plateau at 6 mm. Design one measurable question and reasoned hypothesis that could distinguish these predictions, then identify a confounding variable to control. Treat these as competing proposed models, not observed 40 g results.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes a question investigable? Measurable evidence could answer it under stated conditions.
  2. Prediction or hypothesis? A prediction states an expected result; a hypothesis proposes a testable relationship with reasoning.
  3. Why control variables? To reduce alternative explanations for a change in the dependent variable.
  4. Can a correlation be useful? Yes. It can reveal a pattern worth investigating, but it does not by itself prove causation.
Practice and review
  1. Turn “Do plants like salt?” into an investigable question.
    Name salt concentration, a measurable plant response and a time or method.
  2. Evaluate “More umbrellas cause more rain.”
    Identify correlation, reverse reasoning and weather as the common factor.
  3. Write a hypothesis for light exposure and plant growth.
    Use if–then–because, identify both variables and qualify suitable limits.
Check understanding
  • I write measurable questions.
  • I identify all variable roles.
  • I separate correlation from causation.
  • I justify predictions and hypotheses with science.

Exit ticket: Why can a strong correlation still fail to show causation?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Move from weak questions to measurable versions, then ask students to name variables and the scientific reason before planning a method.

Parent/carer

Use everyday patterns and ask: What changes, what can we measure, and what else might explain the pattern?

Support: use a variable sentence frame.
Core: justify a directional hypothesis.
Extend: compare causal and correlational questions without drifting into senior statistics.
