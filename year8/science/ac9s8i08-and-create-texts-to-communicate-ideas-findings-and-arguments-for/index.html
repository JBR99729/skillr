AC9S8I08 • Year 8 Science

Communicate Science for a Purpose and Audience

Strong scientific communication combines accurate evidence with an appropriate genre, register, structure and representation for the intended audience.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

Purpose + audience → genre and structure → language and representations → evidence and reasoning → accurate scientific message.

AC9S8I08 is not simply a grammar code. Students should be able to create and evaluate scientific texts that communicate findings and arguments clearly, precisely and appropriately.

Success criteria

  • Use a formal scientific register when appropriate.
  • Link claims and conclusions directly to evidence.
  • Adapt terminology and detail for different audiences without changing the scientific meaning.
  • Select graphs, diagrams, infographics, models or digital media that genuinely support the message.
  • Explain method modifications and limitations clearly.
  • Use cultural knowledge respectfully with accurate attribution and relevant protocols.
E1 — Scientific register, voice and precision

Scientific writing should be precise, objective and evidence-based. Passive voice can be useful when the procedure matters more than the researcher, for example “The pH of each sample was measured.” However, passive voice is not automatically better in every sentence.

Replace vague statements such as “the plant grew a lot” with quantitative statements such as “Plant height increased by 4.8 cm over 72 hours.”

Use appropriate hedging: suggests, indicates, may, under the tested conditions. Avoid claiming that data “prove” a universal conclusion unless the evidence truly supports that scope.

E2 — Scientific reports: evidence, assumptions and error

A strong report explains what was found and how confidently the finding can be interpreted.

  • State important assumptions.
  • Identify relevant measurement or method limitations.
  • Link the conclusion to numerical or observational evidence.
  • Use graphs and diagrams with clear labels, variables and units.
  • Distinguish consistency from accuracy.

Better conclusion

Weak: “The fertiliser worked.”

Stronger: “Plants receiving fertiliser showed a mean growth increase of 18% across three trials, suggesting the treatment improved growth under the tested conditions.”

E3 — Explain method modifications

When a method changes, explain what problem the change addresses.

  • Video timing → reduces human reaction-time effects.
  • Identical light sources → controls a possible confounding variable.
  • Larger sample size → provides better information about variation and reliability.
  • Same chemical batch → reduces concentration differences between trials.

A method is not better merely because it uses newer technology.

E4 — Persuasive scientific texts

Scientific persuasion should remain scientific. It should not rely on exaggerated claims or emotional language alone.

For a topic such as artificial organs, a strong argument might use:

  1. a specific claim
  2. relevant clinical or performance evidence
  3. reasoning linking the evidence to the claim
  4. limitations or risks
  5. a qualified recommendation or conclusion.

The detail and terminology should suit the intended audience, such as policymakers, medical professionals or the general public.

E5 — Documentary language, models and analogies

Documentaries often use active voice, visuals and analogies to maintain engagement. These features must still preserve scientific accuracy.

For example, tectonic plates may be compared with slowly shifting puzzle pieces. This helps visualise boundaries and movement, but the communicator should explain that real plates deform, interact in three dimensions and are not rigid puzzle pieces on a flat surface.

Analogy = useful similarity + stated limitation.

E6 — Digital infographics

Effective scientific infographics use:

  • concise text
  • relevant icons or diagrams
  • clear labels and consistent units
  • visual hierarchy
  • arrows or layout that show relationships, transfers or transformations.

Decorative visuals should not compete with or distort the scientific message.

E7 — First Nations communication protocols

When communicating First Nations Australian knowledges, students should:

  • acknowledge the cultural source appropriately
  • retain relevant cultural context
  • represent the knowledge accurately rather than modifying it to fit a preferred explanation
  • follow any relevant permissions or restrictions on sharing.

First Nations knowledges are diverse and place-based; avoid presenting them as one uniform system.

Choosing sources and evidence

Do not use simplistic rules such as “peer reviewed = always correct” or “industry = always unreliable”. Compare:

  • sample size
  • method quality
  • relevance
  • transparency
  • possible bias or conflicts of interest
  • agreement with other credible evidence.

A small peer-reviewed study and a large industry report can each have strengths and limitations.

Common mistakes
  • Using passive voice everywhere simply to sound scientific.
  • Using technical vocabulary that the audience cannot understand.
  • Using “proves”, “always” or “never” when the evidence is limited.
  • Including a graph or image that does not support the message.
  • Making a persuasive claim without explaining the evidence-to-claim link.
  • Changing scientific meaning when simplifying for a younger audience.
  • Using cultural knowledge without context, attribution or appropriate permission.
Assessment strategy
  1. Purpose: What is this text trying to do?
  2. Audience: Who needs to understand it?
  3. Evidence: What data or sources support the message?
  4. Register: Is the language precise, objective and appropriately qualified?
  5. Representation: Do visuals or models improve understanding?
  6. Accuracy: Has simplification preserved the scientific meaning?
  7. Responsibility: Are sources and cultural knowledge represented appropriately?
