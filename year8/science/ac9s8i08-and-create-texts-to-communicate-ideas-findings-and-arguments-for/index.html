YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Tell the same findings to two audiences AC9S8I08

Learning intention: Create scientific texts for a clear purpose and audience, using suitable language, representations and digital tools while preserving evidence and uncertainty.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One dataset, a report and a family notice

An original hypothetical paper-bridge report records three independently made flat bridges holding 80, 100 and 90 g, and three folded bridges holding 140, 150 and 160 g. All use the same paper type, dimensions and amount, are dry, span 15 cm and are tested with 10 g load increments. “Held” means the largest tested mass supported for 10 seconds. Means are 90 and 150 g, a 60 g difference. A peer report needs method and variation; a family notice needs a clear finding and accessible explanation. Neither should say folding makes every bridge unbreakable.

Peer report model: “Three independently made bridges per design were tested across a 15 cm span in 10 g load increments. Mean largest supported mass was 90 g for flat and 150 g for folded bridges; each group’s range was 20 g. Folding was associated with greater supported mass in this small test. Only these paper, span and loading conditions were examined.”
Family notice model: “In our fictional paper-bridge study, folded bridges held a higher average load: 150 g compared with 90 g for flat bridges. We used the same paper amount and a 15 cm gap. Only three bridges of each design were tested, so this does not tell us how every paper bridge will behave.”

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Choose purpose, audience and form

A report helps peers examine and reproduce a method; a notice shares an accessible finding; a persuasive proposal supports a specified decision with evidence and trade-offs. Choose detail, vocabulary and structure for the purpose. Audience adaptation can change expression but must keep values, units, evidence strength and limitations accurate.

A peer wants to repeat the bridge comparison. A family reader wants to understand the main finding.
Try it · Which extra detail belongs in the peer report, and which finding must both keep?

The report needs the design specification, span, independent sample, loading steps and endpoint. Both must preserve the 90 g and 150 g means, small sample and tested-condition limits. Do not invent missing design dimensions: request them before actual replication.

2. Use language and text features precisely

Results describe observations; discussion interprets them and explains limitations. Use labelled tables or figures, units and a reference in the prose. Active voice can identify responsibility; passive voice can focus on process. Choose for clarity. “Supported 150 g on average” is more precise than “was amazingly strong”.

Sentence A: “The folded mean was 150 g.” Sentence B: “Folding may have changed how the load was supported, but the mechanism was not isolated by these results.”
Try it · Which belongs mainly in results and which in discussion?

A reports a calculated result. B interprets it and states a limitation. If a diagram explains load distribution, label it as a proposed model rather than direct measurement of the mechanism.

3. Create, check and revise an accessible text

Draft a short text, check it against raw records and revise after specific feedback. A spreadsheet can calculate a mean or plot a comparison, but audit references, axes and units. Add a meaningful caption and text alternative; avoid colour-only distinctions. Attribute supplied or external evidence and images and check permission before sharing.

Draft poster: “Folded bridges are unbreakable! 150 versus 90.” Feedback: missing units, scope, source and an accessible explanation.
Try it · Write a corrected caption.

“In this hypothetical SkillrHub dataset, three folded bridges averaged 150 g and three flat bridges 90 g for the largest mass held for 10 seconds across 15 cm. Results apply to the tested paper and loading conditions.” Include the raw table or a text alternative and label the two designs directly.

Common mistakes

“Simplifying means dropping limitations.” Use clear words while preserving what the evidence can and cannot establish.

“Passive voice is always more scientific.” Choose active or passive voice according to clarity and purpose.

“A polished graphic checks itself.” Verify numbers, labels, units, attribution and accessibility.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write, exchange and revise two texts

Write a four-sentence peer summary and a two-sentence family notice from the supplied records. Add a labelled figure or table caption. Ask a partner to check evidence, units, purpose, limitations and source label; revise one specific weakness and explain the change.

Adjust the challenge Support: highlight facts both texts must retain. Core: distinguish results from discussion. Extend: propose a revised method addressing one limitation and explain why it would improve the evidence.

Use the fictional dataset and paper drafting. No loading experiment, physical bridge construction or public publication is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Are three loads applied to one bridge three independent bridges?

No. The supplied dataset uses three separately made bridges per design. Repeated loading on one bridge can affect it and does not create independent specimens.

2. Can the mean difference of 60 g be called 60% stronger?

No. Grams and percent are different quantities, and “stronger” needs an operational definition. Report the 60 g difference in mean largest supported mass under these conditions.

3. Does “our study” prove that the student performed the practical?

No. The family notice is an explicitly fictional mentor text. When using supplied records, identify them; only describe work as personally performed when it actually was.

FAQ

Which digital tool should I use?

Choose one that serves the purpose, such as a checked spreadsheet, word processor or slide tool. A clear paper text can also meet the communication goal.

What source label is suitable for these numbers?

Identify them as the original hypothetical SkillrHub dataset on this topic page. External material needs its own creator, title, date/source and any required permission.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Select genre, register and detail for purpose and audience.
  • Link claims and conclusions directly to evidence.
  • Use visuals, models and digital tools that clarify rather than decorate.
  • Preserve attribution, context and cultural protocols.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall claim–evidence–reasoning, graph conventions, source credibility, uncertainty and the limits of conclusions.

Key concept

Use purpose + audience → genre and structure → scientific register → evidence and reasoning → representation → accurate message.

A formal scientific register is precise and appropriately qualified. Passive voice can focus on a method—“The pH was measured”—but should not be used everywhere. Replace “grew a lot” with “increased by 4.8 cm over 72 hours”.

Hedging words such as suggests, indicates, may and under the tested conditions keep claims within evidence. A report can state that fertilised plants showed a mean growth increase of 18% across three trials rather than saying fertiliser always works.

Explain why methods changed: video timing can reduce reaction-time effects and a larger sample size can reveal variation. Newer technology is not automatically better.

Source labels are not verdicts: peer review does not guarantee perfection, and industry evidence is not automatically unreliable. Compare sample size, method quality, relevance, transparency, possible bias and independent support.

Worked examples

Scientific report

State assumptions, methods, numerical findings, limitations and a qualified conclusion. Label graphs with variables and units and distinguish consistency from accuracy.

Persuasive scientific text

An argument about artificial organs needs a precise claim, clinical or performance evidence, reasoning, risks and a qualified recommendation—not emotional language alone.

Documentary analogy

Comparing tectonic plates with puzzle pieces can show boundaries, but explain that real plates deform and interact in three dimensions.

Digital infographic and protocols

An infographic needs concise text, labelled visuals, consistent units and hierarchy. First Nations knowledge requires accurate attribution, context and relevant permissions.

Common misconceptions
Passive voice always sounds more scientific. Correction: Choose voice for clarity and purpose.
Technical vocabulary suits every audience. Correction: Adapt language without changing meaning.
A graph automatically improves a text. Correction: It must support the message with clear labels and honest scale.
Public cultural knowledge can be reused freely. Correction: Attribution, context and permissions still apply.
Guided practice
  1. Identify the text purpose and audience.
  2. Underline the claim and supporting evidence.
  3. Replace vague or absolute language with precise qualification.
  4. Choose a visual and explain how it adds meaning.
Independent practice
  1. Rewrite “the plant grew a lot” using 4.8 cm over 72 hours.
  2. Explain when passive voice helps a method description.
  3. Write a qualified conclusion using the 18% fertiliser result.
  4. Explain why video timing or a larger sample improves a method.
  5. State the use and limit of the tectonic-plate puzzle analogy.
  6. Plan an infographic that preserves units, uncertainty, sources and cultural protocols.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Use the supplied hypothetical bridge dataset to create two short explanations: one for Year 6 students and one for laboratory staff. Change vocabulary and detail while keeping values, units, uncertainty and conclusion identical.

Questions and answers
  1. What can change for an audience? Vocabulary, detail, structure, genre and medium.
  2. What must remain accurate? Evidence, meaning, units, uncertainty and attribution.
  3. Why qualify conclusions? To keep the message within the tested evidence.
  4. What makes a visual useful? It clarifies a relationship or finding without distorting it.
Practice and review
  1. Improve “The fertiliser proved plants always grow faster”.
    Use the 18% result, three trials and tested-condition qualification.
  2. Evaluate a documentary analogy for plates.
    Explain its useful similarity and stated limitation.
  3. Plan communication of First Nations knowledge.
    Identify appropriate source, attribution, context and permission before sharing.
Check understanding
  • I adapt texts for audience and purpose.
  • I link claims to evidence.
  • I use precise, qualified language.
  • I select responsible visuals and sources.

Exit ticket: What may change for an audience, and what scientific meaning must not change?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Have students adapt one dataset into a report paragraph, public infographic and short argument while preserving the same evidence.

Parent/carer

Ask the student to explain the same finding to a younger child and an expert without changing the result.

Support: use a purpose–audience checklist.
Core: produce an evidence-linked text.
Extend: compare how genre changes expression while preserving meaning.
