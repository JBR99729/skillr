Learn the skill

1. Read relative motion

Divergent plates move apart; new oceanic crust can form at spreading ridges. At convergence, oceanic lithosphere may descend at a trench, or colliding continental crust can thicken into mountains. Transform plates slide laterally past one another. The mantle is mainly solid rock that can deform slowly, not a global ocean of magma.

Case A: oceanic lithosphere descends beneath another plate. Case B: two continental masses collide and thicken.
Try it · Why should both cases not be labelled “volcanoes must form”?

Subduction can be associated with melting and volcanic arcs. Continental collision mainly thickens crust and builds mountains; convergence does not guarantee volcanism.

2. Connect motion and features

Relative movement stresses rock. Sudden fault slip can cause earthquakes. Subduction produces trenches and earthquake patterns; divergence creates ridges or rifts. Transform motion can offset features without creating or destroying crust at the boundary.

A hypothetical boundary has opposed sideways motion, offset roads and earthquakes, with no evidence of crust being created.
Try it · Which boundary model best fits, and why?

Transform: the observations fit lateral slip and offset rather than separation with new crust or convergence with consumption of crust.

3. Test with several evidence lines

Seafloor ages, magnetic patterns, earthquake distributions and present-day geodetic measurements test different aspects of plate motion. Fossil and rock matches also supported earlier continental-drift ideas. A paper model that creates symmetry shows a proposed mechanism; real field records are needed to test it.

Supplied model: at 40 km either side of the ridge the crust age is 2 million years; at 80 km it is 4 million years.
Try it · What pattern supports spreading, and what cannot this tiny table establish?

Age increases with distance on both sides, consistent with spreading. The small idealised dataset cannot establish every boundary’s history or prove all proposed driving mechanisms.

Common mistakes

“The continents move over a liquid ocean.” Plates are pieces of lithosphere moving over a mainly solid, slowly deforming mantle.

“Every boundary makes volcanoes.” Boundary type and local conditions matter; many transform and collision settings are not volcanic.

“One matching outline proves the theory.” A strong explanation must fit multiple independent kinds of evidence.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make three movement models

Use two paper strips to show separation, convergence and sideways slip. Draw relative-motion arrows and one plausible feature for each. Add a separate continental-collision case and explain one limit of the paper model.

Adjust the challenge Support: match motion to feature cards. Core: explain a mechanism. Extend: identify a new observation that could challenge one proposed boundary interpretation.

Use tabletop paper models. Do not visit unstable ground, cliffs or active geological sites for this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is a magnetic stripe a differently coloured strip of ocean-floor rock?

No. Colour on a map codes measured magnetic properties or polarity; it is a representation, not necessarily a visible rock colour.

2. A single sample does not fit the age pattern. Should it be deleted?

No. Check its location, dating, sampling and uncertainty, consider local complexity, and compare further evidence before deciding how it affects the explanation.

3. Does the paper model show the real speed or force of plate motion?

No. Its scale, materials and movement mechanism differ. It represents relative motion and needs field evidence to test its geological interpretation.

FAQ

Are all earthquakes at plate boundaries?

No. Many occur near boundaries, but earthquakes can also occur within plates.

Why use different evidence types?

They test different predictions and can expose limitations that one dataset alone would miss.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify divergent, convergent and transform boundaries.
  • Explain how boundary motion forms ridges, trenches, volcanoes, mountains and faults.
  • Use fossil, seafloor, magnetic, earthquake and GPS evidence for plate tectonics.
  • Explain hazards and why risk reduction depends on exposure and engineering as well as plate processes.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall Earth’s crust, mantle and surface features, that evidence can support or challenge a scientific model, and that earthquakes release stored energy when rocks move suddenly along faults.

Key concept

At a divergent boundary plates move apart; magma rises and new crust can form at mid-ocean ridges or continental rifts. At a convergent boundary plates move together; subduction can form trenches, deepening earthquake zones and volcanic arcs, while continent–continent collision can uplift fold mountains. At a transform boundary plates slide past, producing faults and frequent shallow earthquakes.

Plate motion is driven by interacting processes. Slab pull acts where cold dense oceanic lithosphere sinks; ridge push is gravity-assisted movement away from elevated ridges; mantle movement also interacts with plates. Avoid the oversimplification that plates float on a global ocean of liquid magma.

The theory is supported by converging evidence: Wegener’s continental-fit/fossil/rock/climate evidence, Marie Tharp’s seafloor maps, symmetric magnetic stripes and seafloor ages, earthquake/volcano belts, and modern GPS measurements.

Worked examples

Subduction evidence

An offshore trench, earthquakes that become deeper inland and a continental volcanic arc form a linked pattern consistent with an oceanic plate subducting beneath a continent.

Seafloor spreading

Youngest ocean-floor rocks at a ridge plus symmetrical age and magnetic patterns on both sides support formation of new crust and outward movement.

Transform hazard

Repeated shallow earthquakes along a strike-slip fault are explained by friction locking the fault, stress building and sudden slip releasing seismic energy.

Common misconceptions
Plates float on liquid magma. Correction: lithospheric plates move over a weaker, slowly deforming asthenosphere.
All convergent boundaries make volcanoes. Correction: continent–continent collision mainly thickens and uplifts crust.
Convection alone pushes every plate. Correction: slab pull, ridge push and mantle interactions vary in importance.
Earthquakes only happen at plate boundaries. Correction: most cluster there, but intraplate earthquakes also occur.
Guided practice
  1. Match ridge, trench and transform fault to boundary type.
  2. Explain why earthquake depth can increase inland from a subduction trench.
  3. Use a seafloor-age pattern to infer a ridge location.
  4. Explain how GPS provides a different kind of evidence from fossils.
Independent practice
  1. Describe one feature formed at each boundary type.
  2. Explain slab pull and ridge push.
  3. Explain Marie Tharp’s contribution to evidence for plate tectonics.
  4. Explain why symmetrical magnetic stripes support seafloor spreading.
  5. Compare an earthquake hazard at a transform boundary with one at a subduction boundary.
  6. Explain why strong buildings can reduce risk without changing the tectonic hazard itself.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A region has a deep trench, a line of volcanoes inland, earthquakes ranging from shallow near the trench to deep farther inland, and GPS vectors pointing toward the boundary. Construct an evidence chain identifying the boundary and explaining each observation.

Questions and answers
  1. What distinguishes the three boundaries? Relative plate motion: apart, together or sliding past.
  2. What evidence supports seafloor spreading? Ridge topography, seafloor age and symmetric magnetic patterns.
  3. What did Marie Tharp contribute? Detailed ocean-floor mapping that revealed major ridges, rift valleys and fracture zones.
  4. Why is GPS important? It directly measures current plate motion, adding modern quantitative evidence.
Practice and review
  1. Explain how an oceanic–continental convergent boundary forms a trench and volcanic arc.
    Start with density/subduction, then connect processes to both features.
  2. Use two independent lines of evidence to support plate tectonics.
    Choose evidence that measures different things, such as magnetic stripes and GPS.
  3. A city lies near a transform fault. Explain why engineering can reduce risk but not eliminate the tectonic hazard.
    Separate natural process from vulnerability/exposure.
Check understanding
  • I identify boundary types from motion and evidence.
  • I explain features rather than only naming them.
  • I use multiple evidence lines.
  • I separate hazard from risk reduction.

Exit ticket: Why is a pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes more useful than one isolated event when testing plate tectonic theory?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Use maps and cross-sections together. Require students to state plate motion before naming features, and preserve the evidence-history sequence from Wegener to Marie Tharp to magnetic stripes and GPS.

Parent/carer

Ask the student to identify a boundary from three clues rather than from a memorised diagram.

Support: use labelled boundary cross-sections and motion arrows.
Core: explain features and interpret evidence maps/cross-sections.
Extend: compare competing plate-driving mechanisms or evaluate hazard-reduction choices without senior geophysics.
