Read a rock’s history and test its use AC9S8U04

Learning intention: Trace alternative rock-cycle pathways, compare process timescales, and justify a rock use from formation and tested properties.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A useful building stone needs more than a name

In this hypothetical matched abrasion test, equal-mass samples receive the same treatment: A loses 1 g and B loses 6 g. A is the better candidate for resisting that tested wear. Its interlocking coarse crystals are consistent with relatively slow cooling of an igneous rock, but crystal size alone does not establish path suitability. Strength, slip resistance, exposure, availability and other tests still matter. A polished carbonate marble may look attractive yet be a poor choice for repeated acidic exposure.

Learn the skill

1. Trace processes, not a compulsory lap

Melt cools and crystallises into igneous rock. Weathering breaks down rock; erosion and transport move material; deposition leaves it elsewhere. Compaction and cementation can turn sediment into sedimentary rock. Heat and pressure can change solid rock into metamorphic rock without bulk melting.

Start with granite. Route 1: weathering → transported sediment → deposition → compaction/cementation. Route 2: heat and pressure without melting.
Try it · What rock class can each route produce?

Route 1 can produce sedimentary rock; route 2 can produce metamorphic rock. If the granite melts and the melt later solidifies, the product is igneous instead.

2. Compare timescales using conditions

A fragment may be transported in seconds during a flow, while repeated deposition builds a sequence over a long period. Thick lava can remain hot internally long after its surface crust forms. Rates depend on thickness, material and conditions; no single clock times a whole rock cycle.

Supplied observations: a grain moves across a tray in 5 seconds; a sediment sequence accumulates through many separate deposition events.
Try it · Does the short transport time prove the sedimentary rock formed in 5 seconds?

No. Transport is one event; accumulation and later rock-forming processes have their own durations. The given observation does not date the finished rock.

3. Link formation, properties and purpose

Interlocking crystals, cemented grains and solid-state recrystallisation can help explain observed texture. Use a provided identification key alongside observations; texture is evidence, not an infallible label. Suitability depends on a measured property and the demands of a particular use.

Hypothetical same-treatment wear test: A loses 1 g; B loses 6 g. Both started with equal mass.
Try it · What bounded conclusion can you make?

A resisted this abrasion treatment better. You cannot conclude that every igneous sample is stronger than every sedimentary sample, or that A meets every requirement for a public path.

Common mistakes

“Metamorphic means melted.” Metamorphism changes rock in the solid state; melting followed by solidification produces igneous rock.

“Every rock follows the same route.” The process network branches; a rock may weather, be buried, change or melt by different paths.

“A shiny finish proves durability.” Appearance and resistance to wear or chemical exposure are different properties.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make two histories for one starting rock

Draw two possible pathways starting with igneous rock. Name each process and resulting material. Add a use decision supported by the supplied abrasion test, plus one further property you would test.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort process cards. Core: explain a branching pathway. Extend: explain how a fossil or mineral texture supports a formation inference while leaving uncertainty.

Use photographs, drawings or teacher-selected loose samples. No quarrying, chipping, acid testing, heating or collecting from culturally significant places is part of this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Must a sedimentary rock show obvious layers?

No. Some have visible layers, but this is not universal. Use several properties and a suitable key.

2. Must all metamorphic rocks have bands?

No. Some, such as marble, can be unbanded. Texture depends on original material and metamorphic conditions.

3. A cooled lava surface can be walked on safely because it is solid. Is that conclusion justified?

No. A solid surface can cover a very hot interior, and other hazards may remain. A classroom diagram cannot establish site safety.

FAQ

Does weathering always take longer than erosion?

No. Both vary with conditions; distinguish an individual event from long-term accumulated change.

Can rock use affect the environment?

Yes. Extraction, transport and rehabilitation have environmental and social consequences as well as material benefits.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Describe weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction, cementation, heat, pressure, melting, crystallisation and uplift.
  • Use evidence to distinguish igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks.
  • Explain how process rate and cooling conditions affect rock features.
  • Connect rock properties to practical use, quarrying/mining choices and rehabilitation.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that rocks are made of minerals, erosion transports material, heat and pressure can change materials, and evidence such as fossils or crystal texture can be used to infer past conditions.

Key concept

The rock cycle is a network, not one compulsory circle. Surface weathering breaks rock down; erosion transports it; deposition accumulates sediment; burial, compaction and cementation can form sedimentary rock. Heat and pressure can change solid rock into metamorphic rock without melting the material being metamorphosed. A melt that cools and crystallises produces igneous rock; the melt can come from partial melting of a source rock.

Rock properties record formation. Slow underground cooling can produce larger igneous crystals, while rapid surface cooling produces smaller crystals or glassy textures. Layers, grains and fossils can support a sedimentary interpretation; foliation and recrystallisation can support metamorphism.

Properties influence use. Hardness, toughness, grain size, workability and durability affect whether stone suits construction, grinding or cutting. First Nations quarry and tool-stone knowledge should be taught as purposeful material selection grounded in knowledge of Country. Mining and rehabilitation decisions also involve erosion, habitat and water impacts.

Worked examples

Fossil-bearing layers

Clear layers plus shell fossils suggest deposited sediment that was buried and lithified rather than melted, supporting a sedimentary interpretation.

Crystal-size evidence

Large interlocking crystals suggest slower cooling that allowed crystals more time to grow; a fine-grained igneous rock suggests faster cooling.

Rehabilitation reasoning

After vegetation and soil are removed, a strong plan stabilises slopes, manages drainage, replaces suitable soil and re-establishes vegetation rather than only improving appearance.

Common misconceptions
Colour alone identifies a rock. Correction: texture, layers, grains, fossils and foliation are stronger evidence.
Metamorphic rock is melted rock. Correction: metamorphism occurs under heat and pressure without melting the material being metamorphosed.
Every rock follows the same cycle path. Correction: rocks can enter many pathways or remain stable for long periods.
Mining impact ends when extraction stops. Correction: disturbed land and water systems may require long-term rehabilitation and monitoring.
Guided practice
  1. Classify three samples from texture/layer/fossil evidence.
  2. Order weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation.
  3. Explain why cooling rate affects igneous crystal size.
  4. Link one rock property to a suitable use.
Independent practice
  1. Explain why fossils are commonly associated with sedimentary rocks.
  2. Distinguish weathering from erosion.
  3. Explain how sedimentary rock can become metamorphic without melting.
  4. Explain how metamorphic rock can later become igneous.
  5. Choose a rock property important for a grindstone or cutting tool and justify it.
  6. Describe two environmental risks of a quarry or mine and a rehabilitation response for each.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A rock sample is dark, fine-grained and hard; another is layered and contains fossils; a third has wavy mineral bands. Build a formation explanation for each and state what evidence would make your classification more reliable.

Questions and answers
  1. What separates metamorphic from igneous formation? Metamorphism changes rock in solid form; igneous rock forms when a melt cools and crystallises, even when only part of the source rock melted.
  2. Why can large crystals indicate slow cooling? Crystals have more time to grow before the melt solidifies.
  3. Why is the rock cycle not one circle? Multiple processes and pathways can connect the three rock groups.
  4. Why do properties matter for use? A material must withstand the forces, wear or processing required by its application.
Practice and review
  1. A rock has layers and fossils. Classify it and explain the likely formation process.
    Use observed evidence before naming the rock group.
  2. Compare intrusive and extrusive igneous textures using cooling rate.
    Link time for crystal growth to crystal size.
  3. Evaluate a mine-rehabilitation plan that only plants grass over exposed spoil.
    Consider slope stability, drainage, soil quality and ecosystem recovery.
Check understanding
  • I describe rock-cycle processes accurately.
  • I infer formation from evidence.
  • I connect properties to use.
  • I explain why timescales vary.

Exit ticket: Why does the statement “metamorphic rock is rock that melted” break the rock-cycle model?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Use real samples or high-quality images and require observation before classification. Preserve First Nations quarry/tool-stone contexts as material-property knowledge, not as decorative examples.

Parent/carer

Ask the student to choose a building stone and explain why its properties suit the job.

Support: use a process word bank and rock-evidence table.
Core: classify from evidence, explain timescales and connect property to use.
Extend: compare alternate rock-cycle pathways or evaluate rehabilitation trade-offs without senior mineralogy.
