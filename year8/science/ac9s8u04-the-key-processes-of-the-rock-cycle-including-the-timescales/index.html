Rocks Record Their Formation

Rock properties are evidence. Texture, layers, crystals, fossils and foliation can reveal how a rock formed, how long processes may have taken, and why the rock is useful.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

In this topic, students use observable evidence to classify rocks and explain rock-cycle processes. The strongest answers connect observable property → formation process → timescale or use.

Success criteria

  • Use visible properties and a dichotomous key to identify igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks.
  • Describe weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction, cementation, crystallisation, melting, uplift, heat and pressure.
  • Explain why intrusive and extrusive igneous rocks can have different crystal sizes.
  • Use fossils as evidence for how and where sedimentary rocks formed.
  • Connect rock properties to practical uses, including tool stone and construction materials.
  • Explain environmental impacts of mining and how rehabilitation can reduce harm.
Important notes

Do not use colour alone

Colour can help describe a sample, but crystal texture, layers, grain size, foliation and fossils are usually stronger evidence.

Melting is the dividing line

Metamorphic rocks form under heat and pressure without fully melting. Once a rock melts and later crystallises, the new rock is igneous.

The cycle is not one fixed circle

A rock can move through many pathways. It may weather, melt, be buried, uplifted or remain stable for long periods.

Use is linked to property

Granite, basalt, sandstone, marble and tool stones are useful for different reasons: hardness, durability, abrasiveness, workability or polish.

Worked examples: Observe → Connect → Conclude

1. Identify a rock from evidence

Observe: The sample has clear layers and shell fossils.

Connect: Layers suggest sediment was deposited over time, and fossils are most commonly preserved in sedimentary environments.

Conclude: The rock is most likely sedimentary, and it probably formed where remains could be buried by sediment rather than melted or strongly metamorphosed.

2. Explain crystal size

Observe: One igneous rock has large visible crystals; another has very small crystals.

Connect: Large crystals usually grow when magma cools slowly underground. Small crystals form when lava cools quickly at the surface.

Conclude: The large-crystal sample is likely intrusive igneous, while the fine-crystal sample is likely extrusive igneous.

3. Predict the next rock-cycle step

Observe: Sedimentary rock is buried deeply during mountain building but does not melt.

Connect: Deep burial can increase heat and pressure. If melting does not occur, the original rock can be changed in solid form.

Conclude: The rock may become metamorphic, not igneous, because the key process is heat and pressure without complete melting.

4. Link rock properties to use

Observe: Basalt is dense, hard and durable.

Connect: Road base needs rock that can resist crushing and wear.

Conclude: Basalt can be useful for road base because its properties suit the job. The explanation uses properties, not just the rock name.

5. Evaluate mine rehabilitation

Observe: Mining removed vegetation and exposed soil on a slope.

Connect: Exposed soil can erode and affect waterways. Rehabilitation should stabilise landforms, manage drainage, replace suitable soil and re-establish vegetation.

Conclude: A strong rehabilitation plan addresses both land stability and ecosystem recovery, not only the appearance of the site.

First Nations geological knowledge and quarrying

Teach this respectfully as knowledge of Country, material properties and purpose. Avoid reducing rock selection to “whatever was nearby”. Many tools require stone with suitable hardness, toughness, grain size or abrasiveness.

Grindstones

Require surfaces that can grind seeds or pigments without crumbling too easily.

Hammerstones and anvils

Need toughness so they can withstand repeated impacts.

Cutting tools and axe heads

Often require hard, fine-grained stone that can hold an edge.

Quarry evidence

Repeated extraction of particular stone types can show deliberate selection based on properties and cultural knowledge.

Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S8U04: describe the key processes of the rock cycle, including the timescales over which they occur, and examine how the properties of sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic rocks reflect their formation and influence their use.

  • E1 — Dichotomous keys: classify unknown samples from observations such as layers, crystals, fossils, grain size and foliation.
  • E2 — Rock-cycle processes: trace a sediment grain from weathering and erosion to deposition, burial, compaction and cementation.
  • E3 — Heat, forces and timescales: compare slow underground cooling, rapid surface cooling, regional metamorphism and shorter weathering/erosion events.
  • E4 — Fossil evidence: use body, trace or opalised fossils to infer whether a rock formed in a sedimentary setting and what past environment it may record.
  • E5 — Rock uses: justify uses of granite, basalt, sandstone or marble by connecting properties to formation and performance.
  • E6 — First Nations knowledges: explore how rock properties are recognised and selected for particular cultural and practical purposes.
  • E7 — Quarrying: examine quarry evidence and tool-stone selection for grindstones, hammerstones, anvils, cutting tools or axe heads.
  • E8 — Mining and rehabilitation: evaluate impacts such as vegetation removal, erosion and water effects, then propose rehabilitation actions.
Common misconceptions: test yourself

“The rock cycle is one fixed circle.”

False. It is a network of possible pathways. Rocks do not all pass through every stage.

“Metamorphic rocks have melted.”

False. Metamorphic rocks change under heat and pressure without fully melting.

“Colour is enough to identify a rock.”

False. Colour is weak evidence by itself. Texture, layers, fossils and crystal structure matter more.

“Fossils are common in igneous rocks.”

False. Fossils are most commonly preserved in sedimentary rocks because heat and melting usually destroy remains.

“Mining rehabilitation just means planting trees.”

False. Good rehabilitation also considers landform stability, soil, drainage, water quality and monitoring.

10 important questions to solve
  1. Which visible evidence helps distinguish igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks?
  2. Why are fossils most often found in sedimentary rocks?
  3. Explain the difference between weathering and erosion.
  4. Why do intrusive igneous rocks often have larger crystals?
  5. Why does metamorphic rock formation require heat and pressure but not complete melting?
  6. Use a data table to decide which environment has the fastest sediment deposition.
  7. Explain why sedimentary rock formation can be slow.
  8. Connect one rock property to one practical use.
  9. How can quarry evidence show purposeful selection of rock types?
  10. Name two environmental impacts of mining and one rehabilitation strategy.
Solutions with explanations
  1. Igneous: interlocking crystals/glassy; sedimentary: layers, grains or fossils; metamorphic: foliation or recrystallised texture.
  2. Sediments can bury remains in low-heat conditions; melting or strong metamorphism usually destroys fossils.
  3. Weathering breaks rock down; erosion transports the broken material.
  4. Slow underground cooling gives crystals more time to grow.
  5. Heat and pressure change minerals and texture in solid rock; complete melting would later form igneous rock.
  6. Choose the largest rate in the table, then state what the unit means.
  7. Deposition is not enough; burial, compaction and cementation take time.
  8. For example, basalt is dense and strong, so it can suit road base.
  9. Repeated extraction of suitable stone shows knowledge of properties and purpose.
  10. Mining can remove vegetation/soil and affect waterways; rehabilitation can reshape land, manage drainage, replace soil and revegetate.
