How Science Communication Shapes Viewpoints and Decisions

Scientific evidence has greater social value when people can understand it. Effective communication can influence individual choices, emergency responses, public debate, policies and regulations.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core concept

Scientific evidence → communication for an audience → understanding and trust → viewpoint or behaviour → possible policy or regulation.

Communication does not make evidence true and does not guarantee agreement. Its role is to make scientific information understandable, accurate and useful for decisions.

Success criteria

  • Identify the audience, purpose and evidence in a science message.
  • Explain how wording, visuals, stories and data may influence understanding.
  • Evaluate accuracy, uncertainty, source credibility and possible exaggeration.
  • Explain how science communication can inform behaviour, policy and regulation without directly determining them.
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1 — Organ donation campaigns

Campaigns can explain donation, address misconceptions, present evidence and encourage family discussions. Personal stories may make an issue relatable, while verified statistics and medical information support informed decisions. Strong campaigns respect consent and avoid claiming that communication guarantees registration.

E2 — Seismic data and tsunami alerts

Monitoring agencies across the Asia-Pacific collect and share seismic and sea-level data. Governments then translate risk assessments into warnings. Effective alerts must be timely, clear, accessible and actionable. Multilingual messages, maps and evacuation instructions can improve reach. Because predictions contain uncertainty, transparent explanation is important for maintaining trust.

E3 — Promoting biodegradable materials

Public information can influence how people view packaging and waste. Accurate messages should explain both benefits and conditions. For example, some compostable materials break down rapidly only in industrial composting facilities. Disposal instructions matter because incorrect sorting can prevent the intended environmental benefit.

E4 — Science organisations and communicators

Science organisations and communicators such as Professor Lisa Harvey-Smith or Dr Karl Kruszelnicki can make complex ideas accessible to broad audiences. Analogies, stories and visual models can improve understanding, but simplification should preserve scientific meaning and acknowledge important limitations. Fame alone is not evidence: claims should still be checked against relevant expertise and reliable sources.

What makes science communication effective?
  • Accurate: the message matches the evidence.
  • Audience-appropriate: vocabulary and detail suit the people receiving it.
  • Clear: the main idea or required action is easy to identify.
  • Transparent: important uncertainty and limitations are not hidden.
  • Credible: claims are linked to relevant evidence and trustworthy expertise.
  • Accessible: language, visuals and formats allow different community members to understand the information.
Worked example — tsunami warning

Evidence: Seismic and sea-level instruments indicate a possible tsunami.

Poor message: “An anomalous hydrodynamic event may occur following seismic displacement.”

Better public message: “Tsunami threat: people in the marked coastal evacuation zone should move to higher ground now. Monitoring continues and the warning will be updated as new data arrive.”

Why better? It preserves the scientific uncertainty but gives the audience clear, location-specific action.

How communication can influence policy

Science communication may help decision-makers and communities understand evidence about risks, benefits and possible responses. This can influence public support, emergency procedures or regulations. However, policy decisions can also involve economic, ethical and social considerations.

Important distinction: evidence informs a policy; communication helps people understand the evidence; neither automatically produces one compulsory policy choice.

Common misconceptions
  • “If a campaign is effective, everyone will change behaviour.” Communication can influence behaviour but cannot guarantee it.
  • “Simpler language means less accurate science.” Good communication can simplify vocabulary while preserving meaning.
  • “Uncertainty should be hidden so people trust the message.” Transparent uncertainty can strengthen credibility.
  • “A famous communicator is automatically correct.” Claims still require relevant evidence and expertise.
  • “Science communication makes policy.” It informs viewpoints and decisions; governments and communities weigh additional considerations.
How to answer AC9S8H04 questions
  1. Audience: Who needs the information?
  2. Evidence: What scientific information supports the message?
  3. Communication: Is it accurate, clear, accessible and appropriately qualified?
  4. Impact: How might it affect understanding, trust, behaviour or viewpoints?
  5. Decision: Could it inform a community policy or regulation?
