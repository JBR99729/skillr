YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Follow evidence into a public decision AC9S8H04

Learning intention: Explore how accurate science messages can inform individual viewpoints, community policies and regulations, while other considerations also shape decisions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A popular message is not a measured change of mind

A hypothetical school refill-station pilot recorded 120 discarded bottles on a baseline day and 70 on a pilot day: 50 fewer, about 41.7% of the baseline count. Different attendance or events could also affect the counts. A message was delivered to 400 families; 60 voluntarily replied and 45 supported the proposal. That is 75% of respondents, not 75% of all families. Neither record measures a change in individual viewpoints. The school can communicate this limited evidence and seek broader feedback before deciding its own policy.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Match message, audience and evidence

A family notice needs clear quantities, a traceable source and an explanation of uncertainty. A technical report can add counting methods, dates and attendance. Both must preserve the same findings. Credibility comes from relevant expertise, transparent methods and evidence, rather than a striking image or famous speaker.

Draft headline: “Refill station cuts all plastic impacts by 42%.” Only the two bottle counts above are supplied.
Try it · Rewrite the headline accurately.

“The pilot day had about 42% fewer discarded bottles than the baseline day.” Add that these are two days of counts, not a measure of all plastic impacts or proof the station alone caused the difference.

2. Distinguish reach from influence

Delivery is possible reach; reading, understanding, trust, viewpoint and behaviour are different outcomes. To investigate influence, compare ethically collected before/after viewpoints using the same question and consider other events, non-response and voluntary participation. A change after a message does not by itself isolate the message as its cause.

Of 60 voluntary respondents, 45 support the proposal. No earlier viewpoint survey was taken.
Try it · Can you say the message persuaded 45 families?

No. They may already have supported it, and other influences are unknown. Report 45 supportive replies and explain the missing baseline and self-selection.

3. Explain the route to a rule

A policy sets an organisation’s direction; a regulation is a rule made by an authorised body. Scientific communication can inform both. Decision-makers also consider access, cost, rights, feasibility and community values. Consultation lets people question evidence and explain effects on them; a large response count does not automatically create a regulation.

The school can decide its refill-station arrangements. A hypothetical council is considering a separate public-place rule and asks for evidence, costs and access concerns.
Try it · Why would the council need more than the school’s support percentage?

It needs evidence relevant to public places, who responded, likely benefits and burdens, access, costs and the authority and process for the decision. School feedback can inform discussion but does not determine a council regulation.

Common mistakes

“Delivered means persuaded.” A delivered message may not be read, understood or change a viewpoint.

“75% support is the community view.” Name the denominator: this is 75% of 60 voluntary respondents.

“Science alone creates regulations.” Evidence informs a decision that also involves authority, values and practical considerations.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create a balanced family notice

Write a short notice using both supplied records. Include the count reduction, the correct response denominator, one uncertainty and a voluntary invitation for feedback. Add a second sentence explaining one access or cost issue the school should consider.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “Of the … respondents”. Core: separate findings from recommendations. Extend: propose a privacy-respecting way to investigate viewpoint change and a possible confound.

Use the fictional records. Do not collect personal opinions or identify families for this paper activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is 45/400 = 11.25% also correct?

Yes: supportive replies were received from 11.25% of all recipients. It does not tell you the opinions of those who did not reply.

2. Could a clear, accurate message fail to change a viewpoint?

Yes. People may already agree, weigh other considerations differently or lack the resources to act. Accuracy and influence are separate questions.

3. If a message hides uncertainty to win support, is it effective science communication?

It may attract support but misrepresents the evidence. Explain what is known, what remains uncertain and what action is justified.

FAQ

Must every message contain every technical detail?

No. Select relevant detail for the audience, preserve meaning and uncertainty, and provide a route to the fuller evidence.

Does disagreement mean the evidence is useless?

No. Examine whether people disagree about the evidence, its interpretation or the values and trade-offs in the decision.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify audience, purpose and evidence in a science message.
  • Evaluate accuracy, credibility, accessibility and uncertainty.
  • Explain how wording and visuals can influence viewpoints or behaviour.
  • Explain how communication can inform policy and regulation without automatically determining them.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that evidence quality and source credibility matter. Distinguish a scientific finding from the way it is framed for an audience.

Key concept

Use the chain evidence → audience-appropriate message → understanding and trust → viewpoint or behaviour → possible policy or regulation.

Effective communication is accurate, clear, accessible, transparent about uncertainty and linked to credible evidence and expertise.

Risk communication should distinguish relative and absolute risk. Saying a risk “doubled” can mislead if it changed only from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000.

Communication informs policy and regulation but does not mechanically create them; communities also weigh ethics, economics, feasibility and values.

Worked examples

Tsunami warnings

A useful tsunami alert names the credible agency, affected area and required action while preserving calibrated uncertainty rather than changing “possible” into “guaranteed”.

Organ donation campaigns

Organ donation stories can engage audiences, but informed choice also requires accurate evidence about eligibility, consent and safeguards.

Biodegradable materials

Communication about biodegradable packaging should state conditions needed for decomposition, including when industrial composting is required.

Science communicators

Professor Lisa Harvey-Smith and Dr Karl Kruszelnicki illustrate how analogies and stories can make science accessible; popularity never replaces evidence.

Common misconceptions
If a campaign works, everyone changes behaviour. Correction: communication can influence choices but cannot guarantee them.
Simpler language is automatically less accurate. Correction: clear language can preserve meaning while reducing jargon.
Uncertainty should be hidden to build trust. Correction: transparent, calibrated uncertainty can strengthen credibility.
A famous communicator is automatically correct. Correction: claims still require relevant evidence and trustworthy sources.
Guided practice
  1. Identify the audience and purpose.
  2. Underline the scientific evidence.
  3. Circle wording that changes certainty or magnitude.
  4. Suggest one improvement for clarity or credibility.
Independent practice
  1. Fictional classroom warning from Example Coastal Science Agency: “A tsunami is possible after an offshore earthquake. Low-lying beaches in fictional Bay A may be affected. People there should follow the local emergency authority’s advice and keep away from the shore while updates are assessed.” Rewrite this supplied message for a coastal community, preserving the uncertainty, location and action. This is a fictional text exercise, not a current warning.
  2. Explain why absolute risk should accompany the claim “risk doubled”.
  3. Identify two credibility checks for a health claim shared on social media.
  4. Explain how a personal story and statistical evidence play different roles in an organ-donation campaign.
  5. Evaluate a biodegradable-packaging claim that omits disposal conditions.
  6. Explain why science communication can influence a regulation without being the only factor in the policy decision.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Scientists report that flooding is likely, but a headline says flooding is guaranteed. Analyse how this wording change could affect trust, preparedness and policy debate.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes a science message credible? Relevant evidence, transparent sources, appropriate expertise and accurate representation of uncertainty.
  2. Why identify the audience? Different audiences need different vocabulary, detail, formats and actions.
  3. Why communicate uncertainty? It prevents overclaiming and shows the strength and limits of evidence.
  4. Does science communication make policy? No; it informs viewpoints and decisions alongside ethical, social and economic considerations.
Practice and review
  1. Evaluate a tsunami alert for credibility, clarity and actionability.
    Identify source, audience, evidence, uncertainty and required action.
  2. A headline says risk “doubled” from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000. Improve it.
    Add absolute risks and explain the magnitude accurately.
  3. Assess a social-media disease-prevention claim linked only to a celebrity interview.
    Trace the claim to research, methods and relevant expert review.
Check understanding
  • I identify audience and purpose.
  • I distinguish evidence from framing.
  • I preserve uncertainty and magnitude accurately.
  • I explain how communication may influence policy without determining it.

Exit ticket: Why can hiding uncertainty make science communication less trustworthy?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Compare headlines, agency alerts, graphs and public campaigns. Require students to distinguish message quality from whether they agree with the policy.

Parent/carer

Ask: Who is speaking, what evidence supports the message, what uncertainty remains, and what action is being requested?

Support: use an audience–evidence–message organiser.
Core: evaluate risk and source framing.
Extend: compare communication choices for different audiences without drifting into senior media studies.
