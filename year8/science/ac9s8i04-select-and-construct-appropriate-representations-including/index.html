Show the time gaps honestly AC9S8I04

Learning intention: Select and construct useful tables, graphs and models, and process data with transparent mathematical relationships and digital formulas.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Unequal time intervals need unequal graph spacing

The hypothetical record contains temperatures of 20, 23 and 25°C at 0, 3 and 7 minutes. On a time graph, the 3-to-7-minute interval must be wider than the 0-to-3 interval. Overall change divided by total elapsed time is (25 − 20)/(7 − 0) = 5/7, about 0.71°C/min. The two interval rates are 1°C/min and 0.5°C/min. Their simple mean, 0.75°C/min, is not the overall rate because the intervals last different amounts of time.

Learn the skill

1. Match form to purpose

Use a table for exact records, a bar chart for category comparisons, a time graph for ordered change and a scatter plot for paired quantitative values. A map shows spatial distribution; a labelled scientific model can explain structure or transfers. Choose the form that answers the question, and state what it leaves out.

Case A compares counts in three material categories. Case B compares paired mass and extension readings. Case C shows earthquake locations.
Try it · Choose a useful representation for each.

A: a bar chart with counts and separate categories. B: a scatter plot of paired quantitative values. C: a labelled map with a key. Each can be supported by a table of exact records.

2. Construct the graph faithfully

Label axes with quantities and units and use a consistent numerical scale. This graph has one-minute x steps and one-degree y steps. Its y-axis explicitly begins at 20°C; this can reveal changes in a time series, but disclose the restricted range. Plotted points are observations; connecting segments summarise change between them.

A graph places 0, 3 and 7 minutes at equally spaced tick positions and joins all three temperatures.
Try it · What should be repaired?

Use spacing proportional to elapsed time: the 4-minute interval must be four one-minute steps, and the 3-minute interval three steps. Label axes and preserve exact points. The joined line does not establish every unmeasured temperature.

3. Show formulas and preserve provenance

A rate is a change divided by the corresponding elapsed interval. In the supplied spreadsheet, =(B4-B2)/(A4-A2) computes the overall rate; =(B3-B2)/(A3-A2) gives the first interval. Check references and units. Keep original observations separate from processed values; a spreadsheet can calculate a wrong formula very neatly.

A learner calculates =AVERAGE(1,0.5) from the two interval rates and labels it “overall rate”.
Try it · Why is that label wrong, and how do you fix it?

It weights a 3-minute interval and a 4-minute interval equally. Use total change 5°C divided by total time 7 minutes, or weight each rate by its duration: (1×3 + 0.5×4)/7 = 5/7°C/min.

Common mistakes

“Equal list spacing means equal time spacing.” Plot according to numerical time gaps, not row order.

“Every graph axis must start at zero.” Choose and disclose a suitable scale; category bar lengths normally need a zero baseline.

“A computed cell is automatically correct.” Audit the formula, references, units and source values.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Construct and audit the three-point graph

Draw axes, choose even scales and plot the supplied three points with correct time spacing. State the y-axis start, show the overall-rate calculation and add a sentence distinguishing observations from the connecting line. Check a partner’s graph against the table.

Adjust the challenge Support: mark all minute ticks first. Core: justify the representation and rate. Extend: explain why a map or particle model might answer a different science question better than this graph.

Use the supplied hypothetical records. No heating or digital account is needed; an optional spreadsheet should retain the raw table and labelled formulas.

Three tricky questions

1. What is the overall temperature rise?

5°C, from 20 to 25°C. This is a change, not a rate; divide by 7 minutes to obtain the average rate.

2. Can the graph establish an exact 24°C reading at 5 minutes?

No. A straight connecting segment would estimate 24°C there, but no 5-minute observation is supplied and the real change need not have been linear.

3. Can a model that omits all energy transfer to the surroundings be useful?

Only for a stated limited purpose. An energy account needs important transfers and a clear boundary; omission must not imply that transferred energy vanished.

FAQ

Should I round the raw values to make the graph simpler?

Keep the source observations intact. Choose readable scales and label any derived or rounded summary separately.

What makes a graph accessible?

Readable labels, units, sufficient contrast and non-colour cues, plus a text description and exact table where helpful.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Choose graphs and tables that suit data type and purpose.
  • Use spreadsheet formulas and consistent units correctly.
  • Represent energy and particle changes accurately.
  • Evaluate what a graph, model or simulation shows and omits.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall independent and dependent variables, table headings with units, measurement precision and the difference between categories and continuous quantities.

Key concept

Use the chain question → data type → representation → processing → pattern → limitation. A neat representation is not necessarily an honest or useful one.

In a spreadsheet, =AVERAGE(B2:B6) calculates a mean and =SUM(B2:B6) a total. Convert to consistent units before combining or averaging values.

Use a line graph for change over a continuous variable, a scatter plot for two quantitative variables, a bar chart for categories and a pie chart for parts of a whole—not change over time.

Models and diagrams must preserve the science. An energy-flow diagram includes important stores and transfers, with arrows showing the direction of transfer or transformation; a particle model distinguishes physical rearrangement from chemical formation of new substances.

Worked examples

Spreadsheet mean

For repeated readings in B2:B6, use =AVERAGE(B2:B6). Using =SUM gives the total. Cell references let the result update when a source value changes.

Graph choice

Use a line graph for temperature over time, a scatter plot for mass and extension, a bar chart for material categories and a pie chart only for proportions of a whole.

Rollercoaster energy

At the top a rollercoaster has high gravitational potential energy. Downhill, kinetic energy increases while some energy transfers to the surroundings as heat and sound.

Physical and chemical changes

Evaporation spreads particles farther apart; they do not disappear. A model of rusting must include oxygen as a reactant because new substances form.

Common misconceptions
A pie chart shows change over time well. Correction: A line graph is usually appropriate for continuous time data.
The dependent variable belongs on the x-axis. Correction: Conventionally the independent variable is on x and dependent on y.
An outlier should be deleted. Correction: Investigate it and justify any exclusion.
A model is an exact copy of reality. Correction: Every representation selects features and has limitations.
Guided practice
  1. Identify whether the data are categorical or quantitative.
  2. Choose a graph and justify it.
  3. Check axes, units, title and scale.
  4. State one pattern and one limitation.
Independent practice
  1. Choose a representation for temperature over 20 minutes.
  2. Explain why SUM cannot replace AVERAGE.
  3. Correct a graph with mixed units and an uneven scale.
  4. Draw an energy-flow description for a rollercoaster.
  5. Explain how particle models differ for evaporation and rusting.
  6. Evaluate what a cardboard plate model or simulation shows and leaves out.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Three groups measured the same variable using different units and methods. Explain the checks and conversions needed before collating the data, then choose a representation that exposes rather than hides disagreements.

Questions and answers
  1. Which graph for categories? A bar chart, with separated category bars.
  2. Why use cell references? Formulas update when source data changes and are easier to audit.
  3. What belongs on graph axes? Independent variable on x and dependent variable on y, with names and units.
  4. How is a model evaluated? By its purpose, evidence, strengths, assumptions and omitted features.
Practice and review
  1. Choose a graph for battery voltage across recharge cycles.
    Cycles are ordered quantitative values, so a line graph can show change.
  2. Evaluate a pie chart used for temperature over time.
    It represents parts of a whole, not a continuous sequence.
  3. Assess a model that shows only useful kinetic energy.
    Include energy transferred to heat and sound and state the model purpose.
Check understanding
  • I match representations to data.
  • I process values with correct formulas and units.
  • I label graphs honestly.
  • I explain model strengths and limitations.

Exit ticket: Why can a technically correct graph still mislead?
