Choose Representations That Reveal the Science

Good scientific representations organise data, reveal patterns and make relationships easier to interpret. The best representation depends on the purpose, and every representation has strengths and limitations.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

Question → choose a representation → process data correctly → identify the pattern → evaluate what the representation shows and hides.

A representation is not automatically good because it looks neat. It must match the data and the scientific purpose.

E1 — Spreadsheet formulas

Spreadsheets can process repeated measurements efficiently. Use cell references so results update when the source data changes.

  • =AVERAGE(B2:B6) calculates the mean of a range.
  • =SUM(B2:B6) calculates a total, not a mean.
  • Check the number of values if calculating a mean manually.
  • Use consistent units before combining or averaging data.
E2–E3 — Choose and construct graphs

Line graph: change over a continuous variable such as time or temperature.

Scatter plot: relationship between two quantitative variables.

Bar chart: comparison among categories.

Pie chart: proportions of a whole, not change over time.

Graph conventions

  • Independent variable on the x-axis.
  • Dependent variable on the y-axis.
  • Label axes with variable names and units.
  • Use a descriptive title.
  • Use a consistent, honest scale.
E4 — Energy-flow diagrams

Energy diagrams should show important energy stores and transfers, not just one form. Arrows indicate direction of transfer or transformation.

Rollercoaster example

At the top: high gravitational potential energy.

Moving downhill: gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases.

Real system: some energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat and sound.

E5 — Represent physical and chemical changes

Choose a representation that matches the type of change.

  • Physical change: particle arrangement or spacing changes, but the substances remain the same.
  • Chemical change: new substances form, so equations or particle models should show reactants and products.

For evaporation, particles spread farther apart; they do not disappear. For rusting, oxygen must be represented as a reactant.

E6 — Collate data from multiple sources

Before combining group data:

  • convert to consistent units
  • check that measurement methods are comparable
  • identify possible outliers
  • inspect unexpected differences before excluding data
  • then calculate appropriate summary values.

Different results are not automatically wrong; they may reveal natural variation or a difference in method.

E7 — Evaluate strengths and limitations

Every model, graph or diagram simplifies reality.

  • A cardboard plate model can show relative motion but not real mantle forces.
  • A simulation can slow, enlarge or colour processes to make patterns visible, but may distort scale or timing.
  • An anatomy diagram may show structure clearly while omitting quantitative detail.

The key question is not “Is the model perfect?” but “What is it useful for, and what does it leave out?”

Common mistakes
  • Using a pie chart for change over time.
  • Putting the dependent variable on the x-axis by habit.
  • Omitting units or using uneven scales.
  • Using SUM when the question asks for an average.
  • Combining mixed units before converting them.
  • Deleting outliers without investigating them.
  • Treating a model or simulation as an exact copy of reality.
Assessment strategy

Ask these questions:

  1. What relationship or pattern needs to be shown?
  2. Which representation best suits that purpose?
  3. Are formulas, units, labels and scales correct?
  4. What pattern does the representation support?
  5. What limitation should be acknowledged?
