Has a New Substance Formed?

Compare physical and chemical changes using evidence, then identify energy changes and explain how chemical indicators are used in real tests.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

A physical change changes state, shape or arrangement without producing a new substance. A chemical change forms new substances with different properties. Strong explanations use before-and-after evidence rather than relying on a single clue.

Success criteria

  • Classify physical and chemical changes and justify the decision.
  • Recognise gas formation, precipitate formation, persistent colour change and temperature change as possible reaction indicators.
  • Interpret before-and-after data and temperature trends.
  • Distinguish exothermic from endothermic changes.
  • Explain how material properties affect production and use.
  • Explain how chemical indicators are used in soil, water and medical tests.
Important notes
  • One clue is not always enough. Boiling makes bubbles without necessarily creating a new substance; a dye can change colour without a chemical reaction.
  • Use evidence chains. Gas + temperature change, or precipitate + changed properties, is stronger evidence than one observation alone.
  • Exothermic: energy is transferred to the surroundings; the measured temperature often rises.
  • Endothermic: energy is absorbed from the surroundings; the measured temperature often falls.
  • Physical reversibility can help: melting/freezing and dissolving/recovery often support a physical change, but reversibility alone is not a universal rule.
Reaction-indicator visual
Reasoning rule: observation → what it indicates → conclusion. Example: “A new solid formed from two clear solutions → this is a precipitate → new substances likely formed, so the change is chemical.”

Energy changes: read the trend

Exothermic: measured temperature rises because energy is transferred from the reacting system to the surroundings. Endothermic: measured temperature falls because the system absorbs energy from the surroundings.

Worked examples: Observe → Connect → Conclude

1. Vinegar + bicarbonate

Observe: bubbles form and the flask becomes colder.

Connect: gas production indicates a new gaseous substance; the temperature decrease shows an endothermic energy change.

Conclude: the combined evidence supports a chemical reaction.

2. Melting ice

Observe: solid water becomes liquid water.

Connect: only the state changes; the substance is still H₂O.

Conclude: melting is a physical change.

3. Yellow solid forms

Observe: two clear liquids form an insoluble yellow solid.

Connect: the solid is a precipitate with different properties from the original solutions.

Conclude: new substances formed, so the change is chemical.

4. Rusting iron

Observe: iron develops a reddish-brown coating over time.

Connect: iron reacts with substances in the environment to form iron oxide with different properties.

Conclude: rusting is a chemical change and can reduce the usefulness of iron.

Australian Curriculum elaborations — actionable teaching

E1 — Indicators

Run small safe reactions and record gas, precipitate, persistent colour and temperature observations. Require students to distinguish observation from conclusion.

E2 — Before/after data

Compare state, colour, temperature and recoverability before and after an interaction. Students justify physical vs chemical using at least two evidence points.

E3 — Energy changes

Collect temperature-time data for contrasting reactions and classify the trends as exothermic or endothermic.

E4 — Properties and use

Compare copper, plastic, glass, aluminium, iron and stainless steel. Link conductivity, transparency, durability and corrosion resistance to use.

E5 — Testing kits

Interpret simulated soil pH, nitrate, water-quality or glucose-strip results and discuss what each test can and cannot conclude.

Common misconceptions
“Any bubbles prove a reaction.”

False. Boiling can produce bubbles by physical change. Identify whether a new gas formed.

“Any colour change proves a reaction.”

False. Use context and supporting evidence.

“Melting is chemical because energy is involved.”

False. Energy transfer occurs in physical changes too.

“Energy is created in an exothermic reaction.”

False. Energy is transferred from the reacting system to the surroundings.

Teacher toolkit: 50-minute lesson
  1. 0–5 min: classify melting ice, rusting iron, burning paper and dissolving sugar.
  2. 5–15 min: explicitly model observation → indicator → conclusion.
  3. 15–27 min: students analyse before/after data cards.
  4. 27–38 min: graph or interpret temperature change in two reactions.
  5. 38–45 min: apply chemical indicators to testing-kit scenarios.
  6. 45–50 min: exit ticket: “Name two pieces of evidence and justify whether a new substance formed.”
