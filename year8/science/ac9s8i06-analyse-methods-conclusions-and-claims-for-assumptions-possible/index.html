Evaluate the Method Before You Trust the Claim

Strong scientific evaluation asks what was assumed, what could have affected the evidence, whether other results agree, and what still needs to be tested before a conclusion is accepted broadly.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core evaluation chain

Claim → assumptions → method quality → sources of error → conflicting evidence → justified conclusion → next question.

The goal is not to find a vague flaw. A strong evaluation identifies a specific limitation, explains how it could affect the evidence, and proposes a targeted way to test or reduce its influence.

Success criteria

  • Identify assumptions that a method or claim takes for granted.
  • Distinguish specific sources of error from vague labels such as “human error”.
  • Compare conflicting results by examining methods, samples and measurement conditions.
  • Keep conclusions within the range and quality of the evidence.
  • Propose follow-up questions that test an uncertainty or boundary of the original claim.
E1 — Assumptions and variable control

An assumption is something treated as true without being directly checked. Examples include assuming samples start at the same temperature, soil moisture is equal, instruments are calibrated, or different brands have identical composition.

Ask: If this assumption is false, could it change the dependent variable? If yes, it should be controlled, measured or acknowledged.

E2 — Sources of error and method improvement

Useful evaluations name the mechanism of the error.

  • Parallax: reading a scale from the wrong angle.
  • Calibration error: an instrument is consistently offset.
  • Timing inconsistency: measurements are taken at different stages.
  • Uncontrolled variables: beaker size, stirring speed or temperature changes between trials.
  • Instrument condition: worn graduations, unstable balances or poorly positioned sensors.

A good improvement is specific: “read the meniscus at eye level” is stronger than “be more accurate”.

E3 — Conflicting evidence

Different groups or sources may reach different results without either being automatically wrong. Compare:

  • materials and sample characteristics
  • controlled variables
  • instrument resolution and calibration
  • sample size and tested range
  • measurement procedures
  • environmental conditions

Conflicting evidence should lead to investigation and replication, not selective acceptance of whichever result matches the prediction.

E4 — Unanswered questions and limits of conclusions

A conclusion should not extend beyond what was tested. If only one plant species was studied, the data do not establish that the same effect occurs in all plants. If temperatures were tested only up to 40°C, the trend above 40°C remains unanswered.

Strong follow-up questions test the boundary of a claim: different species, wider temperature ranges, more battery cycles, higher wind speeds or different surface materials.

E5 — Assumptions hidden inside claims

Broad claims often contain unstated premises. For example, “bigger batteries always last longer” assumes physical size reliably predicts usable chemical capacity under comparable conditions. “Stronger winds always produce more turbine output” assumes the turbine never reaches operating limits or shuts down for safety.

To evaluate a claim, identify what must be true for the reasoning to work, then ask whether that premise is reasonable and supported.

Worked example

Claim: warmer water causes faster dissolving

Method issue: warmer trials were also stirred faster.

Why it matters: stirring itself can increase dissolving rate, so the effect cannot be attributed to temperature alone.

Revised conclusion: dissolving was faster in the warmer, faster-stirred trials, but the separate effect of temperature was not isolated.

Follow-up: repeat while keeping stirring speed constant.

Precision, accuracy and systematic error

Precision describes agreement among repeated measurements. Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. A probe can give tightly grouped readings that are all 2°C too high: precise, but inaccurate.

If every measurement is shifted in the same direction because of calibration, the problem is systematic error. Repeating the same biased method does not remove it.

Common weak evaluations
  • “Human error.” Too vague. Name the actual error and effect.
  • “Do more trials.” Useful only if random variation or sample size is the problem; it does not fix a calibration bias.
  • “The textbook must be right.” Published evidence still needs contextual comparison.
  • “The prediction matched, so the method is valid.” Matching a prediction does not rule out confounding variables.
  • “Any uncertainty makes the conclusion wrong.” Conclusions can be supported with appropriate limits and uncertainty.
How to answer AC9S8I06 questions
  1. Name the claim.
  2. Identify the assumption or specific method issue.
  3. Explain how it could affect the measured evidence.
  4. Compare other evidence or methods if results conflict.
  5. State how far the conclusion is justified.
  6. Propose one targeted follow-up.
