YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Trace a surprising claim back to the method AC9S8I06

Learning intention: Analyse assumptions, specific error mechanisms, conflicting evidence and unanswered questions before judging a scientific claim.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The image grew; the cell did not

Two hypothetical readings measure the same dimension of the same fixed prepared cell. With a 10× ocular and 10× objective, eight ocular divisions at a calibrated 5 µm/division give 40 µm. With the same ocular and a 40× objective, 32 divisions at the new calibration of 1.25 µm/division also give 40 µm. Reusing the first conversion factor at the second setting gives an incorrect 160 µm. The claim “the cell grew fourfold” confuses a changed image scale with physical growth.

Learn the skill

1. Name the assumption and error mechanism

An assumption is treated as true without checking. Here the faulty method assumes that ocular divisions represent the same physical length after an objective change. Link flaw → effect on data → effect on claim → targeted correction. “Human error” does not identify what happened or how to improve it.

The second reading is 32 divisions. A learner uses 5 µm/division and reports 160 µm.
Try it · What exact repair addresses this error?

Use the calibration for the second setting: 32 × 1.25 = 40 µm. Record objective and calibration with every reading. Taking more readings while reusing 5 µm/division would keep the scale error.

2. Match the conclusion to the method

Repeated measurements can reveal random scatter, but averaging does not remove a systematic conversion error. Confirm that both measurements use the same cell dimension, preparation and appropriate calibration. In the supplied record, the calculated physical lengths agree; the records provide no evidence of cell growth between settings.

A second example has warmer-water dissolving trials stirred faster than cooler trials.
Try it · Why can the results not isolate temperature’s effect?

Stirring changed with temperature and can affect dissolving. Keep stirring method consistent in a targeted repeat. More trials of the confounded method would not separate the two effects.

3. Compare conflicts and ask a discriminating question

When reports differ, compare what was measured, samples, preparation, equipment, calibration and conditions before deciding they contradict each other. Do not select the result you prefer or automatically average incompatible measurements. Identify what missing information or controlled comparison could distinguish explanations.

Report A gives a mean cell length of 40 µm; report B gives 55 µm. Cell type, preparation and calibration records for B are not supplied.
Try it · What should you ask before calling one report wrong?

Ask whether both measured the same cell type and dimension, how samples were prepared and selected, and whether each setting was calibrated. A follow-up could compare the same prepared specimen with recorded calibration on both instruments.

Common mistakes

“More repeats fix every error.” Repeats of the same biased conversion retain the error.

“A larger image proves growth.” Convert through the correct scale before comparing physical size.

“Conflicting evidence must be discarded.” Compare methods and contexts and identify useful follow-up questions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build an error–effect–improvement chain

Use the three rows of the calibration table. Write the hidden assumption, calculate the incorrect and correct second lengths, and explain why the growth claim fails. Then write one precise question needed to compare the 40 µm and 55 µm reports.

Adjust the challenge Support: match setting to calibration first. Core: explain the full error chain. Extend: identify which further evidence could distinguish a biological difference from a measurement difference.

Use supplied paper records. This activity does not involve preparing cells, changing microscope equipment or repeating an actual practical.

Three tricky questions

1. If two measurements agree exactly, does that prove they are accurate?

No. They could share a calibration bias. Agreement is useful evidence, but accuracy also needs appropriate calibration or comparison with an accepted reference.

2. If an expected result is obtained, can the method still be flawed?

Yes. A confound or biased measurement can happen to produce a matching result. Evaluate the method as well as the outcome.

3. Can a small study support any conclusion?

Yes, a carefully qualified conclusion about the tested sample and conditions. A limited study does not justify an unlimited claim, but limitations do not make all its evidence worthless.

FAQ

Must every error have a known direction?

No. Explain the likely direction when justified; otherwise state the added uncertainty and how you would investigate it.

What makes a follow-up question useful?

It targets an unresolved condition, alternative explanation or range, and proposes evidence that could change the judgement.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify assumptions hidden in methods and claims.
  • Name specific random, systematic and procedural errors.
  • Compare conflicting evidence through methods and conditions.
  • Set conclusion limits and propose targeted follow-up questions.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall controlled variables, precision, accuracy, anomalies, sample size and the difference between evidence and a claim.

Key concept

Use the chain claim → assumption → method quality → source of error → conflicting evidence → justified conclusion → follow-up.

An assumption is treated as true without being checked. Examples include equal starting temperatures, moisture, composition or instrument calibration. Ask whether a false assumption could change the dependent variable.

Name the mechanism: parallax, timing inconsistency, uncontrolled stirring speed or calibration bias. A consistent offset is systematic error; repeating the biased method does not remove it.

Conflicting evidence should trigger comparison of samples, controls, equipment, range and conditions—not selective acceptance. A conclusion must stay within the tested species, temperature, cycle or operating range.

Worked examples

Warmer water and dissolving

Warmer trials were also stirred faster. Stirring speed can affect dissolving, so the separate temperature effect was not isolated. Repeat while keeping stirring speed constant.

Parallax and instrument condition

Parallax occurs when a scale is read from the wrong angle. Read a measuring-cylinder meniscus at eye level. Also check worn graduations, unstable balances and poorly positioned sensors before blaming a vague “human error”.

Precise but inaccurate

A probe gives tightly grouped readings that are all 2°C high. The results are precise but inaccurate because calibration creates systematic error.

Conflicting battery evidence

Compare load, temperature, battery age, end point, sample size and calibration before deciding whether two battery studies truly conflict.

Hidden claim limits

“Stronger winds always produce more turbine output” ignores operating limits and safety shutdowns. Test a wider range and qualify the claim.

Common misconceptions
“Human error” is a sufficient evaluation. Correction: Name the action, mechanism and effect.
More trials fix every problem. Correction: Repeats do not remove a systematic calibration bias.
A matching prediction proves the method valid. Correction: Confounding variables may still produce the result.
Any uncertainty makes a conclusion wrong. Correction: Evidence can support a qualified conclusion with stated limits.
Guided practice
  1. State the claim exactly.
  2. Identify one assumption or method issue.
  3. Explain how it could change the evidence.
  4. Propose one targeted improvement or follow-up.
Independent practice
  1. Replace “human error” with a specific error mechanism.
  2. Explain why more repeats do not fix calibration bias.
  3. Evaluate the warmer-water dissolving method.
  4. Compare two battery studies conducted under different loads.
  5. Limit a conclusion tested on one plant species.
  6. Write a follow-up question about wind turbine output near operating limits.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Two groups report opposite results. One used a larger sample; the other used better-calibrated equipment. Explain what further information is needed before judging the evidence and design a fair replication.

Questions and answers
  1. What is an assumption? A condition taken as true without direct checking.
  2. What makes an improvement targeted? It addresses the named source of error or uncertainty.
  3. Why compare methods when evidence conflicts? Different conditions can explain different results.
  4. What is an unanswered question? A relevant condition, range or group not resolved by the investigation.
Practice and review
  1. Evaluate “human error affected the results”.
    Name a mechanism such as parallax or timing and explain its effect.
  2. Assess “do more trials” for a biased probe.
    More trials show consistency but do not correct systematic error.
  3. Limit a claim based on temperatures to 40°C.
    Do not extend beyond the tested range; propose a safe wider-range test.
Check understanding
  • I identify assumptions.
  • I name errors specifically.
  • I compare conflicting methods fairly.
  • I qualify conclusions and propose follow-ups.

Exit ticket: Why is “do more trials” sometimes a poor improvement?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Require the sequence limitation → effect on evidence → targeted improvement; reject vague error labels.

Parent/carer

Ask what the claim assumes and what evidence would change the student’s judgement.

Support: use an error–effect–improvement table.
Core: evaluate conclusion scope.
Extend: reconcile conflicting evidence across methods.
