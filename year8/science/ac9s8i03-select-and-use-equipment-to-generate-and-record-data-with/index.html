YEAR 8 SCIENCE

Calibrate the scale before measuring AC9S8I03

Learning intention: Select suitable equipment, use it consistently and record defensible precision, including when microscopes and digital tools are appropriate.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Twelve divisions can represent sixty micrometres

In this original hypothetical calibration, a 10× ocular is paired with a 10× objective: total magnification is 100×. A 60 µm stage-scale span aligns with 12 ocular divisions. Each ocular division therefore represents 60 ÷ 12 = 5 µm at this setting. An object estimated as about seven ocular divisions long is about 7 × 5 = 35 µm. Writing 35.000 µm would imply unsupported detail. These calibration values belong to the stated instrument and setting; changing the objective requires calibration again.

Learn the skill

1. Choose range and resolution for the question

Range must cover the expected values; resolution is the smallest displayed step or scale division. Accuracy concerns closeness to an accepted value, and repeatability concerns agreement among repeated readings. A fine display cannot guarantee accuracy. Choose equipment that can distinguish a change of interest without exceeding its range.

You need to distinguish a 0.2 g mass difference. Balance A displays steps of 1 g; balance B displays steps of 0.01 g. Both cover the expected mass range.
Try it · Which is the better initial choice, and what check remains?

B has a resolution suitable for distinguishing 0.2 g. Check its condition, zero and calibration and use it consistently; 0.01 g display steps do not by themselves prove 0.01 g accuracy.

2. Convert a calibrated reading honestly

An ocular scale has divisions, not fixed micrometres. Align known stage and ocular spans at the chosen setting, divide known length by ocular divisions, then multiply the object reading by that factor. Record units and the approximate nature of the reading; do not let a calculator create unsupported digits.

Use the supplied 5 µm/division factor. A second object spans about 9 ocular divisions at this same setting.
Try it · What size should be reported?

About 45 µm: 9 divisions × 5 µm/division. The word “about” reflects the stated estimate; the givens do not justify 45.000 µm.

3. Check microscopy and digital tools

Begin with the scanning objective to locate and centre a prepared specimen; use the instrument’s safe focusing method. Total magnification is ocular × objective, while resolution concerns separating nearby details. Image enlargement alone may not add detail. A camera scale bar or sensor display also needs valid calibration, units and suitable sampling.

A digital image is enlarged twofold after a scale bar was inserted. The image and bar are enlarged together, but the caption still calls the displayed image “100× on every screen”.
Try it · What should be checked or changed?

Display magnification varies with screen and resizing, so that claim is unreliable. Keep a correctly calibrated scale bar that scales with the image, verify its units and avoid claiming enlargement improves resolution.

Common mistakes

“More digits mean more accuracy.” Digits must be supported by the instrument and method.

“One ocular division is always 5 µm.” That value is specific to this calibration and setting.

“Magnification equals resolution.” Larger displayed features are not necessarily more distinguishable.

Adjust the challenge Support: label length, divisions and units first. Core: show both conversion steps. Extend: explain how repeated readings could agree yet share a calibration bias.

Use the supplied scale model on paper. Any microscope use follows teacher and manufacturer instructions with suitable prepared slides; no specimen collection or chemical preparation is required.

Three tricky questions

1. If an uncalibrated balance repeatedly reads 2.50 g for a 2.70 g standard, is repetition enough?

No. The agreement shows repeatability, but the repeated low reading indicates inaccuracy for that standard. Check calibration rather than averaging away the bias.

2. Does a digital thermometer reading to 0.01°C guarantee that accuracy?

No. Display resolution and accuracy differ. Calibration, sensor specifications, placement and conditions matter.

3. Can you measure an object by counting the decorative pixels in this diagram?

No. Use its stated scale alignment and records. It is an explanatory model, not a micrograph from which new measurements should be taken.

FAQ

Why record settings as well as the final size?

They make the calibration and measurement traceable and help another investigator use or check the appropriate conversion.

When is a data logger useful?

When repeated timed measurements help answer the question. Choose a suitable sampling interval and check sensor range, calibration, units and stored raw data.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Choose equipment whose range and resolution suit the measurement.
  • Distinguish precision from accuracy.
  • Record justified digits, signs and units.
  • Use digital tools and repeated samples appropriately.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall the independent and dependent variables and why a reproducible method records consistent measurement conditions.

Key concept

Precision describes measurement resolution and agreement among repeats; accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. A balance can repeatedly show 2.50 g for a 2.70 g standard: precise but inaccurate.

Choose suitable range and smallest graduation. Read the bottom of a concave meniscus at eye level to reduce parallax. Record an exact microscope setting such as 400×, not “high”.

Digits must match the instrument. On an analogue scale, make only a reasonable intermediate estimate between graduations; do not invent unsupported decimal places. For 4.32 g, 4.28 g and 4.30 g, the mean is 4.30 g. Writing 4 g discards information; writing 4.300000 g invents precision.

A digital microscope, video, data logger or simulation can extend observation, but calibration, sampling rate, sensor resolution and model assumptions still matter. More decimal places cannot replace an adequate sample size.

Worked examples

Volume and meniscus

A measuring cylinder is usually more suitable than a large beaker for about 25 mL. Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level and record units.

Mean with justified digits

For 4.32 g, 4.28 g and 4.30 g, total 12.90 g and divide by three to obtain 4.30 g. The total is not the mean.

Digital measurement

Video can analyse fast motion frame by frame; a data logger can sample at regular intervals. Neither is automatically accurate without calibration and suitable sampling.

Signs and notation

Changing −5.0°C to +5.0°C changes the value. Trailing zeros can show resolution, and 0.00032 m can be recorded as 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ m.

Common misconceptions
Precise means accurate. Correction: Tightly grouped readings can share a calibration bias.
Digital instruments are automatically correct. Correction: Calibration, resolution and sampling still matter.
More decimal places improve evidence. Correction: Unsupported digits invent precision.
One careful measurement is representative. Correction: Repeated measurements are needed to reveal variation.
Guided practice
  1. Match an expected measurement to a suitable instrument.
  2. Identify the correct meniscus reading position.
  3. Round a mean to justified precision.
  4. Label a results table with variables and units.
Independent practice
  1. Choose equipment for measuring 25 mL and justify the choice.
  2. Explain how parallax affects a measuring-cylinder reading.
  3. Distinguish precision and accuracy using the 2.50 g reading.
  4. Calculate the mean of 4.32 g, 4.28 g and 4.30 g.
  5. Explain one benefit and one limitation of a data logger.
  6. Write headings for Temperature (°C) and Reaction time (s), with the independent variable first.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A sensor gives 20.1, 20.1 and 20.2°C while a calibrated reference gives 22.1°C. Evaluate precision, accuracy and the value of simply taking more repeats.

Questions and answers
  1. What determines useful digits? The instrument resolution and justified estimation.
  2. Why record 5.0 V rather than 5 V? The trailing zero can communicate measurement to the nearest 0.1 V.
  3. Why repeat measurements? To reveal random variation and support a mean or range.
  4. When is a simulation useful? For unsafe, slow, fast or impractical systems, provided its assumptions are recognised.
Practice and review
  1. Select equipment to distinguish volumes near 25 mL.
    Compare range and smallest graduation, not appearance.
  2. Evaluate 4.320000 g from a two-decimal balance.
    The extra zeros are unsupported precision.
  3. Assess a single digital reading used as a typical result.
    Discuss repeats, sample size, calibration and variation.

Teacher + parent guidance
