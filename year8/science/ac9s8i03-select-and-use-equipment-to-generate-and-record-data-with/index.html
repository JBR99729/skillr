Measure and Record Data with Useful Precision

Good science is not about writing the most decimal places. It is about choosing suitable equipment, measuring carefully, recording only justified precision and organising data so another person can interpret and repeat the work.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Core idea

Choose suitable equipment → measure correctly → record justified precision → repeat enough times → organise values with variables and units.

Precision is not the same as accuracy

  • Precision: how finely a measurement is recorded and how closely repeated measurements agree.
  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the accepted or true value.

A balance can repeatedly read 2.50 g for a 2.70 g standard. Those readings are precise but inaccurate.

E1 — Select and use equipment

Choose an instrument whose range and smallest graduation suit the measurement. A 25 or 50 mL measuring cylinder is normally more useful for measuring about 25 mL than a large beaker.

Meniscus

For water and many common liquids in a measuring cylinder, read the bottom of the concave meniscus at eye level. Reading from above or below introduces parallax error.

Microscopes

Select magnification appropriate to the structure being observed and record the exact magnification, for example 400×, rather than vague terms such as “high”. Exact settings make observations easier to compare and reproduce.

E2 — Record only justified precision

The instrument determines the useful number of digits. If a digital balance displays 4.32 g, recording 4 g throws away useful information. But recording 4.320000 g invents precision the instrument did not measure.

On an analogue scale, you may make a reasonable intermediate estimate between graduations. Do not add several unsupported decimal places simply because a calculator allows them.

Worked example

Measurements: 4.32 g, 4.28 g, 4.30 g

Mean = (4.32 + 4.28 + 4.30) ÷ 3 = 4.30 g.

12.90 g is the total, not the mean. Reporting 4 g would round away useful measurement precision.

E3 — Use digital tools appropriately
  • Digital microscope: save images, compare structures and measure using a calibrated scale.
  • Video: analyse fast motion frame by frame and reduce reaction-time error.
  • Data logger: collect measurements automatically at regular intervals.
  • Simulation: investigate unsafe, very slow, very fast or impractical systems.

A digital display is not automatically more accurate. Calibration, sampling rate, sensor resolution and model assumptions still matter.

E4 — Tables, spreadsheets and variables

Results must be interpretable without guessing.

  • Put the independent variable first.
  • Follow with the dependent variable.
  • Give each column a meaningful heading.
  • Include units in headings, for example Temperature (°C) and Reaction time (s).
  • Use consistent units within a column.

When graphing, the independent variable is conventionally on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis.

E5 — Signs, decimals and exponential notation

Signs and units are part of a measurement. Changing −5.0°C to +5.0°C changes the value, not just its appearance.

Trailing zeros can communicate instrument precision: 5.0 V indicates measurement to the nearest 0.1 V, whereas 5 V does not communicate that same resolution.

Scientific notation is useful for very large or very small measurements. For example:

0.00032 m = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ m

Sample size and replicable data

One highly precise measurement does not show whether a result is typical. Repeating measurements under the same conditions helps reveal random variation and allows a mean or range to be calculated.

More decimal places cannot compensate for too few measurements.

Common mistakes
  • Choosing equipment with graduations too coarse for the expected change.
  • Reading the wrong part of the meniscus or viewing it at an angle.
  • Recording more decimal places than the instrument supports.
  • Rounding digital measurements unnecessarily.
  • Omitting units or mixing units within a column.
  • Changing or omitting negative signs.
  • Assuming simulations or digital sensors are automatically exact.
  • Using only one trial and calling the result typical.
Assessment strategy

For AC9S8I03, ask five questions:

  1. Is the equipment suitable for the size of the measurement and expected difference?
  2. Was the instrument read correctly?
  3. Are the recorded digits justified?
  4. Are units, signs, variables and table conventions correct?
  5. Are there enough measurements to support a replicable result?
