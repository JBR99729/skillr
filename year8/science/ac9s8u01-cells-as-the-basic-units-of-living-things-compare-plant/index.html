Inside Plant and Animal Cells

Cells are the basic units of living things. Their specialised structures perform different jobs that help cells function and survive.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

We are learning how cell structures help plant and animal cells carry out the processes needed for life.

Success criteria

  • Identify the nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, cell wall, chloroplasts and vacuole in models and diagrams.
  • Connect each structure to its function.
  • Compare typical plant and animal cells using evidence.
  • Interpret simple microscope images without assuming they look exactly like textbook diagrams.
  • Explain one strength and one limitation of a cell model.
Visual guide: plant and animal cells

Start with what the cells share, then identify the structures commonly shown only in a typical plant-cell model.

Typical animal cell

  • Cell membrane: controls movement into and out of the cell.
  • Cytoplasm: where many chemical reactions occur.
  • Nucleus: contains genetic material and helps control cell activities.

Typical plant cell

  • Cell wall: provides rigid support.
  • Chloroplasts: carry out photosynthesis in photosynthetic cells.
  • Large vacuole: stores cell sap and helps support the cell when filled with water.
  • Also contains a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.
Quick check: A root cell is a plant cell, but it may have no chloroplasts. Why? Think about whether an underground root receives enough light to photosynthesise.

Microscope evidence vs cell models

Microscope evidence

A light microscope shows real specimens, but structures may be faint, overlapping or difficult to distinguish. Stains can increase contrast.

Look for: visible boundaries, overall shape, repeated cells and structures that can genuinely be resolved.

Scientific model

A labelled diagram or 3D model simplifies a cell so important structures and relationships are easier to understand.

Remember: colours, spacing and relative sizes may be conventions rather than literal observations.

Quick check: A textbook chloroplast is bright green and very large, but a microscope image is faint. Does that make the microscope image wrong? Explain.
Concept boundary and prerequisites

Must understand

  • Cells are the basic units of living things.
  • The nucleus, cell membrane and cytoplasm are represented in both typical plant and animal cells.
  • Typical plant-cell models also show a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole, with functions linked to the cell's needs.
  • Different cells can have different shapes and structures depending on their function and location.
  • Microscopes extended human observation and contributed to changing scientific understanding of cells.

Helpful prior knowledge

  • Living things have needs and carry out life processes.
  • A scientific model represents selected features of something.
  • Light microscopes magnify small specimens.

What we are focusing on

You do not need senior-level detail about biochemical pathways, organelle ultrastructure or prokaryote–eukaryote classification here. Focus on identifying the key structures, explaining their functions, comparing plant and animal cells and interpreting models and microscope evidence carefully.

Teaching progression
  1. 1. Locate cells within a living organism
    Move from organism → organ → tissue → cell.
    Check: Why can you see a leaf without a microscope but not an individual leaf cell?
  2. 2. Identify cell structures
    Learn the shared structures first, then add plant-specific structures.
    Check: Which three structures are represented in both typical plant and animal cells?
  3. 3. Connect structure to function
    Do more than name a structure: explain the job it performs.
    Check: Why is a cell membrane useful to both plant and animal cells?
  4. 4. Separate observations from model conventions
    Compare what a microscope can show with what a diagram communicates.
    Check: Name one feature of a cell diagram that might not match the real specimen exactly.
  5. 5. Compare plant and animal cells
    Use evidence to identify similarities and differences.
    Check: Explain why “all plant cells contain chloroplasts” is too broad.
  6. 6. Evaluate models and scientific tools
    Identify what a model helps explain, what it leaves out and how microscopes provided new evidence.
    Check: Give one strength and one limitation of a labelled cell model.
Worked examples

From label to explanation

Weak: “This is a chloroplast.”

Better: “Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll and are the site of photosynthesis, allowing photosynthetic plant cells to capture light energy.”

Compare with evidence

Both models contain a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus. The typical plant-cell model also contains a rigid cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole.

Evaluate a model

A coloured diagram is useful because it separates structures clearly, but its colours and relative sizes may not represent what would actually be observed under a microscope.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Zoom from a whole organism to one cell

Explore a plant or animal at different scales to understand that organs contain tissues and tissues contain microscopic cells.

E2: Match cell structures to their functions

Identify the nucleus, cell membrane, cell wall, cytoplasm, chloroplasts and vacuoles and explain the job each performs.

E3: Examine real cell evidence

Use light or digital microscopes, simulations and photomicrographs to examine different cells, including single-celled organisms.

E4: Compare plant and animal cells

Use microscope evidence and physical or digital models to identify similarities and differences without assuming every cell looks identical.

E5: Design and evaluate a cell model

Explain what a model represents well and what it simplifies, omits or shows out of scale.

E6: How microscopes changed cell science

Explain how microscopes made previously invisible structures observable and provided evidence that improved scientific understanding.

Common misconceptions: Myth vs Fact

Myth: Plant cells have a cell wall instead of a cell membrane.

Fact: Plant cells have both. The cell membrane sits inside the cell wall and regulates movement of substances.

Myth: Every plant cell contains chloroplasts.

Fact: Chloroplasts are common in photosynthetic tissues such as leaves. Many root cells do not contain them.

Myth: Microscope images should look like colourful textbook diagrams.

Fact: Diagrams use colour and simplified shapes to communicate structures. Microscope observations may be faint and less distinct.

Myth: The biggest structure in a diagram must be biggest in the real cell.

Fact: Unless a model is explicitly drawn to scale, its proportions cannot be used as measurements.
