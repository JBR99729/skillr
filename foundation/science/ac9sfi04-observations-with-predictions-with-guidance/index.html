FOUNDATION SCIENCE · AC9SFI04

What did you expect? What did you notice?

With guidance, compare your original prediction with your observation. Describe what matched and what differed.

Learn the skill

1. Find the prediction

An adult helps you read or recall what you expected before the event. Keep that record unchanged.

2. Check the same feature

Look at what was actually observed. Compare the feature named in the prediction. If the record lacks that feature, you need more information.

3. Say what matched or differed

Put prediction and observation side by side. An adult can help compare one part at a time.

Common mistakes

“A different prediction means I failed.” A prediction can differ from evidence. Explain what you learned.

“I'll erase what I predicted.” Keep it. The two records let you compare honestly.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Before-and-after detectives

2 minutes An adult provides Prediction / Observation / Comparison boxes. 3 minutes Predict a visible feature on a safely accessible garden plant. 3 minutes Observe and draw that feature. 2 minutes Compare with adult guidance.

Adjust the challenge Support: compare one feature with same/different choices. Extend: compare two predicted features separately.

Observe without picking unfamiliar plants. Follow adult boundaries and sun protection.

Three tricky questions

1. You predicted two birds and observed two birds. What matched?

The number of birds matched: two predicted and two observed during that visit.

2. You predicted a flower, but only recorded leaf colour. Has the prediction been disproved?

No. You do not yet have the observation needed to compare the flower prediction.

3. Only one part of a prediction matched. Must we call the whole thing right or wrong?

No. With guidance, say which part matched and which differed.

FAQ

Can an observation differ from a good prediction?

Yes. Keep both and use the observation to update your thinking.

Do children compare without help?

This code includes guidance. Spoken responses, drawings and adult scribing are appropriate.

🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: After an investigation, compare the prediction with the observation. A different result is useful because it helps us update our thinking.

Success looks like: Recall prediction; State observation; Compare same/different; Use evidence; Update thinking.

Learning routine: Recall prediction → Observe result → Compare → Say same/different → Learn

Explain, check and applyReason

Keep both parts of the comparison

With adult guidance, put the original prediction beside the observation. Say what was expected, what was actually noticed and whether they match. A different result is useful; do not rewrite the prediction to make it correct.

Compare the same feature

Prediction: the plant will have a flower. Observation: the plant has green leaves. Is that enough to decide whether the prediction matched?

Answer and teaching guidance

Not yet. The observation does not say whether a flower was present. Look at the plant and record the feature named in the prediction before comparing.

A different result is still evidence

Before a garden visit, a child predicts three birds on a branch. They observe one bird. What should go in the comparison?

Answer and teaching guidance

Predicted three birds; observed one bird; different. Keep both records. Do not add imagined birds to the observation or erase the original prediction.

Some parts can match

Prediction: the plant will have four leaves and a flower. Observation: four leaves and no flower. What matched, and what differed?

Answer and teaching guidance

The leaf count matched. The flower prediction did not match. With guidance, compare each feature separately rather than calling the whole prediction right or wrong.

Ready to move on

Use the supplied sentence: “I predicted ____. I observed ____. They matched/differed because ____.” Prediction: two birds. Observation: two birds.

Answer and teaching guidance

The number matched: two were predicted and two observed. Accept a spoken or drawn response with adult recording. For a new example, change the observation and compare again.

👀 Model and worked examplesLearn

Prediction and observation have different jobs

Prediction: “The large marble will roll farther.”
Observation: “The small marble rolled farther.”
Compare: The observation was different from the prediction.

Think aloud: Describe what is observed first, then use that evidence to explain the idea.

Use evidence, not embarrassment

We do not change the prediction after the test. We keep it, record the observation and explain whether they matched.

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Changing the old prediction: Keep the original prediction so the comparison is honest.
  • Calling a wrong prediction a failure: A different result still gives useful evidence.
  • Comparing without stating the observation: Say what actually happened before deciding whether it matched.

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E3

Content description: compare observations with predictions with guidance

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

E1

revisiting their predictions and with guidance identifying whether their predictions matched their observations

Teaching example: Place the original prediction beside the observation. With guidance, compare the named feature and keep both records even when they differ.

E2

comparing, with guidance, observations of plants or animals made during field work with their predictions

Teaching example: Before and after a garden visit, record the predicted and observed number of birds. Compare only what was actually observed during the stated visit.

E3

using a provided table to draw or dictate their prediction and their observation and identifying whether they are the same or different

Teaching example: Provide Prediction / Observation / Match or different headings. Children draw or dictate each entry; help them explain the comparison using the recorded feature.
