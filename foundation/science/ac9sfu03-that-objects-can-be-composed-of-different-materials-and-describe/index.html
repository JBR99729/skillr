AC9SFU03: Objects and Materials

recognise that objects can be composed of different materials and describe the observable properties of those materials

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
Learning goals

Learning intention: Objects can be made from one or more materials. Materials can be described using observable properties such as hard, soft, smooth, rough, bendy, stiff, shiny or dull.

  • Name common materials.
  • Describe observable properties.
  • Compare two materials.
  • Distinguish an object from the material it is made from.
  • Explain why a material may suit a particular use.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should be able to name familiar classroom and household objects, use simple describing words and compare two things by what they can see or feel. Encourage safe observation rather than guessing.

Key concept

Object, material and property are different ideas. A chair is an object; wood may be one material; hard and stiff are observable properties. One object can contain more than one material.

object

a thing made or used for a purpose

material

what an object is made from

property

a feature that can be observed or tested

compare

notice similarities and differences

Worked teaching sequence and checks

One object, several materials

Look closely at a pencil with an eraser. Name two materials. Describe one property you can observe.

Show answer and teaching guidance

Possible materials: wood, graphite, metal and rubber. Possible observations: the wooden body is stiff; the rubber eraser is softer than the metal holder. Use the actual pencil: some pencils have different parts.

Sort by what you observe

Place a fabric scrap, wooden block and metal spoon on a tray. Look and gently feel. Sort them by one property, such as flexible or stiff. Explain your rule.

Show answer and teaching guidance

For these samples, the fabric may bend easily while the block and spoon stay stiff. Accept a different grouping when supported by what the child observes. Colour alone does not identify a material.

Look closer and record

Look at a fabric scrap with your eyes, then through a magnifying glass. Draw what you notice. Tell a partner what the magnifying glass helped you see.

Show answer and teaching guidance

Accept observed detail such as threads, gaps or a woven pattern. Record what is visible in this sample; do not require every fabric to look the same.

Important questions and answers

Is “spoon” a material? Can two spoons be made from different materials?

Show answer and teaching guidance

Spoon names an object. Spoons can be made from materials such as metal, wood or plastic. Inspect examples rather than assuming every spoon is metal.

Can two materials share a property?

Show answer and teaching guidance

Yes. A wooden block and a metal spoon can both be hard. One property does not uniquely identify a material.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Choose two safe objects. Name a material in each. Say one property they share and one difference you observe.

Review hint: “This is a ____. It is made from ____. I notice ____.”

Show answer and teaching guidance

Answers depend on the objects. Check that the child distinguishes object names, materials and observable properties. Accept pointing and spoken descriptions.

Exit ticket

Look at the pencil. Name two materials and describe one property you can observe. Point to the part you mean.

Show answer and teaching guidance

For this pictured pencil: wood and rubber; the wood is stiff, or the rubber eraser is soft. Accept other accurate observations and adapt to the actual pencil. Reteach with object → material → property if the child gives only object names.

Worked examples

Object → material → property

chair → wood → hard / stiff
cloth → fabric → soft / bendy
spoon → metal → hard / shiny

Match a material to a use

towel → absorbent fabric
window → transparent glass
raincoat → water-resistant material

Use an observable or safely testable property to explain the choice.

Questions and answers
What is an object?
A thing made or used for a purpose.
What is a material?
What an object is made from.
What is a property?
A feature of a material that can be observed or safely tested.
Can one object contain several materials?
Yes. Many everyday objects combine materials because different parts need different properties.
Common misconceptions
  • Object = material: name the object first, then what it is made from.
  • Using only colour: also use properties such as hard, soft, rough, smooth, bendy or stiff.
  • Guessing without observing: look, touch or use a safe simple test before describing the property.
Guided practice
  1. Find three classroom objects. Name each object, its material and one property.
  2. Compare wood and fabric using two property words.
  3. Choose a suitable material for a towel, spoon or raincoat and explain why.
Independent practice
  1. Name one material used to make classroom objects.
  2. Which word describes a property: soft or chair?
  3. Find an object made from more than one material and name both materials.
  4. Choose two objects and compare the materials they are made from.
Reasoning and problem-solving

You need to choose a material for a lunchbox that should be light, stiff and easy to wipe clean. Compare two possible materials and explain which one you would choose using observable properties.

Practice and review
  1. Look at a classroom object. Name the object, one material and one property.
  2. Explain why fabric is more suitable than glass for a towel.
  3. Give an example of an object made from two materials and explain why both may be useful.

Review hint: use the sentence pattern “The object is ____. It is made from ____. The material is ____.”

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Use real objects and safe observation. Ask students to say the object, material and property as three separate ideas. For First Nations elaborations, use locally appropriate community-approved sources and respect cultural authority.

For parents and carers

Choose safe household objects and ask, “What is it? What is it made from? What can you observe about the material?”

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

observing and manipulating objects to identify the materials they are made of and recognising that some objects are made of more than one type of material

Teaching example: Inspect a pencil with an eraser. Point to its wooden body, graphite core, metal holder and rubber eraser where present. Explain that one object can contain several materials.

E2

recognising that tools such as magnifying glasses enable more-detailed observations

Teaching example: Observe a fabric scrap with the eyes and then with a magnifying glass. Draw a detail that becomes easier to see, such as threads or gaps.

E3

sorting and grouping materials based on observed properties such as colour, hardness, texture and flexibility

Teaching example: Sort a fabric scrap, wooden block and metal spoon by an observed property. State the sorting rule, then try a different rule.

E4

creating a display of different materials, naming each material and exploring language to describe properties of materials

Teaching example: Make a small labelled display: object, material and an observed property. Invite a partner to check each label against the sample.

E5

using a digital camera to collect images of objects on a materials scavenger hunt

Teaching example: Photograph three safe classroom objects. Add a material label and one observed property to each picture; avoid photographing people.

E6

suggesting why different parts of everyday objects, such as saucepans and clothing, are made from different materials

Teaching example: Compare the wooden body and rubber eraser of a pencil. Discuss why a stiff body is useful for holding and why an eraser needs different properties.

E7

investigating the ways in which First Nations Australians make utensils for different purposes by combining different materials

Teaching example: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved example of a utensil made by named First Nations Peoples. Follow the source when identifying its materials and purpose, and respect permissions.
