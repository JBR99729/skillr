FOUNDATION SCIENCE · AC9SFI05

Show what you noticed. Tell your story.

Share questions, predictions, observations and ideas. Use a clear record, listen and respond to others.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Help a partner understand the evidence

Question: Will the tube roll on its curved side?
Prediction: I think it will, because a similar tube rolled before.
Observation: It rolled down our low ramp.
Idea: Its curved side may help it roll. I can show how we placed it.

Tell which part was a question, prediction or observation. Show the object or matching record so the listener can understand, then respond to their questions.

Learn the skill

1. Choose what to share

You can share by speaking, drawing, pointing, modelling or using a photograph. Match the representation to the observation.

Choose the evidence

You noticed three holes in a leaf. You have an accurate drawing and an unrelated colourful picture.

Try it · Which would help explain the holes?

The accurate drawing. Point to its three holes. Decoration does not replace evidence.

2. Make the message clear

Keep what you expected separate from what happened. Point to the part of your record you mean.

A clear comparison

“I thought I would find three leaves, but I observed two.”

3. Listen and respond

Give your partner time to speak. Ask a question about their evidence. You can safely demonstrate or point to help them understand.

Common mistakes

“Just give the final answer.” Show or describe the observation that supports the idea.

“Sharing means only talking.” Listening, pointing, demonstrating and responding also help communicate.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Science show and tell

2 minutes Choose a real observation and matching drawing or object. 5 minutes Tell a partner what you wondered, expected and noticed; the partner asks a question. Swap roles. 3 minutes Add a label or demonstration to make each message clearer.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “I noticed …” and point. Extend: build a three-box Question / Prediction / Observation storyboard.

An adult selects safe objects. Ask before using a camera and avoid photographing people without permission.

Three tricky questions

1. A child predicted three leaves but observed two. Can they report only “There were three”?

That confuses prediction with observation. Say, “I predicted three; I observed two.”

2. A model leaf is much larger than the real leaf. Can it still help explain the veins?

Yes, if the child explains what the model represents. A model can show a feature without matching the real size.

3. A partner does not understand the result. Should you invent more detail?

No. Point to the actual record, add an accurate label or safely demonstrate. Say when you do not know.

FAQ

Is a long written report needed?

No. Children can speak, point, draw, model or dictate their ideas.

Can we share a nature experience from home?

Yes. Describe what you actually noticed, show a relevant record if available and separate questions from observations.

🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: Scientists share what they asked, predicted and observed so other people can understand the investigation and discuss the evidence.

Success looks like: Share a question; Share a prediction; Share an observation; Use evidence; Listen and respond.

Learning routine: Think → Choose evidence → Say/show → Listen → Respond

Explain, check and applyReason

Make the evidence understandable

Share what you asked, predicted, observed or think. Point to a drawing, record or object that helps the listener follow. Foundation children can speak, point, draw or model; a long written report is not required.

Separate prediction from observation

A child predicted three leaves but observed two. They tell a partner only, “There were three.” How could they make the message accurate?

Answer and teaching guidance

Say, “I thought there would be three leaves, but I observed two.” Show the observation record so the partner can see which number describes the result.

Choose a useful representation

You observed a leaf with three holes. Would an accurate labelled drawing or an unrelated colourful picture help a partner understand? Why?

Answer and teaching guidance

The labelled drawing shows the observed leaf and holes. Useful evidence matters more than decoration. Do not add details that were not observed.

Listen and ask about the evidence

A partner says, “This toy rolled.” What could you ask to understand the observation better?

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, “Can you show me how it moved?” or “What surface was it on?” Listen to the response and refer to what the partner actually shared.

Ready to move on

Show a simple observation record to a partner. Say what you noticed, then listen and respond to one question.

Answer and teaching guidance

Look for an accurate observation, a matching record and a relevant response. If the listener is confused, add a label, point to a feature or demonstrate safely rather than inventing a new result.

Use a picture, object, words or simple record

Foundation students can share by speaking, pointing to a drawing, showing a simple table or demonstrating with an object.

3. Listen and respond

Listen to a partner, then say one thing you heard or one question you still have.

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Only saying the answer: Share the observation or evidence that supports the idea.
  • Mixing prediction and result: Use separate sentences for what you thought and what you observed.
  • Not listening to others: Science sharing includes listening and responding respectfully.

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E7

Content description: share questions, predictions, observations and ideas with others

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

sharing questions, making predictions and describing observations to others through discussions and circle groups

Teaching example: In a circle discussion, share a question, prediction or observation with a matching object or drawing. Invite a partner to ask about the evidence.

E2

recounting stories and posing questions about their own experiences learning about the natural world, such as when gardening or observing plants and animals at home or visits to conservation areas or centres

Teaching example: Recount a real gardening or nature experience and distinguish what was noticed from what the child still wants to find out.

E3

acknowledging and exploring First Nations Australians’ ways of communicating information about anatomical features of plants and animals

Teaching example: Use an authorised example from named Peoples and Country/Place to discuss how plant or animal features are communicated. Respect permissions and do not reproduce restricted designs or assign invented meanings.

E4

showing or describing how objects can be moved in different ways and responding to questions

Teaching example: Safely demonstrate a toy rolling or sliding, describe the movement and respond to a partner’s question about what happened.

E5

role-playing or showing how people use different equipment to make scientific observations

Teaching example: Role-play using a magnifying glass or camera, naming what the tool helps observe and showing a relevant record.

E6

representing external features of animals and plants using a range of materials such as blocks, modelling clay, craft materials or paper

Teaching example: Make a simple model of an observed plant or animal feature. Point to the represented part and explain what the model shows and leaves out.

E7

communicating questions, predictions and observations using posters, collages, digital displays, drawings or storyboards

Teaching example: Use a provided storyboard with Question / Prediction / Observation boxes. Draw or dictate each part and check that the observation matches the actual evidence.
