FOUNDATION SCIENCE · AC9SFI03

Record it. Find what goes together.

Use a provided template to show observations. With guidance, look for shared features or repeated results.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

What do these movement records show?

Example observations on the same floor
ObjectObserved movement
Large ballRolled
Toy carRolled on its wheels

The ball and toy car both rolled in these observations. The block slid. Point to the records that support your statement. This table does not show that every object rolls.

Learn the skill

1. Use the headings

An adult provides the template. Check what each row or box asks you to record. Pictures, marks and dictated words can all help.

Object | Material

Your observations are spoon—metal and block—wood.

ObjectMaterial
SpoonMetal
BlockWood
Try it · Where should “wood” go?

In the Material box beside block. Keep the object and its material linked.

2. Record what you noticed

Put each observation in its matching place. Do not change a result to make the record look neat or match an expectation.

Check a copied entry

You observed the ball roll. Its row accidentally says “slid”.

Actual observationCorrect row
Ball rolledBall → rolled
Try it · What should you change?

Correct the copying error to “rolled” so the record agrees with the actual observation.

3. Look across the record

With an adult, find a shared feature or repeated movement. Say which entries support it. Different results belong in the record too.

Use the worked table

Ball—rolled; toy car—rolled; block—slid.

Toy carRolled

Try it · Which two share a movement?

The ball and toy car both rolled in these observations. The block did something different.

Common mistakes

“Everything must fit the pattern.” Keep all accurate observations, including different results.

“A nice-looking display is enough.” Its pictures, labels and links must match what was observed.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make a movement record

2 minutes An adult draws Object / Movement headings and chooses three safe toys. 5 minutes Observe each on a clear floor and fill its row. 3 minutes Point to a shared movement or explain a difference.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose from roll and slide picture labels. Extend: use a second provided template to group animal cards by a visible feature.

Use large safe toys and adult guidance. Record the trials that actually occur.

Three tricky questions

1. Two objects rolled and one slid. Can we write “all three rolled”?

No. The block's different result must stay in the record.

2. A row is empty. Does that mean the object stayed still?

No. An empty box means there is no recorded result. Check the observation or repeat safely with an adult.

3. A metal spoon and metal key are in your display. Must every spoon and key be metal?

No. The display describes these samples. Other spoons or keys may use different materials.

FAQ

Does a pattern have to be stripes or repeated colours?

No. In science records you can also notice shared features or repeated kinds of movement.

Must children make their own tables?

No. Adults provide the template and help children identify patterns.

🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: We can record observations using pictures, marks, words or simple tables. Looking across the records can help us notice a pattern.

Success looks like: Record observations; Use a provided template; Compare records; Notice a simple pattern; Support pattern with evidence.

Learning routine: Observe → Record → Look across results → Notice pattern → Describe

Explain, check and applyReason

Use the supplied structure

Record what was actually observed in the given boxes or rows. Use drawings, marks or words. With guidance, compare the completed records to identify a shared feature or a pattern in what happened.

Fill the correct group

Use two headings: feathers / no feathers. Cards show a duck with feathers, a pigeon with feathers and a dog with fur. Where does each card go?

Answer and teaching guidance

Duck and pigeon go under feathers; dog goes under no feathers. The feature named in the headings determines the grouping.

Keep the record accurate

The observation says a ball rolled, but its row says slid. Should you keep the entry because it makes the rows look alike?

Answer and teaching guidance

No. Correct the copying error to rolled so the record matches the observation. Do not change a genuine result simply to create an expected pattern.

Describe only what the records support

A table records a ball rolling, a toy car rolling and a block sliding. What do the first two have in common? Can you say every object rolls?

Answer and teaching guidance

The ball and toy car both rolled in these observations. Not every object rolled: the block slid. Point to the entries that support both parts of the answer.

Ready to move on

Use the provided headings Object / Material. Observations: spoon—metal; key—metal; block—wood. Fill the rows, then identify a shared material.

Answer and teaching guidance

The spoon and key are both recorded as metal; the block is wood. This pattern concerns these samples, not every spoon, key or block.

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Changing the record after seeing the answer: Record what was actually observed at the time.
  • Pattern not supported by data: Point to at least two observations that show the pattern.
  • Too much detail: Use the provided simple template and record the important feature only.

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E5

Content description: represent observations in provided templates and identify patterns with guidance

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

E1

using provided tables or graphic organisers to sort images or models of plants and animals into groups based on external features

Teaching example: Supply headings feathers and no feathers. Help children place observed animal pictures consistently and explain the shared feature.

E2

collaborating to create a floor or wall display to link images or samples of materials to images of observed objects

Teaching example: Provide Object and Material headings. Link samples or labelled drawings to the objects actually observed rather than guessing their materials.

E3

identifying common features of familiar groups of animals, such as fish, birds or reptiles

Teaching example: Compare the observed bird cards for a shared covering of feathers. Keep the statement tied to visible features in the examples.

E4

identifying patterns of movement of objects, with guidance, such as that balls roll easily in a straight line when pushed, or toy cars move in certain ways because of their wheels

Teaching example: Provide an object-and-movement table for a ball, block and toy car. Record the actual trials and identify any repeated movement with guidance.

E5

identifying patterns in uses of everyday objects made of similar materials, such as wood, plastic, metal or glass

Teaching example: Compare recorded material examples such as a metal spoon and key. Identify shared material use without claiming that every such object uses that material.
