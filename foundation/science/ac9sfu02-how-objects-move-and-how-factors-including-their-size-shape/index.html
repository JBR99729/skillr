FOUNDATION SCIENCE · AC9SFU02

Roll, slide, spin or bounce?

Describe how objects move. Explore how their size, shape and material can affect what happens.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One tube, two starting positions

Put the same empty tube on a low ramp in each position, with adult help. On its curved side it may roll. On a flat end it may slide, tip or stay still. Describe what actually happens. The tube is the same; the part touching the ramp changes.

Learn the skill

1. Name the movement

Watch what the object does. Rolling turns it over and over; sliding moves it along without rolling. Spinning turns it around; bouncing takes it up again after it hits a surface.

Observe a safe toy

A toy car moves along on turning wheels. A flat block moves along without turning over.

Try it · Which movement describes the block?

Sliding. The block moves along without rolling over.

2. Compare shape and size

A curved surface can help an object roll. Compare different-sized balls with adult help. Keep the starting place and surface alike; notice any other differences too.

Same shape, different size

A small rubber ball and a large foam ball differ in both size and material.

3. Explore the material

Compare similar-sized balls made from different materials on the same surface. An adult releases each from the same low height. Watch and repeat.

Common mistakes

“A ball can only roll.” The same ball can roll, slide, spin or bounce in different situations.

“Bigger always goes farther.” Compare actual results. Shape, material, surface and the start can also matter.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Movement detectives

2 minutes Choose a large ball, block and empty tube with an adult. 5 minutes Move each on the same clear floor area, then try the tube in two positions. 3 minutes Name the movements and point to a feature that may matter.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose between roll and slide. Extend: group toys by observed movement, then try a new starting position.

Use large objects, a low ramp and a clear space. Avoid small balls, stairs and throwing at people.

Three tricky questions

1. A toy rolls in one trial and slides in another. Is one observation wrong?

Not necessarily. An object can move in different ways. Check what actually happened in each trial.

2. Two balls are different sizes and made from different materials. Can we say size alone explains their bouncing?

No. Both features differ. Describe the result without choosing one cause from this comparison.

3. The tube does not move on a flat end. Should we write “it slid” because we expected it?

No. Write or say that it stayed still in that trial.

FAQ

Does faster tell us the kind of movement?

No. Also say whether the object rolled, slid, spun or bounced.

Do children need to measure speed?

No. Naming, comparing and describing observed movement is enough here. Adults guide the comparisons.

🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: Objects can move in different ways such as rolling, sliding, spinning and bouncing. Their size, shape and material can affect how they move.

Success looks like: Name movement types; Notice shape effects; Compare fairly; Change one feature; Describe evidence.

Learning routine: Observe → Move → Describe → Change one feature → Compare

Explain, check and applyReason

Describe the motion you observe

Use roll, slide, spin or bounce to describe what happens, not only fast or slow. An object can move in more than one way. Observe each trial rather than assigning one permanent movement to an object.

Same object, different starting position

With adult guidance, put an empty tube on its curved side on a low ramp, then try it resting on a flat end. What changes?

Answer and teaching guidance

On its curved side it may roll; on a flat end it may slide, remain still or tip. Record the actual result. Its material has not changed, but how its shape meets the surface has changed.

Use the evidence, not a size rule

Two balls of different sizes are released from the same low ramp. The smaller one travels farther in this trial. Does the bigger ball always travel farther?

Answer and teaching guidance

No. This result does not support “bigger always goes farther”. Other differences, such as material, can matter too. Describe these objects and this trial without claiming size alone caused the result.

Compare materials with guidance

Compare similar-sized rubber and foam balls on the same surface. Release each from the same low height without pushing. What could you observe?

Answer and teaching guidance

Observe whether and how they bounce, and which rises higher in repeated trials. Keep the start and surface alike; report the result rather than assuming every rubber or foam ball behaves identically.

Ready to move on

Move two safe objects. Name how each moves and describe one visible feature that may help explain the difference.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, a round ball rolls while a block slides on a flat face. Accept actual observations. Formal force laws, speed calculations and independent experimental design are not required.

🧠 Try it in different waysApply

1. Roll or slide?

Test a marble and a block on the same surface. Describe how each moves.

2. Ramp test

Release two objects from the same starting point on a simple ramp. Compare the movement.

3. Change one feature

Change only size, shape or material and say what changed about the movement.

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Changing two things at once: Change only one feature so the comparison is fair.
  • Only saying fast or slow: Also describe the kind of movement: roll, slide, spin or bounce.
  • Assuming bigger always moves farther: Observe and compare instead of guessing from size alone.

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E5

Content description: describe how objects move and how factors including their size, shape or material influence their movement

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

observing how toys move, and grouping them based on their movement

Teaching example: Observe safe toys in motion and group by what happened, such as rolling or sliding. Allow a toy to show different movements in different trials.

E2

observing and describing ways different and unusually shaped objects such as blocks, tubes or eggs move when rolled down a slope

Teaching example: Try an empty tube on its curved side and on a flat end on a low ramp. Describe actual rolling, sliding, tipping or remaining still.

E3

comparing the way different-sized, similar-shaped objects such as tennis balls, golf balls, marbles or basketballs roll and bounce

Teaching example: Compare different-sized balls with adult guidance. Keep the release point and surface alike, and notice when material also differs so size alone cannot explain the result.

E4

exploring how the material a ball is made from affects the way it moves, such as plastic, foam, cloth or rubber balls on a surface

Teaching example: Release similar-sized balls of different materials from the same low height onto the same surface. Compare observed bouncing and repeat before describing a pattern.

E5

exploring how the size and shape of traditional instructive toys used by First Nations Australians influence their movement

Teaching example: Use an authorised account of a named traditional instructive toy. Observe or discuss its documented movement and respect local permissions rather than inventing cultural designs.
