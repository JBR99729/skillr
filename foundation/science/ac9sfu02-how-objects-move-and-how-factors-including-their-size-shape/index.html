AC9SFU02: How Objects Move

describe how objects move and how factors including their size, shape or material influence their movement

What students learn in AC9SFU02

This unit helps students build a clear, usable understanding of Describe how objects move and how factors including their size, shape or material influence their movement. The goal is not to memorise one answer pattern. Students should be able to explain the idea, recognise it in a new example and apply it in a short practice or worksheet task.

  • Start with the main idea: describe how objects move and how factors including their size, shape or material influence their movement.
  • Use concrete examples, pictures, oral explanation and short written responses before moving to independent practice.
  • observing how toys move, and grouping them based on their movement observing and describing ways different and unusually shaped objects such as blocks, tubes or eggs move when rolled down a slope
  • Finish with a mixed activity so students must choose the correct strategy rather than copy the last example.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: describe how objects move and how factors including their size, shape or material influence their movement
  • E1: observing how toys move, and grouping them based on their movement
  • E2: observing and describing ways different and unusually shaped objects such as blocks, tubes or eggs move when rolled down a slope
  • E3: comparing the way different-sized, similar-shaped objects such as tennis balls, golf balls, marbles or basketballs roll and bounce
  • E4: exploring how the material a ball is made from affects the way it moves, such as plastic, foam, cloth or rubber balls on a surface
  • E5: exploring how the size and shape of traditional instructive toys used by First Nations Australians influence their movement (teaching context)

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Describing what is seen without connecting it to a question, pattern or prediction.
  • Using everyday words only and missing simple scientific vocabulary.
  • Changing more than one thing in an investigation, which makes the comparison unfair.
