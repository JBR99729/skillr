FOUNDATION SCIENCE · AC9SFH01

Notice. Wonder. Look again.

Explore how people use observations and questions to learn about the natural world.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

What can this leaf tell us?

Observation: I can see holes in the leaf.
Question: What might have made the holes?
Next step: Look for more evidence, with adult help.

The holes are visible evidence. “An insect made them” is a possible explanation. The picture alone does not show the cause.

Learn the skill

1. Notice with a purpose

People look, listen and use safe senses to learn about nature. A magnifying glass can reveal detail; binoculars can help observe a distant bird.

Choose a tool

You want to examine the tiny lines on a leaf without removing or damaging it.

Try it · What might help you see detail?

A magnifying glass, used with adult guidance. Never look at the Sun through it.

2. Ask a connected question

An observation tells what someone noticed. A question says what they want to find out. A question can lead to another observation.

Observation → question

A gardener sees a seedling with four leaves. They ask, “Will it have more leaves next week?”

Observed nowWant to find out
4 leavesMore next week?
Try it · Is “The seedling has four leaves” a question?

No. It reports an observation. “Will it have more leaves?” asks something to find out.

3. Keep a useful record

People use drawings, photographs, notes and sound recordings to compare observations and share them. Scientists and citizen scientists can return to look again.

A dated plant record

A drawing shows four leaves today. Another drawing next week will show what was observed then.

Try it · Why add the date and use the same plant?

So people know when and what was observed and can make a useful comparison.

Common mistakes

“I like this leaf” is an observation. That tells a preference. “It has two holes” describes visible evidence.

“I know the cause from one look.” Separate what you noticed from an explanation that needs more evidence.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Be a nature observer

2 minutes Choose an adult-approved natural object. 5 minutes Look closely, draw one detail and ask a connected question. 3 minutes Explain how a tool or another visit could help.

Adjust the challenge Support: an adult records the child's words. Extend: compare a drawing and a photograph and say what each helps preserve.

Observe without picking unfamiliar plants or disturbing animals. Follow outdoor boundaries and sun protection.

Three tricky questions

1. A leaf has holes. Does that prove an insect ate it?

No. Holes are observed; the cause needs more evidence.

2. A naturalist returns to the same plant next week. Why keep today's drawing?

It preserves today's observed details for comparison with the later visit.

3. Do only professional scientists ask questions about nature?

No. Gardeners, families, naturalists and citizen scientists also use observations and questions to learn.

FAQ

What is the natural world?

Living things and non-living things in nature, including plants, animals, rocks and water.

How can we explore different knowledge traditions?

Use an authorised source from named First Nations Peoples and Country/Place, with adult guidance. Discuss the observations shown and respect the source's permissions. See the detailed curriculum notes below.

🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

Success looks like: Make observations; Describe evidence; Ask useful questions; Use simple tools; Connect questions to observations.

Learning routine: Notice → Observe closely → Describe → Ask → Learn more

Explain, check and applyReason

People use observations to learn

A gardener notices leaf edges have changed and asks what might be happening. Careful observations, questions and records help people learn about nature. The first observation may not tell them the cause.

Observation or explanation?

A child sees a leaf with holes and says, “An insect ate it.” What was directly observed, and what is still an idea to investigate?

Answer and teaching guidance

The holes were observed. An insect eating the leaf is a possible explanation, not something established by the holes alone. Ask what more the child could observe.

Choose a useful record

A naturalist wants to compare the same seedling today and next week. How could a record help?

Answer and teaching guidance

A dated drawing or photograph showing the leaves gives something to compare later. Include actual visible detail and identify the same plant; memory alone may miss changes.

Look closer with a purpose

A child notices tiny marks on a leaf. What tool could help, and what question could they ask?

Answer and teaching guidance

A magnifying glass may show the marks more clearly. A connected question is “What do the marks look like close up?” Observe without damaging the plant; use tools with adult guidance.

Ready to move on

Describe something a person could observe outdoors, ask a connected question and choose a way to record it.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example: “There are two birds on the branch. Do more visit later?” A dated drawing or photograph records the sighting. Accept other linked examples grounded in what is actually observed.

Observation and question are different

Observation: “The seedling has 4 leaves.”
Question: “Will it grow more leaves next week?”

Think aloud: Describe what is observed first, then use that evidence to explain the idea.

eyes + magnifying glass + simple photos → closer observation

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Opinion instead of observation: Use words that describe what can be seen, heard, felt or otherwise safely sensed.
  • Question with no link to evidence: Start from something you observed first.
  • Guessing instead of looking: Observe before deciding what is happening.

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E5

Content description: explore the ways people make and use observations and questions to learn about the natural world

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

using their senses to make observations and exploring how scientists use their senses as well as equipment to make observations

Teaching example: Compare what can be seen on a leaf unaided and with a magnifying glass. Explain which extra detail the tool helped reveal.

E2

viewing examples of observations such as rock paintings, bark drawings, age-appropriate written reports, labelled drawings or photographs to explore ways they can make and record observations

Teaching example: Compare a labelled drawing and a photograph of the same natural object. Discuss what each record preserves; use permissioned cultural records in their proper context.

E3

exploring how First Nations Australians gain knowledge about the land and its vital resources, such as water and food, through observation

Teaching example: Use a community-approved account from named Peoples and Country/Place to discuss how careful observations inform local knowledge of food, water or land. Do not generalise one account to all communities.

E4

interacting with stories or documentaries about scientists such as Dame Jane Goodall or Sir Joseph Banks and noticing the ways they make their observations such as through drawings, collections, sound recordings and photography and how they ask questions about what they think they will observe and find

Teaching example: Use an age-appropriate account of a scientist already named in the curriculum. Identify the observations, questions and recording methods actually shown in that source.

E5

watching an age-appropriate documentary; noticing how people including scientists, engineers, naturalists or citizen scientists ask questions; and posing their own questions

Teaching example: Pause an appropriate nature documentary when someone asks a question. Identify the observation behind it and suggest one related question children could explore safely.
