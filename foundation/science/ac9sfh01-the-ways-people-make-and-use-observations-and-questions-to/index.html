AC9SFH01: Explore the ways people make and use observations and questions to learn about the natural world

explore the ways people make and use observations and questions to learn about the natural world

Start lessonTeacher slidesWorksheetPracticeTest
🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

Success looks like: Make observations; Describe evidence; Ask useful questions; Use simple tools; Connect questions to observations.

Learning routine: Notice → Observe closely → Describe → Ask → Learn more

👀 Model and worked examplesLearn

Observation and question are different

Observation: “The seedling has 4 leaves.”
Question: “Will it grow more leaves next week?”

Think aloud: Describe what is observed first, then use that evidence to explain the idea.

Use tools to notice more detail

eyes + magnifying glass + simple photos → closer observation

Describe what you actually notice before asking what you want to find out.

📚 Key words4 words

observation

something noticed carefully

question

something we ask to learn more

natural world

living and non-living things in nature

evidence

what we observe that supports an idea

🧠 Try it in different waysApply

1. Nature notice

Choose a leaf, rock or plant. Say three things you can actually observe.

2. Turn noticing into a question

Use one observation to make a question beginning with what, how, which or will.

3. Look again

Use a magnifying glass or close photo. Add one new detail to your observation.

⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Opinion instead of observation: Use words that describe what can be seen, heard, felt or otherwise safely sensed.
  • Question with no link to evidence: Start from something you observed first.
  • Guessing instead of looking: Observe before deciding what is happening.
✅ Quick checkCheck
  • What is an observation?
  • Turn “The leaf has spots” into a science question.
  • How can a magnifying glass help?
  • Why should we observe before we explain?

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E5

Content description: explore the ways people make and use observations and questions to learn about the natural world

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

using their senses to make observations and exploring how scientists use their senses as well as equipment to make observations

Learning connection: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

E2

viewing examples of observations such as rock paintings, bark drawings, age-appropriate written reports, labelled drawings or photographs to explore ways they can make and record observations

Learning connection: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

E3

exploring how First Nations Australians gain knowledge about the land and its vital resources, such as water and food, through observation

Learning connection: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

E4

interacting with stories or documentaries about scientists such as Dame Jane Goodall or Sir Joseph Banks and noticing the ways they make their observations such as through drawings, collections, sound recordings and photography and how they ask questions about what they think they will observe and find

Learning connection: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.

E5

watching an age-appropriate documentary; noticing how people including scientists, engineers, naturalists or citizen scientists ask questions; and posing their own questions

Learning connection: Science often begins by noticing something, observing it carefully and asking a question that can help us learn more about the natural world.
