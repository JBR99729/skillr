Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Count reliably through at least 20, keep track of objects, and use matching or counts to explain more, fewer and enough. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Count once and keep the total

Use one number word for each object. The final number tells how many are in the collection.

Worked example: Count a collection of 20 counters.

  1. Touch or move one counter as you say each number.
  2. Keep counted counters separate, or follow an organised path.
  3. After “twenty”, say “There are twenty counters.”

Why it works: One object gets one count word. The last count includes the whole collection.

Watch for: Do not count the tray, an object’s decorative marks or the same counter twice. Name what counts as one object.

Try it: Collect 13 counters. Count them and answer “How many?” without starting again.

Check the answer

There are 13 counters. Check that each counter was included exactly once.

2. Keep track when objects move

A reliable count needs a starting point and a stopping point. Moving the same objects changes their arrangement, not how many there are.

Worked example: Check a ring of 8 counters.

  1. Choose one counter as the start and mark it.
  2. Move around the ring, counting each counter once.
  3. Stop before counting the marked counter a second time. Spread out the same eight and check.

Why it works: A start marker prevents counting around the ring again. No objects were added or removed when the arrangement changed.

Watch for: A wider spread does not automatically mean more. Ask what changed: position or quantity?

Try it: Count 6 blocks. Rearrange them into two rows without adding or removing any. Predict the new total, then check.

Check the answer

The total is still 6. Rearranging changes where the blocks are, not their number.

3. Compare by making pairs

Match each object in one collection to one in the other. Leftover objects show which has more. If no objects are left unmatched, the numbers are equal.

Worked example: Check 5 people and 4 chairs.

  1. Give each chair to one person.
  2. Four people have chairs; one person has none.
  3. There are more people than chairs and fewer chairs than people.

Why it works: One-to-one matching exposes the shortage without needing a subtraction sentence.

Watch for: Do not give one chair to two people when the rule is one chair each. Each match uses one object from each collection.

Try it: Match 7 counters with 7 blocks. Which collection has more?

Check the answer

Neither. All seven make pairs with none unmatched, so they have the same number.

4. Compare recorded counts

You can compare collections by counting each and remembering both totals. Close together and spread out can represent equal amounts.

Worked example: Compare a close row of 8 with a spread-out row of 6.

  1. Count the first row: eight.
  2. Count the second row: six.
  3. Eight is more than six, so the close row has more counters.

Why it works: The counts measure the amounts. Row length depends on spacing as well as number.

Watch for: Do not use picture size or gaps as a substitute for counting. When several collections tie, include every correct tied collection.

Try it: One collection has 16 objects and another has 16 in a different layout. Which has fewer?

Check the answer

Neither has fewer. Both totals are 16.

5. Find enough for a purpose

Enough means everyone’s stated need can be met. Exactly enough means there are no leftovers.

Worked example: Check 6 spoons for 4 people, one spoon each.

  1. Count the people and spoons.
  2. Give one spoon to each of the four people.
  3. Everyone has one and two remain: six is enough, but not exactly enough.

Why it works: Checking the allocation links the count to the practical need.

Watch for: Leftovers do not mean “not enough”. A shortage leaves someone without the required object.

Try it: There are 9 people and 7 forks, one fork needed each. Are there enough?

Check the answer

No. Seven people can receive a fork and two cannot. Two more forks are needed.

For teachers and parents: Start with a manageable collection, then vary quantity and layout separately. Ask children to demonstrate their method. A wrong total alone does not show whether an object was skipped or counted twice.

Ready to try it independently?

Count a new collection, rearrange it without adding or removing objects, then compare it with another collection and explain your decision.

Use fresh objects or examples. Notice what the child can do independently and what needs another model. A correct choice alone may not show the practical action or reasoning.
