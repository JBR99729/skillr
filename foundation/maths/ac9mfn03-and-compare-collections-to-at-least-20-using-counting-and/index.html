🎯 Learn the key ideaStart here

Learning intention: Count every object exactly once, know that the last count tells how many, and compare collections fairly.

Success looks like: Count each object once; Use final count as total; Keep quantity after rearranging; Compare by count or matching; Explain more / fewer / same.

Learning routine: Touch/Move → Count → State total → Match → Compare → Explain

🧠 Try it in different waysApply
  1. Count 5 objects, then 12, then 20. Move each object once.
  2. Compare two collections by matching one object from each.
  3. Rearrange a counted collection. Explain why its total stays the same.
⚠️ Common mistakes and fixesFix errors
  • Double counting: Move counted objects into a finished area.
  • Spread means more: Count the same collection before and after rearranging.
  • Does not state total: After counting, ask “So how many altogether?”

🇦🇺 Australian Curriculum coverageE1–E5

Content description: quantify and compare collections to at least 20 using counting and explain or demonstrate reasoning

These are curriculum teaching examples used as a breadth check, not separate mandatory outcomes.

First Nations teaching guidance: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved source; name the relevant Peoples and Country/Place; respect cultural authority and permissions; and do not present one example as universal across First Nations Australians.

E1

establishing the language and process of counting, understanding that each object must be counted only once, that the arrangement of objects does not affect how many there are, and that the last number counted answers the question of “How many?”; for example, saying numbers in sequence while playing and performing actions

Teaching example: Move 12 counters one at a time into a counted area. Say the total, spread them out and count again. Ask why the total stays 12.

E2

using counting to compare the size of two or more collections of like items to justify which collection contains more or less items

Teaching example: Compare 8 closely spaced counters with 6 widely spaced counters. Match one-to-one, then count. Ask which group has more and how the child knows.

E3

using counting and one-to-one correspondence to quantify the number of items required for a purpose; for example, when asked to collect enough scissors for each member of their group to have a pair, counting each member and using the total count to know how many to collect

Teaching example: Set out 6 toy animals and 5 plates. Match one plate to each animal. Ask how many more plates are needed; add one and check.

E4

discussing how different cultures may have alternative ways of representing the count; for example, discussing how people of the Asia region use an abacus or Chinese hand gestures

Teaching example: With an accurately sourced example of a counting representation from a named culture, ask an adult to demonstrate how it records a small quantity. Compare that same quantity using classroom counters; do not assume one representation is used throughout Asia.

E5

using body-tallying that involves body parts and one-to-one correspondence from counting systems of First Nations Peoples of Australia, to count to 20

Teaching example: Use a locally appropriate, community-approved demonstration of a named body-tallying system. Follow the authorised sequence and guidance; do not invent a generic First Nations counting system.

Open Classroom View
Worked teaching sequence and checks

Count small groups, then build to 20

Start with 5 objects. Move each object once as you count. Say the total. Repeat with 12, then 20 objects; use a counted area to avoid counting an object twice.

Point to every counter. How many are in collection C?

Show answer and teaching guidance

20. Two rows help us keep track, but children may count each counter once. If the total changes, move counted objects into a separate area and try again.

Does a longer row always have more?

Which row has more counters? Show how you know.

Show answer and teaching guidance

A has 8 and B has 6. A has more, even though B stretches farther. Match one counter from each row; two in A remain unmatched.

Are there enough for everyone?

Put out 6 toy animals and 5 plates. Give each animal one plate. Does every animal have a plate? What should we change?

Show answer and teaching guidance

One animal has no plate. Add one plate to make 6. Look for one-to-one matching, rather than guessing from the space the plates occupy.

Important questions and answers

You counted 12 counters. I moved them farther apart without adding or removing any. How many are there now?

Show answer and teaching guidance

12. Moving counters changes their arrangement, not their number. Recount or match to the original collection if the child is unsure.

What does the last number you say when counting tell you?

Show answer and teaching guidance

It tells how many objects are in the whole collection, provided each object was counted once.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Count a new collection of 17 objects. Make a second collection with the same number. Show that they match.

Review hint: move each object once, say the total, then pair the collections.

Show answer and teaching guidance

Both collections contain 17. Accept accurate counting and/or one-to-one matching as evidence; do not require a written explanation.

Exit ticket

Give the child 14 counters. Ask them to count the counters, rearrange them and tell you the total again.

Show answer and teaching guidance

14 before and after. Continue when the child counts once per object and keeps the total after rearranging. If not, return to 5 counters and model moving each counter once.
