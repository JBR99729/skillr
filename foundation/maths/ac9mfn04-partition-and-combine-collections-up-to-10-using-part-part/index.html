Part–Part–Whole to 10

A whole number can be split into parts in different ways, and the parts can be recombined to make the same whole.

What students learn

A whole number can be split into parts in different ways, and the parts can be recombined to make the same whole.

Learning intention: I can a whole number can be split into parts in different ways, and the parts can be recombined to make the same whole.

Success criteria

  • I can identify whole and parts.
  • I can partition a collection.
  • I can recombine to same whole.
  • I can find a missing part.
  • I can show more than one partition.
Key vocabulary
  • whole — the complete group
  • part — a smaller group inside the whole
  • partition — split a whole into parts
  • combine — put parts together

Learning activities
  • Shake and spill two-colour counters; name both parts and the whole.
  • Hide one part under a cup and work out the missing part.
  • Find all the ways to split a target number such as 6.
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: See parts inside a ten-frame whole

A ten-frame makes five a visible anchor. A filled row of 5 and 2 more can be recognised as 7 without recounting all seven counters.

+→5 and 2 more make 7

Teacher: Fill the first row of the ten-frame from the same starting corner. Subitise the full group of 5 and the extra counters. Name both parts and the whole.

Ask: I used five as an anchor, so I did not need to start counting again at one.

Look for: Show 7 on a ten-frame and explain it as 5 and 2 more.

E2: Partition the same whole in different ways

A whole collection can be separated into different pairs of parts. Moving objects between the parts changes the partition but not the total.

6 = 5 + 16 = 4 + 26 = 3 + 3
different parts • same whole

Teacher: Build and count the whole. Separate it into two visible parts and name each part. Recombine to check the whole, then partition it another way.

Ask: The parts are inside the whole. We did not make extra counters when we split them.

Look for: Show two different partitions of 6 and explain why each still has a whole of 6.

E3: Combine familiar dot patterns into a whole

Standard dice and domino patterns make small parts easy to recognise. Combining the recognised parts gives the whole without counting every dot.

domino |→3 + 2 = 5

Teacher: Subitise each side of the domino or dice pattern. Say the two parts. Combine the parts and name the whole.

Ask: I recognised each part first, then joined the parts to know the whole.

Look for: Name the parts and whole on a domino showing 4 and 2.

E4: Explore sourced First Nations number groupings

A number can be formed and partitioned through grouping conventions represented in different ways. The mathematical focus is how the groups combine to quantify a collection.

sourced group+sourced group→whole collection

Teacher: Select an appropriate local or authoritative First Nations source. Reproduce only the grouping convention the source permits for teaching. Apply it to a collection on Country/Place and explain parts and whole.

Ask: The grouping representation comes from this specific sourced context; our maths question is how its parts form the whole.

Look for: Using the sourced convention, represent one collection as parts and explain how the parts recombine into the whole.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Parts treated as extra — Recombine the same objects to show the parts are inside the whole.
  • Order changes the whole — Compare 4 + 2 and 2 + 4 using the same 6 counters.
  • Guesses hidden part — Use the known whole and visible part to reason what is missing.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Split 7 into 5 and ___.
  • Show another way to make 6 using two parts.
  • If the whole is 8 and one part is 3, what is the other part?
  • Are 4 + 2 and 2 + 4 the same whole? Explain.

Mastery

  • Identify whole and parts
  • Partition a collection
  • Recombine to same whole
  • Find a missing part
  • Show more than one partition
