Parts and wholes to 10 (AC9MFN04)

Explore how a number can be split into parts and put back together. Five objects and three objects make eight altogether: the parts change, but the whole stays eight.

Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: See small parts, combine them and split a whole in different ways. Keep the whole at 10 or less and explain what changed. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Recognise and name the parts

A whole collection can be seen as smaller parts. The parts together include every object in the whole. Recognise small parts at a quick look without counting each counter, then explain what you saw.

Worked example: See 5 as a part of 3 and a part of 2.

  1. Look at the two visible groups.
  2. Name the parts: three and two.
  3. Point to all five and name the whole.

Why it works: The groups are parts because together they account for all the counters.

Watch for: Two groups does not mean two counters. Name the amount in each group.

Try it: Show 4 fingers on one hand and 1 on the other. Name the parts and whole.

Check the answer

The parts are 4 and 1. The whole is 5.

2. Combine parts to find the whole

Combine means put parts together. Count or recognise the complete collection after combining.

Worked example: Combine 4 blocks and 3 blocks.

  1. Keep the four and three visible as separate parts.
  2. Bring both parts together without adding other blocks.
  3. Check the whole: seven blocks.

Why it works: All four and all three are included once, so the whole is seven.

Watch for: A whole must account for both parts. Reporting only the larger part leaves objects uncounted.

Try it: Combine 6 counters and an empty group. What is the whole?

Check the answer

6. The empty part is 0 and adds no counters.

3. Split and represent a given whole

Partition means split a whole into parts. Begin with the whole, then divide its objects between two places.

Worked example: Split 8 counters into 5 and 3.

  1. Make and check a whole of eight.
  2. Move five into one place and the other three into another.
  3. Say “Five and three are parts of eight.” Recombine to check.

Why it works: Every counter belongs to one part and the total stays eight.

Watch for: Do not lose a counter while moving the parts. A correct split must still contain the original whole.

Try it: Split 7 counters into two parts, with at least one in each.

Check the answer

Possible splits include 1 and 6, 2 and 5, or 3 and 4, with either order. Check that the parts make 7 and neither is empty.

4. Make another split and compare

You can partition the same whole in different ways. Moving one object from one part to the other changes both parts.

Worked example: Change 5 and 3 into 4 and 4.

  1. Start with eight split as five and three.
  2. Move one from the five-part to the three-part.
  3. The parts are now four and four. Recombine: the whole is still eight.

Why it works: One part decreased by one while the other increased by one. Nothing entered or left the whole.

Watch for: Spreading out the same parts is not a new numerical split. The numbers in the parts must change.

Try it: Make 6 as 2 and 4, then move one from the larger part to the smaller.

Check the answer

The new parts are 3 and 3. The whole is still 6.

5. Find a missing part

If you know the whole and one part, you can find the other part with objects or a picture.

Worked example: The whole is 10; a visible part is 7.

  1. Make ten counters.
  2. Move seven into the visible part.
  3. The three left make the missing part. Check seven and three together.

Why it works: The whole is completely accounted for by the visible and missing parts.

Watch for: Do not guess from the size of a cover. Use the stated whole and visible amount.

Try it: A whole of 9 has a visible part of 4. What is hidden?

Check the answer

5. Four and five make nine. Show all nine to check.

For teachers and parents: Use counters, fingers, cube trains or ten-frames. Ask which parts the child sees before recording numerals. Recognising parts and explaining them is more useful here than racing through equations.

Ready to try it independently?

Make a whole of 9, show two different splits and hide one part. Explain how the visible part helps find the hidden part.

Use fresh objects or examples. Notice what the child can do independently and what needs another model. A correct choice alone may not show the practical action or reasoning.
