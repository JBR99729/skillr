Subitising to 5 (AC9MFN02)

Subitising means recognising how many objects are in a small group without counting them one at a time. Learn to recognise amounts up to 5, even when the same objects are arranged differently.

Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Recognise a small amount at a quick look, then name, match and compare it. Explore different arrangements of collections up to 5. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Recognise and name the amount

Subitising means recognising how many without counting each object one by one. Familiar arrangements can help; practise new arrangements too. An empty collection is zero.

Worked example: See 5 as four and one.

  1. Look briefly at the five-dot model, then cover it.
  2. Say the amount you saw: five.
  3. Reveal it and explain: four corner dots and one in the centre make five.

Why it works: Recognising known small parts can help you recognise the whole. An explanation gives more information than a numeral alone.

Watch for: A correct answer after touching and counting every dot shows useful counting, but does not by itself show subitising.

Try it: An adult briefly shows 3 counters, covers them and asks “How many?” Then reveal and check.

Check the answer

The amount is 3. Ask what was noticed. If the child counted or needed another look, note that support and try a familiar smaller arrangement.

2. Match and recreate the same number

The same number can look different. Matching the amount does not require copying colour, spacing or orientation unless the instruction asks for those too.

Worked example: See four dots, then make four counters.

  1. Briefly show a four-dot card and cover it.
  2. Name the amount and make it with counters.
  3. Reveal the card. Match each dot with a counter to check the amount.

Why it works: Changing the material or arrangement can preserve the number. Rebuilding shows a different skill from choosing a supplied picture.

Watch for: Do not add a counter just because the new arrangement has a larger gap. A gap is not an object.

Try it: Make the same number as a briefly shown 5-dot card, using blocks in a different arrangement.

Check the answer

Any arrangement of exactly 5 blocks matches the amount. Check the first response before using counting to verify.

3. Compare small recognised amounts

More means a larger number of objects; fewer means a smaller number. Same number means the amounts are equal. Look at the amount rather than the space it fills.

Worked example: Compare cards showing 3 and 5.

  1. Recognise three on one card and five on the other.
  2. Five is more than three; three is fewer than five.
  3. Reveal both cards and pair objects to check. Two from the five have no partner.

Why it works: The unmatched objects confirm the comparison. Two different arrangements of four would match with none left over.

Watch for: A long spread-out row may still have fewer objects. Do not compare only the row lengths.

Try it: Compare new cards showing 4, 2 and 4. Which have the same number? Which has the fewest?

Check the answer

The two 4-cards have the same number, even if arranged differently. The 2-card has the fewest. Ask for the amounts noticed.

For teachers and parents: Give the instruction before a quick look. Briefly show and cover a model, ask for the first response, then reveal it to discuss and check. Accept speech or fingers. Record repeat looks or counting as support; there is no universal speed threshold.

Ready to try it independently?

Use a new arrangement up to 5. Ask how many, what the child noticed, and whether they can make the same amount with other objects.

Use fresh objects or examples. Notice what the child can do independently and what needs another model. A correct choice alone may not show the practical action or reasoning.
