1 · Understand

What does “subitise” mean?

Subitising means seeing “how many” immediately instead of touching and counting each object. Adults often do this with dice. Foundation students build the same skill with small collections. This strengthens number sense because children begin to see that 5 can be made from 4 and 1, 3 and 2, or other combinations.

See 5 as 4 and 1

4 + 1 = 5

Say: “I saw four together and one more. I knew it was five without counting each dot.”

Why this matters

Children stop treating every number as a long counting task and start seeing number structure.

5 can look different and still be 5.

This supports later addition, subtraction and part-part-whole thinking.

2 · Teach

Teach it in three moves

1

Flash

Show 1–5 counters or dots for about two seconds, then cover them.

2

Name

Ask, “How many?” Accept instant recognition; do not turn it into a speed race.

3

Explain

Ask, “What did your eyes notice?” A child might say “2 and 2”, “3 and 2” or “4 and 1”.

Teacher model: flash five dots, cover them and say, “I did not count every dot. I saw a group of 4 and 1 more. Four and one make 5.” Then show a different arrangement and ask the learner to name the total and the parts they noticed.
3 · Compare

Different arrangement, same number

Move the same counters without adding or removing any. The amount stays the same even though the picture changes.

Guided check

Show differently arranged cards for 3, 4 and 5. Ask the learner to match equal quantities, order them and explain which collection has more.

What mastery sounds like

“It is still 4. You moved them, but you didn’t add or take any away.”

4 · Apply

Quick-look practice

1

Recognise

Flash collections of 1 to 5 counters. The learner says the total and explains the parts they saw.

2

Match

Match two cards that show the same quantity in different arrangements.

3

Compare

Compare two quick-look cards and explain which has more, fewer or the same number.

5 · Fix misconceptions

What to do when a child gets stuck

Counts every dot
Rapid fix: use familiar 2-, 3- and 4-dot patterns. Flash briefly, cover, then ask what shape or parts they noticed.
Thinks rearranging changes the number
Rapid fix: move the same counters in front of the child and ask, “Did I add any? Did I take any away?”
Guesses without explaining
Rapid fix: ask for evidence: “What smaller groups did your eyes notice?”
6 · Check

Ready for Practice?

RecogniseFlash 5 in a new arrangement. Can the learner name it without one-by-one counting?
MatchCan the learner match two different arrangements that both show 4?
ExplainCan the learner say what they noticed: “3 and 2”, “4 and 1”, or the whole familiar pattern?

Detailed teaching notes and success criteria

Learning intention

Students recognise a collection of up to 5 objects with a quick look, say how many there are without counting each object, and explain the smaller parts they noticed.

E1 — recognise a quantity with a quick look

Teacher model: cover the dots and say, “I did not count every dot. I saw a group of 4 and 1 more. Four and one make 5.” Flash a new arrangement and ask the student to name the total and the parts they noticed.

Guided check: briefly show 3 dots with 2 underneath. Cover it. Ask, “How many? What groups did you see?” A secure response names 5 without recounting and may explain “3 and 2”.

E2 — match, compare and order different arrangements

Different arrangements can show the same quantity. Match cards with equal quantities first, then arrange cards from fewer to more.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise collections up to 5 without counting one by one.
  • I can recognise the same quantity when objects are rearranged.
  • I can explain the parts I saw, such as “3 and 2 make 5”.
  • I can use quick recognition to compare two small collections.
Explain, check and apply

Use a quick look, not a speed race

Briefly show a dot card, then cover it. Ask how many and what arrangement the child noticed. A correct answer after counting every dot is useful counting evidence, but does not yet demonstrate subitising. Give another supported look when needed; do not score children on speed.

Same amount, different arrangement

Make 4 with counters. Move the same counters into a different arrangement without adding or removing any. The quantity stays 4.

Make the method visible

Both a child who counts to 5 and a child who recognises 5 can have the correct total. Recognising the arrangement without counting each dot is the response that demonstrates subitising.

Ready to move on

Use several cards with 1–5 objects in different arrangements. Look for reliable recognition across more than one arrangement. Accept naming the whole immediately or describing recognised small parts.

Curriculum coverage · AC9MFN02

Content description: recognise and name the number of objects within a collection up to 5 using subitising.

  • E1: recognising how many objects are in a collection or in images on a card with a quick look and saying the associated number without counting.
  • E2: playing instructive card games that rely on recognising numbers represented in different ways; matching pairs of quantities shown differently; using subitising to compare and order collections and to say who has more when sharing items in a game.
Common mistakes and revision notes
  • Counting every dot: use brief flashes and structured arrangements, then ask what parts the student saw.
  • Thinking a new arrangement means a new number: rearrange the same counters and confirm none were added or removed.
  • Guessing without explaining: ask, “What smaller groups did your eyes notice?”

Revision notes

  • Subitising means seeing how many without counting each object one by one.
  • 5 can be seen as 4 and 1, or 3 and 2.
  • Moving the same objects into a new arrangement does not change how many there are.
  • Different-looking dot cards can show the same quantity.
