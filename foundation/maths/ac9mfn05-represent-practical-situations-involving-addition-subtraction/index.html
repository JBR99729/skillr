Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Act out familiar situations, make a model, then count or recognise the amount that answers the question. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Work out the amount needed

A practical question tells you what to count and why. Start with the need, then choose the right amount.

Worked example: Find clips for 3 cards, one clip each, from 5 clips.

  1. Count the three cards.
  2. Match one clip to each card.
  3. Three clips are used and two are spare.

Why it works: The one-each rule tells how many clips are needed, even though five are available.

Watch for: Enough and exactly enough are different. Extra supplies are not extra things to use automatically.

Try it: Set out 4 cups for 4 people from a supply of 6 cups.

Check the answer

Use 4 cups, one each. Two cups remain unused.

2. Model something being added

A joining story changes over time: first there is a starting amount, then more arrive.

Worked example: There are 3 blocks. Add 2 more. How many now?

  1. Make the starting group of three.
  2. Move two more into it.
  3. Count or recognise all five: there are five now.

Why it works: The added objects belong in the new whole. You can count all or count on from a known amount.

Watch for: “Three, four” after a starting three incorrectly counts the start again. Count on with “four, five”.

Try it: Start with 5 counters and add 1. How many now?

Check the answer

6. The five remain and one more joins.

3. Combine two groups already there

Two groups can exist at the same time. To find altogether, include every object from both.

Worked example: There are 4 red blocks and 3 blue blocks. How many altogether?

  1. Make four red and three blue blocks.
  2. Bring them together, keeping both parts visible.
  3. Count all seven.

Why it works: Both groups contribute to the requested whole, even though neither group arrived later.

Watch for: Do not count the two groups as two objects. Count the blocks in both groups.

Try it: There are 2 pencils in each of two trays. How many pencils altogether?

Check the answer

4 pencils. The trays are not pencils and are not counted.

4. Model something being taken away

Take away means remove objects from the starting collection. The question may ask how many remain.

Worked example: Start with 7 counters and remove 3. How many remain?

  1. Make and check seven.
  2. Move three to a separate removed area.
  3. Count the four still in the starting area.

Why it works: The removed counters explain the change, but they are not part of the remaining collection.

Watch for: Count the requested set. If the question asks “left”, do not recount removed and remaining together.

Try it: Start with 4 and remove all 4. How many remain?

Check the answer

0. The starting area is empty; all four are in the removed area.

5. Check the story against the model

The same numbers can describe different actions. Retell what happened before choosing what to count.

Worked example: Compare “5 blocks, add 2” with “5 blocks, remove 2”.

  1. Build five for each story.
  2. For the first, add two: the new total is seven.
  3. For the second, move two away: three remain.

Why it works: The action and question determine the model. Number words alone do not tell you what happened.

Watch for: Do not use one keyword as a rule for every story. Check the objects, action and requested amount.

Try it: There are 6 cups and 8 people, one cup needed each. Explain whether there are enough.

Check the answer

There are not enough: six people can have a cup and two cannot. Two more cups are needed.

For teachers and parents: Read short stories aloud. Use real objects or movable counters before drawings and optional number sentences. Keep the starting, added, removed and remaining objects distinguishable. Let the child explain the model.

Ready to try it independently?

Tell a new joining or taking-away story. Make its model, answer the question and explain which objects were counted.

Use fresh objects or examples. Notice what the child can do independently and what needs another model. A correct choice alone may not show the practical action or reasoning.
