Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Choose the attribute, compare it fairly and explain the evidence. Use real objects and events to check predictions. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Compare length with a shared start

Length describes how far an object extends in the direction being compared. Height and width are particular lengths.

Worked example: Compare the straight ribbons shown.

  1. Lay both straight and align one end.
  2. Look along the same direction.
  3. Ribbon A reaches farther, so A is longer and B is shorter.

Why it works: A common start makes endpoint reach a fair comparison of length.

Watch for: If starts differ or a ribbon is curled, the farther endpoint may mislead. Align and straighten before deciding.

Try it: One pencil is moved forward before comparing. How can you make the comparison fair?

Check the answer

Move the pencils so one end lines up, then compare the other ends. The result depends on the actual pencils.

2. Compare mass by testing

Mass is how heavy or light an object is. Predict, then heft safely or use a correctly balanced equal-arm balance.

Worked example: In a balance test, object A’s pan goes lower and object B’s pan goes higher.

  1. Check the empty balance is level.
  2. Place one object on each pan and let it settle.
  3. With this observed result, A is heavier and B is lighter.

Why it works: A properly working equal-arm balance tilts towards the heavier load. If it remains level, the loads balance.

Watch for: A large empty box can be lighter than a small stone. Picture size does not prove mass.

Try it: The pans stay level with one object on each. What does that show?

Check the answer

The objects balance: they have the same mass within what this balance can detect. Retest if the equipment or placement is doubtful.

3. Compare how much containers can hold

Capacity is how much a container can hold when full. A tall container does not necessarily hold more than a short wide one.

Worked example: A full cup A is poured into empty cup B. All the water fits and B still has space.

  1. Begin with A full and B empty.
  2. Transfer all the water without spilling.
  3. B can hold more than A because it has unused space after holding A’s full amount.

Why it works: The transfer compares full capacity, not just the water levels in two partly filled cups.

Watch for: A leak or spill prevents a reliable conclusion. Repeat the test. Do not infer capacity from height alone.

Try it: A full container A exactly fills an empty container B with no spill or space. Compare their capacities.

Check the answer

They have the same capacity under this test. If water from full A overflows B, A holds more than B.

4. Compare how long events take

Duration means how long an event lasts from its beginning to its agreed end. A shared start lets you compare finishing times directly.

Worked example: Two activities start together. Sorting finishes first; packing finishes later.

  1. Agree what counts as finished for each activity.
  2. Start both at the same time.
  3. Observe: packing lasts longer; sorting takes a shorter time.

Why it works: With a shared start, the later finish indicates a longer duration.

Watch for: Finishing later alone proves nothing if the starts differed. Restart together or use another fair comparison.

Try it: Two activities start together and finish together. What can you say about their durations?

Check the answer

They last the same amount of time in this observation. No clock reading or formal unit is needed.

For teachers and parents: Use safe materials, an equal-arm balance where available and adult supervision for pouring. Treat pictures as models of stated observations. A drawing alone cannot establish real mass, capacity or an event’s duration.

Ready to try it independently?

Choose two suitable objects or events, say which attribute you will compare, demonstrate a fair test and explain the result.

Use fresh objects or examples. Notice what the child can do independently and what needs another model. A correct choice alone may not show the practical action or reasoning.
