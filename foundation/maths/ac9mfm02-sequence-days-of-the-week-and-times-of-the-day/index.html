Days and times of day

Follow the repeating week, use yesterday and tomorrow, and place familiar events in a clear daily sequence.

Practice on app ↗Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Complete Saturday, Sunday, …

Why it works: The days always follow the same repeating order. Monday comes after Sunday, even when the calendar is laid out differently.

Learn the skill

1. Follow the repeating week

There are seven named days in the weekly cycle. After Sunday comes Monday again. A display can start with a different day while keeping the same cyclic order.

Try it · Start at Friday and say the next three days.

Saturday, Sunday, Monday. Do not repeat Friday as the first new answer.

2. Use yesterday, today and tomorrow

Today is the stated anchor. Yesterday is the day immediately before it; tomorrow is the day immediately after it.

Try it · If today is Sunday, what were yesterday and tomorrow?

Yesterday was Saturday and tomorrow is Monday. Follow one step each way around the cycle.

3. Sequence times within a day

Use morning, lunchtime, afternoon and night to describe broad times of day. Follow their sequence within the same day.

Try it · In this day sequence, which label is between lunchtime and night?

Afternoon. It is after lunchtime and before night.

Common mistakes

Do not place an extra day between Sunday and Monday. Check all seven names in order.

Do not answer using the real day outside the task when the question says “If today is …”.

Do not use a clock-reading rule for these broad words. People’s meals, work and sleep routines can differ.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Put the day in order

Draw or name three events from today. Order them from earliest to latest. Then name today's day of the week and find the day before and after it.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two familiar events. Extend by ordering the seven days and wrapping from Sunday to Monday.

FAQ

What comes after Sunday?

Monday. The sequence of days repeats each week.

Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Follow the repeating week, use yesterday and tomorrow, and place familiar events in a clear daily sequence. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Follow the repeating week

There are seven named days in the weekly cycle. After Sunday comes Monday again. A display can start with a different day while keeping the same cyclic order.

Worked example: Complete Saturday, Sunday, …

  1. Follow the days forwards.
  2. After Saturday comes Sunday.
  3. After Sunday comes Monday: the cycle continues.

Why it works: The week repeats; Sunday is not the end of time. Starting a list on Monday is a display choice, not the only possible start.

Watch for: Do not place an extra day between Sunday and Monday. Check all seven names in order.

Try it: Start at Friday and say the next three days.

Check the answer

Saturday, Sunday, Monday. Do not repeat Friday as the first new answer.

2. Use yesterday, today and tomorrow

Today is the stated anchor. Yesterday is the day immediately before it; tomorrow is the day immediately after it.

Worked example: If today is Monday, find yesterday and tomorrow.

  1. Find Monday in the cycle.
  2. One step back gives Sunday: yesterday.
  3. One step forward gives Tuesday: tomorrow.

Why it works: The words depend on the anchor day. They are not permanent names for particular weekdays.

Watch for: Do not answer using the real day outside the task when the question says “If today is …”.

Try it: If today is Sunday, what were yesterday and tomorrow?

Check the answer

Yesterday was Saturday and tomorrow is Monday. Follow one step each way around the cycle.

3. Sequence times within a day

Use morning, lunchtime, afternoon and night to describe broad times of day. Follow their sequence within the same day.

Worked example: Order the labels morning, lunchtime, afternoon, night.

  1. Begin with morning.
  2. Lunchtime comes after morning and before afternoon.
  3. Afternoon comes before night.

Why it works: The labels describe a useful daily sequence. Another morning follows the night as the next day begins.

Watch for: Do not use a clock-reading rule for these broad words. People’s meals, work and sleep routines can differ.

Try it: In this day sequence, which label is between lunchtime and night?

Check the answer

Afternoon. It is after lunchtime and before night.

4. Put events before, after and between

A sequence states the order of events. Use the given clues or routine rather than assuming everyone has the same day.

Worked example: A plan says paint before tidy-up, and tidy-up before a story.

  1. Place paint first.
  2. Place tidy-up after paint.
  3. Place the story after tidy-up. Tidy-up is between painting and the story.

Why it works: Both clues must be satisfied. “Between” requires an event before and an event after.

Watch for: An activity does not prove its time of day on its own. Use the plan or the child’s actual routine.

Try it: A plan says snack after play and before reading. Order all three events.

Check the answer

Play, snack, reading. Snack is after play and before reading.
