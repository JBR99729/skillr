Explore familiar shapes

Name and sort familiar shapes by their features, rather than by their colour or size. Turn a triangle around and it still has three straight sides and three corners.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Recognise a triangle with a corner pointing sideways

Why it works: Shape features stay the same when the whole shape turns.

Learn the skill

1. Name shapes when they turn

Familiar shapes include circles, triangles, squares and rectangles. Turning or resizing a shape does not change its name.

Try it · Turn a square paper card. Is it still a square? Explain.

Yes. Turning keeps its four equal straight sides and four square corners.

2. Describe features and give reasons

Explain a shape with its boundary, sides and corners. A square has four equal sides and four square corners. A non-square rectangle has four square corners but not all four sides equal.

Try it · Does a circle have straight sides or corners?

No. Its boundary is curved all the way around and has no corners.

3. Sort and explain the rule

Choose a sorting rule, then check every shape against it. Different valid rules can give different groups.

Try it · Re-sort the four pictured shapes by “has exactly four straight sides” and “does not”.

Square and non-square rectangle have exactly four straight sides. Triangle and circle go in the other group.

Common mistakes

A triangle need not point up or have three equal sides. Do not rely on a memorised upright picture.

Do not say every four-sided shape is a square. Four sides alone are not enough.

If bins must be exclusive, label them clearly. Use “squares” and “non-square rectangles”, since squares are also rectangles.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Sort a shape collection

Cut out or draw circles, triangles and rectangles in different sizes and orientations. Sort them, explain the rule, then turn a shape and decide where it belongs.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two shape families. Extend by sorting the same pieces using a different rule.

FAQ

Learn the idea

Build understanding one step at a time

Learning goal: Name, describe, sort and make familiar shapes. Use their features to explain decisions and notice flat shapes in everyday objects. Open a focus, follow its example, then try the question before checking the answer. An adult can read instructions aloud.

1. Name shapes when they turn

Familiar shapes include circles, triangles, squares and rectangles. Turning or resizing a shape does not change its name.

Worked example: Recognise a triangle with a corner pointing sideways.

  1. Trace its closed boundary.
  2. Find three straight sides and three corners.
  3. Name it a triangle even though it is turned.

Why it works: Shape features stay the same when the whole shape turns.

Watch for: A triangle need not point up or have three equal sides. Do not rely on a memorised upright picture.

Try it: Turn a square paper card. Is it still a square? Explain.

Check the answer

Yes. Turning keeps its four equal straight sides and four square corners.

2. Describe features and give reasons

Explain a shape with its boundary, sides and corners. A square has four equal sides and four square corners. A non-square rectangle has four square corners but not all four sides equal.

Worked example: Compare a square with a non-square rectangle.

  1. Trace four straight sides on each.
  2. Check their four square corners, like a paper corner.
  3. Notice all the square’s sides are equal; the non-square rectangle has two longer and two shorter sides.

Why it works: A square is a special rectangle. Side equality distinguishes it from a non-square rectangle.

Watch for: Do not say every four-sided shape is a square. Four sides alone are not enough.

Try it: Does a circle have straight sides or corners?

Check the answer

No. Its boundary is curved all the way around and has no corners.

3. Sort and explain the rule

Choose a sorting rule, then check every shape against it. Different valid rules can give different groups.

Worked example: Sort the pictured shapes into “has corners” and “has no corners”.

  1. State the rule before sorting.
  2. Put triangle, square and rectangle into the corners group.
  3. Put circle into the no-corners group and explain the feature used.

Why it works: Every shape is placed according to the same stated property.

Watch for: If bins must be exclusive, label them clearly. Use “squares” and “non-square rectangles”, since squares are also rectangles.

Try it: Re-sort the four pictured shapes by “has exactly four straight sides” and “does not”.

Check the answer

Square and non-square rectangle have exactly four straight sides. Triangle and circle go in the other group.

4. Create, join and check shapes

Make a shape using materials, then check the outer boundary. Joining pieces can produce a different whole shape.

Worked example: Join two matching right-triangle paper pieces into a square.

  1. An adult cuts a square along one diagonal to make two matching pieces.
  2. Fit the pieces back together along those cut edges.
  3. Check: no gap or overlap, four equal outer sides and four square corners.

Why it works: The joined boundary is the original square’s boundary. The internal join is not an extra outer side.

Watch for: Two arbitrary triangles do not always make a square. These pieces work because they came from the same square.

Try it: Use three straight sticks to make a closed triangle. Explain how you checked.

Check the answer

A valid triangle has three straight sides joined end to end into a closed boundary. Adjust the sticks if there is a gap or they cannot meet.

5. Find shapes within everyday objects

Look for a flat face, outline or component that resembles a familiar shape. Name the part you are describing.

Worked example: Notice a rectangular window pane.

  1. Point to the flat pane, not the entire building.
  2. Trace its four straight sides and square corners.
  3. Say “This pane has a rectangular outline.”

Why it works: A component can resemble a flat shape even when the whole object has depth.

Watch for: An entire cup is not a circle. Its top rim may have a circular outline when viewed from above.

Try it: Find a flat object face or component in the room. Name a shape it resembles and explain.

Check the answer

Answers depend on the actual object. For example, a rectangular book cover has four straight sides and square corners. Point to the specific face and check its features.
