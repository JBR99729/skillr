FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Break up, blend and change spoken sounds AC9EFLY10

Learning intention: I can hear words in a sentence, blend and separate sounds, and change a sound to make a new word.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Add, remove or replace one sound

Keep this oral. Adult: say “at”, then add the humming beginning sound of moon at the start: “mat”. Remove that added sound to return to “at”. Say “mat” again and replace its last sound with the beginning sound of pig: “map”. Use a counter for each heard sound; show which counter is added, removed or replaced while the others stay in order.

Learn the skill

1. Separate a sentence into words

Say the whole sentence. Repeat it and move one counter for every word, including short words. A word may have more than one syllable, so clapping beats is a different task.

Adult: say “My raincoat is wet.” Repeat naturally, then slowly.

Try it · Move one counter per word. How many words are there?

Four: My | raincoat | is | wet. Raincoat has two syllables but is one word in this sentence. Say the whole sentence again after counting.

2. Blend and segment a single-syllable word

For blending, listen to each supplied speech sound in order and join them smoothly. For segmenting, begin with a whole spoken word and separate its sounds. Avoid adding “uh” to consonants.

Adult: supply /s/ /ʌ/ /n/ using sun, up and nest as cues. Then say “ship” as a whole word. Keep printed spellings hidden from the child.

Try it · Blend the first sounds. Then move a counter for each sound in ship.

The first sequence makes sun. Ship has three sounds: /ʃ/ /ɪ/ /p/. The sh sound uses one counter even though adults spell it with two letters. These are sound tasks, not letter counting.

3. Change just the named sound

First say the starting word and identify the sound position. Add a counter for an added sound, take one away for deletion or swap one for substitution. Reblend and check that the other sounds stayed.

Adult: say “lip”; add the beginning sound of sun at the start. Say “best”; remove its /s/. Say “pin”; replace its middle vowel with the vowel in pen.

Try it · Say the three new words. What operation made each one?

Slip adds initial /s/; bet removes /s/ from best; pen replaces the middle vowel in pin. The other sounds remain in the same order. Work on one operation at a time before mixing them.

Common mistakes

“A long word must count as several words.” Count whole words in the sentence separately from their syllables.

“Each written letter needs one sound counter.” In an oral task, counters stand for heard sounds, including one for sh in ship.

“Change a sound means replace the whole word.” Keep the unchanged sound counters in place and alter only the named position.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

A sound-changing trail

Adult: say “tea” and add /m/, the beginning sound of moon, at the end to make team. Say “smile” and remove its first /s/ to make mile. Say “mop” and replace its first /m/ with /t/ to make top. Let the learner move counters and say each result before you reveal it.

Adjust the challenge Starter: blend two sounds and add an initial sound to at. Core: blend and segment three sounds, then add, delete and replace a specified sound. Stretch: start with cap and insert /l/ after /k/ to make clap, explaining which position was added.

Use familiar words and local pronunciation; teacher sound notation is not child reading material. Work orally with real adult speech. A counter diagram cannot certify hearing or pronunciation. Separate speech-production support from sound-awareness observations.

Three tricky questions

1. Can one-syllable frog have four phonemes?

Yes: /f/ /r/ /ɒ/ /g/. Syllables and individual speech sounds measure different parts of speech.

2. Change the final /p/ in cheap to /t/. Is the new word cheat?

Yes. Keep the initial ch sound and long vowel. Only the final phoneme changes; spelling is not the task.

3. Add /l/ after /k/ in cap. Is this a replacement?

No. It is an insertion: cap becomes clap. There are now four phonemes rather than three.

FAQ

Should the child see printed words?

Keep print hidden for these listening tasks. The adult-facing words and notation specify what to say accurately.

How do we check the whole code?

Across short sessions, observe sentence-to-word segmentation, oral blending and segmentation, isolation, and addition, deletion and substitution at suitable sound positions. One successful change is only one piece of evidence.

Key vocabulary
  • segment — break spoken language into separate parts
  • blend — join separate speech sounds to say a whole word
  • sentence — a group of words that expresses an idea
  • word — a spoken unit that carries meaning
  • phoneme — one individual speech sound
  • manipulate — add, remove or replace a speech sound
Key concept

I can hear words in a sentence and blend, segment and change sounds in one-syllable words.

Learning goal: I can hear words in a sentence and blend, segment and change sounds in one-syllable words.

I am ready when I can

  • I can segment a sentence into words.
  • I can segment a single-syllable word into speech sounds.
  • I can blend speech sounds into a word.
  • I can isolate a first, middle or final sound.
  • I can change one sound and check the new word.
See it, then try it

See the skill in action

Worked-model method: count spoken parts, blend or change them, then check.

1. Listen to the whole sentence, ‘The dog ran home.’2. Say it again slowly and move one counter for The, one for dog, one for ran and one for home.3. Touch and count the four counters, then say the whole sentence again.

Model answer: The spoken sentence has four words because each meaningful spoken word receives one counter.

Worked example: blend and change a spoken word

Worked-model method: count spoken parts, blend or change them, then check.

1. Listen to three sounds: the humming sound at the start of moon, the short vowel sound at the beginning of apple and the tapping sound at the start of top.2. Slide three counters together and blend the sounds to say mat.3. Replace the first sound with the hissing sound at the start of sun, blend again and say sat.

Model answer: Changing only the first speech sound changes mat to sat while the middle and final sounds stay the same.

Materials and spoken prompts for these examples

Adult: deliver actual speech. For picture choices, prepare and name drawings of mat and sat (a person sitting), sun and run (a person running), and map and mop; demonstrate meanings before the listening choice. Use safe counters and change only the specified sound position. Sound notation is for the adult: short i in lip is /ɪ/, not the long ee sound. No picture or written response certifies heard sound without a real oral turn.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Segment a spoken word into sounds

A single-syllable spoken word can be stretched into its individual speech sounds while the sound order remains unchanged.

Think: Say can as a whole word. Stretch it and move counters for the quiet cutting sound at the start of cat, the short vowel sound at the beginning of apple and the humming sound at the beginning of nest. Touch the three counters in order and say the whole word again.

Try: What speech sounds do you hear from the beginning to the end of can?

A strong answer: Say the whole word, move one counter for each heard sound, identify every sound with a cue word and count the separated parts.

Check: The learner represents each individual speech sound in the spoken word with one ordered counter and recombines the word accurately.

If you are stuck: Begin with two-sound words or provide the first counter, then stretch the word together without displaying letters.

E2: Blend separate sounds into a word

Oral blending joins a sequence of separate speech sounds smoothly to make a recognisable single-syllable word.

Think: Provide the flowing beginning sound in leaf, the short vowel sound at the beginning of insect and the popping sound at the beginning of pig. Touch three counters while repeating the sounds in order. Slide the counters together and say lip as one word.

Try: Which whole word do these three cue-word sounds make when you join them?

A strong answer: Listen to the separated cue sounds, move one counter for each, slide the counters together and blend the whole spoken word.

Check: The learner preserves the supplied sound order and blends it into the intended spoken word without adding or omitting a sound.

If you are stuck: Use continuous hand movement under the counters, repeat the first two sounds together and then add the final sound.

E3: Replace one sound to make a new word

Changing the beginning, middle or final speech sound in a single-syllable word can make a new spoken word while the other sounds stay fixed.

Think: Build three counters for run. Replace only the first counter with the soft blowing beginning sound in fish and blend fun. Keep the first and last sounds, change the middle to the short sound in apple and blend fan.

Try: Which sound position changed, which sounds stayed and what new word did you make?

A strong answer: Build the starting word with sound counters, replace only the named sound, blend the new word and state what changed and stayed.

Check: The learner replaces the named speech sound, blends a valid new word and explains which positions changed and stayed.

If you are stuck: Hold two counters in place, exaggerate the replacement action and offer two picture choices for the possible new word.

Common misconceptions
  • Counting syllables gives the number of words — Repeat the sentence and move a counter for every separate meaningful word, including short words.
  • Speech sounds are the same as letter names — Keep the task oral and use cue words to imitate sounds instead of reciting alphabet names.
  • Changing one sound means changing the whole word — Hold two counters still, replace only the named counter and blend all parts again.
Listen, explain and apply

Keep the task oral

Adult: say the target words and individual sounds; the child listens, speaks or moves counters. Use speech sounds rather than alphabet names, and avoid adding an extra “uh” to consonants.

Words and syllables differ

Say “The rabbit hops.” Move one counter per word. Then clap the beats in rabbit. How do the tasks differ?

Answer and teaching guidance

The sentence has three words; rabbit has two syllables. The counter task separates the sentence into words, while clapping separates one word into beats.

Check the sound order

Adult: say the beginning sound of sun, the short vowel at the start of insect, then the beginning sound of top. Blend them. What happens if the final sound changes to the beginning of pig?

Answer and teaching guidance

The sounds blend to sit. Replacing only the final sound makes sip. Keep the first and middle sounds in place.

Add and remove a sound

Say “at”. Add the beginning sound of moon at the start. Now remove that sound again. What words do you say?

Answer and teaching guidance

Adding the sound makes mat; removing it returns to at. Counters can show the added or removed sound without printed letters.

Ready to move on

Adult: say “shop”. Ask for its separate sounds, then ask the child to change the final sound to the beginning of top.

Answer and teaching guidance

Shop has three speech sounds: the initial sh sound, short o and p. Changing the final sound gives shot. Count sounds, not the four printed letters.

Three oral operations, exact positions

Adult says tea, adds /m/ at the end and waits for team; says best, removes internal /s/ and waits for bet; says mop, replaces first /m/ with /t/ and waits for top. Use one operation at a time. New letters are not shown and spelling changes are not assessed.
