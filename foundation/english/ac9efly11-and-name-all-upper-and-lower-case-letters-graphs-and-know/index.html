FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Know letter names, forms and common sounds AC9EFLY11

Learning intention: I can recognise and name both case forms and connect a letter with a commonly taught sound.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One letter identity, two printed forms

M and m look different but both have the letter name em. Their common sound is the humming beginning of moon, not the spoken name em. The table below keeps case, name and cue separate for all 26 letters. Use a few at a time in mixed order, then return to the others across sessions.

Learn the skill

1. Match and name the two forms

Look at the actual printed letter, find its case partner and say their shared name. Recognising a letter in mixed order is different from singing the alphabet sequence.

Printed choices: B, d, D, b.

Try it · Make two case pairs and name each pair.

B pairs with b, named bee. D pairs with d, named dee. Case changes the form, not the letter identity. Trace a distinguishing shape feature if b and d are confused.

2. Separate a name from a common sound

A name identifies a letter. A sound is what the letter can represent in a word. Say a familiar cue word, then isolate the relevant sound. A letter may represent other sounds in other words.

Printed P p. Name: pee. Cue: pet. Printed C c. Name: see. Cue: cup.

Try it · What common sound does each cue teach?

P/p represents the first sound /p/ in pet; C/c represents /k/ in cup. Neither answer is the letter name. Do not add an extra vowel after the consonant or claim c always represents /k/.

3. Find a known letter inside a word

Connect the printed form with its name and a common sound, then apply it in a familiar word. A name card is useful for recognition, but not every personal name follows simple sound patterns.

Printed word: mat. Adult: model /m/ /æ/ /t/ and blend mat. Point to m, then show M.

Try it · Which letter names both forms, and which sound helps read mat?

Both forms are em; /m/, the humming beginning of moon, helps read mat. Say the sounds and blend them, rather than blending the alphabet names em, ay, tee.

A complete alphabet reference

Identification samples in a screen font. Adult: say the cue, then isolate the stated sound. The name is a separate response. Accept taught local name variants. This is one common early association per letter, not every possible correspondence.

Common mistakes

“Upper-case and lower-case are different letters.” Match the forms by their shared name.

“Saying pee answers the sound question.” Pee is the name; give the first sound of pet for this common association.

“Every letter always means one sound.” Letters can work in teams or have different sounds in different words. Explain x and qu carefully.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Name, partner, cue

Make cards for five taught letters in both cases. Mix them, find partners and name each letter. An adult then asks separately for a common sound using the reference cues. Locate one of these letters in a meaningful label or a learner’s preferred printed name.

Adjust the challenge Starter: match one upper-case card to two lower-case choices. Core: name mixed-case letters and give separate common sounds. Stretch: recognise a previously taught form inside a word and explain how it helps with blending. Revisit the full alphabet over later sessions.

Accept conventional local letter-name variants. A selected answer may show recognition but does not demonstrate oral naming. Ask the child to say or communicate each name and sound separately, with suitable access support.

Three tricky questions

1. Does x in fox represent only one phoneme?

No. Final x here represents /k/ followed by /s/: two phonemes. Its letter name is ex.

2. Does q by itself always represent two sounds?

No. In queen, teach the printed team qu with /k/ followed by /w/. Do not attribute the whole team’s sounds to isolated q in every word.

3. Is y’s name why the sound we blend at the start of yes?

No. Why is the letter name. The first sound of yes is the common consonant association used here.

FAQ

Does this table list every sound a letter can represent?

No. It supplies a common early association. Read each cue naturally and teach further correspondences through a systematic school sequence.

Does matching a few pairs show the whole alphabet is secure?

No. Sample both cases in mixed order across sessions. Record recognition, naming and common-sound association separately for all 26 letters.

Key vocabulary
  • letter — a written symbol in the alphabet
  • letter name — the name used to identify an alphabet letter
  • upper-case — the capital form of a letter
  • lower-case — the smaller everyday form of a letter
  • common sound — a speech sound a letter often represents
  • cue word — a known word that helps recall a sound
Key concept

I can name upper- and lower-case letters and say their most common sounds.

Learning goal: I can name upper- and lower-case letters and say their most common sounds.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Recognise letters in a familiar name

A learner's own printed name provides a meaningful place to recognise and name upper-case and lower-case alphabet letters.

Think: Display the learner's name card beside two distractor names. Find the name using its complete printed sequence. Point to and name each familiar letter, noting the first upper-case form when present.

Try: Which printed letters help you recognise your name, and what are their alphabet names?

A strong answer: Find the learner's preferred printed name, point to each letter in order, say its alphabet name and identify its printed case.

Check: The learner selects the correct name and accurately identifies one or more printed letters within it.

If you are stuck: Begin with the first letter and a matching personal-photo cue, then compare the complete name with one contrasting card.

E2: Match upper-case and lower-case partners

Upper-case and lower-case forms can look different while sharing one alphabet letter name.

Think: Display upper-case M, lower-case m, upper-case D and lower-case d. Say each letter name. Match the two em forms and the two dee forms, then explain that case changes but letter identity stays.

Try: Which two printed forms share one letter name even though their shapes differ?

A strong answer: Use the full alphabet reference to match upper-case and lower-case partners, including different-looking forms, and say each shared name.

Check: The learner matches upper-case and lower-case forms by shared letter identity and names the pair accurately.

If you are stuck: Use one upper-case card and only two lower-case choices, say the letter name together and trace distinctive shape features.

E3: Connect letter forms with a common sound

Both case forms of a letter can represent the same commonly taught speech sound, remembered through a familiar cue word.

Think: Show upper-case S and lower-case s and name both as ess. Say sun and listen to the hissing sound at its beginning. Connect both printed forms to that common cue sound and contrast it with another letter.

Try: What is the letter name, and what common sound do you hear at the beginning of its cue word?

A strong answer: Choose a letter pair, say its alphabet name, say the listed cue word and imitate the commonly taught sound, including vowel and consonant examples.

Check: The learner names either case form and gives the taught common speech sound through an accurate cue word.

If you are stuck: Use separate Name and Sound cards, model one response at a time and let the learner repeat the complete cue word before isolating its beginning.

Common misconceptions
  • A letter name and its sound are the same response — Show whether the task asks for the alphabet name or the sound heard in the cue word.
  • Upper-case and lower-case forms are different letters — Say the shared letter name while placing the two case cards together.
  • The first sound of any example word is suitable — Use the agreed cue word and the common sound explicitly taught for that printed letter.
Listen, explain and apply

See the letter, name it, then give a sound

Show the printed letter itself. Ask for its alphabet name separately from a common sound. Use familiar cue words and include both vowels and consonants; a letter can represent different sounds in different words.

Match different-looking partners

Show B, d, D, b. Make two upper-case and lower-case pairs, then name each pair.

Answer and teaching guidance

B pairs with b; D pairs with d. Each pair shares a letter identity and name even though the two forms look different.

Check name versus sound

Show m. A child says “em” when asked for its common sound. What would answer the sound question?

Answer and teaching guidance

Em is the letter name. The common sound is the humming sound at the beginning of moon. Model the two questions separately.

Use a familiar name carefully

Show the name Sam. Name its first letter, find its other case form and give its common sound.

Answer and teaching guidance

S, named ess, pairs with s and commonly represents the beginning sound of sun. Use the printed form rather than assuming every name follows the same sound pattern.

Ready to move on

Show R, e and T. Find each case partner, name the letter and give the common sound using rabbit, egg and top as cues.

Answer and teaching guidance

R/r: ar, rabbit beginning; E/e: ee, egg beginning; T/t: tee, top beginning. Accept the taught local letter-name pronunciation. Sample the rest of the alphabet over later sessions.

Check the exceptional cues

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • Which lower-case letter matches upper-case A?
  • Which upper-case letter matches lower-case m?
  • What is the common sound for the letter m?
  • What is the common sound for the letter s?

Mastery evidence

  • Which lower-case letter matches upper-case A?
  • Which upper-case letter matches lower-case m?
  • What is the common sound for the letter m?
  • What is the common sound for the letter s?
  • The learner selects the correct name and accurately identifies one or more printed letters within it.
  • The learner matches upper-case and lower-case forms by shared letter identity and names the pair accurately.
  • The learner names either case form and gives the taught common speech sound through an accurate cue word.

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Actual printed check choices: A’s lower-case partner is a, not b; m’s upper-case partner is M, not N. For m’s common sound choose the humming beginning of moon rather than its letter name em. For s choose the hissing beginning of sun rather than its name ess. These four checks sample only two letters; use the full reference to revisit both cases and separate names from sounds across all 26.
