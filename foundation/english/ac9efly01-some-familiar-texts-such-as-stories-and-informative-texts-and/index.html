familiar texts and their purposes

I can identify a familiar text, say what it is for and use a cover, symbol or content clue to explain my choice.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can identify a familiar text, say what it is for and use a cover, symbol or content clue to explain my choice.

Learning intention: I can identify a familiar text, say what it is for and use a cover, symbol or content clue to explain my choice.

Success criteria

  • I can name a familiar text such as a story, information page, sign or instruction.
  • I can state whether the text is made to entertain, inform, instruct or guide.
  • I can use a cover, symbol, word, image or layout clue to justify the purpose.
  • I can group texts by topic without assuming they all have the same purpose.
  • I can check an early cover prediction against the content inside the text.
Key vocabulary
  • text — something people make to communicate meaning through words, images, sounds or symbols
  • purpose — the main job a text is designed to do
  • story — a literary text with characters and events, often made to entertain
  • informative text — a text made to teach facts or information about a topic
  • symbol — a picture or mark that carries an agreed meaning
  • cover — the front of a book, which can show its title and clues about its content
  • topic — what a text is mainly about
  • clue — a feature that helps a reader identify the text or its purpose
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: use book-cover clues to identify purpose

Compare two covers: The Wombat Who Lost a Sock shows an illustrated character and an adventure title; Wombat Facts shows a labelled photograph and promises true information.

Worked example 2: identify the purpose of an everyday symbol

A green EXIT sign shows a running figure, a doorway and an arrow.

The same routine works for a story, fact page, recipe, sign or message: identify it, name its job and point to the clue.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Explain why an everyday symbol is used

Symbols in everyday places can guide, warn, identify or give a shared message quickly.

Teacher: Name where the symbol appears. Describe its shapes, pictures or colours. Explain what people should know or do.

Ask: What does this symbol help people know or do, and which feature gives the clue?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a familiar symbol and explains its practical purpose using a visible feature.

Look for: The learner identifies a familiar symbol and explains its practical purpose using a visible feature.

If incorrect: Act out the action the symbol requests, then return to the arrow or picture that signalled it.

E2: Group imaginative and informative books by cover clues

Covers can suggest whether a book is an imaginative story or an informative text, and the inside content confirms the choice.

Teacher: Inspect the title and cover image. Predict Story or Informative. Open the book and confirm with content evidence.

Ask: What does the cover make you predict, and what inside the book confirms it?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: groups the text appropriately and cites both a cover clue and a confirming content clue.

Look for: The learner groups the text appropriately and cites both a cover clue and a confirming content clue.

If incorrect: Offer one story cover and one fact cover with strongly contrasting clues, then check one inside page together.

E3: Group texts by a shared topic

Imaginative and informative texts can be grouped together because they share a topic even when their types and purposes differ.

Teacher: Name each text's topic. Place texts with the same topic together. Compare the purpose of texts inside the group.

Ask: Which texts belong in the farm-animal group, and what different jobs do they do?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: groups by shared topic and then distinguishes at least two purposes within the group.

Look for: The learner groups by shared topic and then distinguishes at least two purposes within the group.

If incorrect: Use a large topic picture in the centre and ask whether each cover belongs near it before discussing purpose.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • All books have the same purpose — Place a story and fact book side by side and use inside-text clues to name the different jobs.
  • Pictures always mean a text is imaginative — Compare an illustrated story with a labelled information diagram and ask what each picture helps the text do.
  • Texts about the same topic must be the same type — Keep the topic fixed, then sort by purpose: entertain, inform, instruct or guide.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Which cover clue suggests that The Wombat Who Lost a Sock is a story?
  • What job does the EXIT symbol do, and which feature shows that job?
  • How can a farm-animal story and a farm-animal fact page share a topic but have different purposes?
  • After predicting from a cover, what should you check inside the text?
