AC9EFLY06 • Foundation English

shared editing of short written texts

I can say an idea, draft a short sentence, spell a CVC word by listening to its sound positions, then reread and make helpful edits.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can say an idea, draft a short sentence, spell a CVC word by listening to its sound positions, then reread and make helpful edits.

Learning intention: I can say an idea, draft a short sentence, spell a CVC word by listening to its sound positions, then reread and make helpful edits.

Success criteria

  • I can say one intended idea.
  • I can draft a complete short sentence.
  • I can spell a CVC word by sound position.
  • I can reread for meaning.
  • I can edit a capital, full stop or spelling choice and explain why.
Key vocabulary
  • draft — a first version that can be improved
  • edit — check and improve a text
  • sentence — words that work together to express a complete idea
  • capital letter — a large letter used at a sentence beginning or for a name
  • full stop — a mark that can show a telling sentence has ended
  • spelling — choosing and ordering letters to write words
  • CVC word — a short word with a consonant sound, a vowel sound and a final consonant sound, such as cat
Model and guided application

Worked example: edit a sentence about an event

Worked-model method: say, draft, spell a CVC word, reread and edit.

1. The group wants to report that Sam fed the class cat; the visible draft ‘sam fed the cat’ begins the name with lower-case s and has no ending mark.2. Say cat slowly, segment it into three sound positions, spell one taught letter for each position and blend the written CVC word back to check it.3. Read the whole draft aloud, edit the name to begin with upper-case S and add a full stop: ‘Sam fed the cat.’

Model answer: The edited sentence clearly reports the event; cat is checked as a CVC spelling, upper-case S begins the sentence and the full stop marks its end.

Worked example: spell a CVC word inside a short observation

Worked-model method: say, draft, spell a CVC word, reread and edit.

1. Say the observation first: ‘We saw a cat.’2. Say cat slowly, segment its three sound positions, spell the CVC word and blend it back to check that the written word says cat.3. Reread the visible sentence ‘We saw a cat.’ and explicitly check that upper-case W begins it, the full stop ends it and the words carry the intended meaning.

Model answer: The sentence records one clear observation, uses checked CVC spelling for cat and has a clear capital-letter beginning and full-stop ending.

Learning activities
  1. Shared sentence — Say one class event together and choose the clearest words before the teacher writes them.
    idea → spoken sentence → written draft
  2. CVC spelling check — Choose one CVC word in the sentence, say it slowly, segment its sound positions, spell it and blend it back to check.
    say • segment • spell • check
  3. Publish and check — Copy the edited sentence clearly and use the class checklist before sharing it.
    meaning • capital • ending mark • spelling
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Use beginning writing knowledge to create a text

A short text grows from a spoken idea through known words, taught sound–letter relationships and basic sentence punctuation.

Teacher: Say, 'My seed grew.' Write known words and use taught sound–letter knowledge for the remaining word. Add a capital at the beginning and a full stop at the end, then reread.

Ask: What idea are you recording, and which writing choices help another person read it?

Students: Say the intended sentence, draft it, segment and spell its CVC word, then add the sentence boundary marks and reread the complete text.

Look for: The learner writes or contributes to one meaningful sentence using known vocabulary, plausible spelling and sentence-boundary punctuation.

If incorrect: Provide the known words on cards, stretch one decodable word orally and let the learner place a capital and full-stop card.

E2: Respond to a text through drawing and writing

Drawing, labels, early sentences and digital tools can work together to communicate a personal response to a text idea or event.

Teacher: Choose one meaningful event from the shared text. Draw or select an image that clearly represents it. Add a label, dictated sentence or typed beginning that shares the response.

Ask: Which text event are you showing, and what do you want another person to understand about your response?

Students: Choose one text event, draw or select an image for it and add a connected label, dictated sentence or typed response.

Look for: The learner's drawing and words represent a recognisable text event and communicate a connected thought or feeling.

If incorrect: Place the event picture beside the response page and model a complete response: 'I thought Niko felt worried because the boot was missing.'

E3: Read back a self-created text

Reading a draft back to an experienced writer lets the learner check whether the written marks still carry the intended message.

Teacher: Place the learner's draft where both writers can see it. Invite the learner to point and read the intended sentence aloud. Compare spoken meaning with the marks and choose one useful edit.

Ask: What does your text say, and is there any place where the writing does not yet show your intended idea?

Students: Point to and read the self-created draft, compare its marks with the intended message and choose one edit that improves clarity.

Look for: The learner reads or explains the draft, tracks its parts and identifies or accepts one edit that improves message clarity.

If incorrect: Let the learner dictate the sentence again while the experienced writer points to the corresponding written parts.

E4: Find capital letters during shared editing

Circling capital letters at sentence beginnings makes one sentence-boundary feature visible during shared editing.

Teacher: Reread the shared text and listen for where each sentence begins. Point to the first printed letter of each sentence. Circle the capital and explain its sentence-beginning job.

Ask: Which capital begins this sentence, and how do you know this is the beginning?

Students: Reread each sentence, locate its ending, circle the upper-case letter at its beginning and explain the capital letter's job.

Look for: The learner locates and circles sentence-opening capital letters and links each one to a complete sentence boundary.

If incorrect: Show one sentence at a time, mark its ending first and then return to circle the capital at its beginning.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Editing means copying the same draft neatly — Compare draft and edited sentence, then name the change and why it helps.
  • Every possible change should happen at once — Choose one check at a time: meaning first, then sentence boundary and spelling.
  • A correct full stop fixes unclear meaning — Read the whole sentence aloud and repair the words or order before checking punctuation.
