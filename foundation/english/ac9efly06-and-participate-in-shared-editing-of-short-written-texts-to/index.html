FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Write a short message and edit it together AC9EFLY06

Learning intention: I can say an idea, record it with words or pictures, read it back and help improve its meaning, capitals, full stops or CVC spelling.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

An edit makes a chosen improvement

The supplied draft says “the cat sat”. The intended idea is a cat sitting. Shared editing changes the first letter to T and adds a full stop: “The cat sat.” Read the result back. Cat can be spelt by mapping /k/ /a/ /t/ to c, a, t. The two sentence-boundary edits and the sound-spelling check have different jobs.

Learn the skill

1. Start with a message worth recording

Say or show one idea about a real, imagined or text event. An adult can scribe it while you contribute known words and taught sound–letter choices. A drawing and words should communicate the same intended idea.

Intended message: “My cat sat.” Cat is the CVC target.

Try it · Which three sound positions can help spell cat?

/k/ /a/ /t/. Use c, a, t in this word, then blend and reread. My needs prior word knowledge or adult support; do not pretend every word is CVC.

2. Read your own draft back to a partner

Read or explain what your marks mean to an experienced writer. Compare that intention with the visible draft. You can keep an effective word and change one confusing part. Editing is more than copying neatly.

Draft word cards: “sam the cat fed”. Intended meaning: Sam gave food to the cat.

Try it · How could shared editing make that intended event clear?

Arrange the words as “Sam fed the cat.” Capitalise Sam and add the full stop. Keep the intended meaning rather than accepting any different plausible event.

3. Check one sentence feature at a time

After checking meaning, locate the beginning and ending of each sentence. Find the sentence-opening capital, then the full stop. Check a taught CVC spelling by sounds. One capital may do two jobs.

Complete text: “Sam fed the cat. It sat.”

Try it · Which capitals begin sentences, and does Sam need a separate second capital for the name?

S and I begin the two sentences. The same S starts the name Sam and sentence one; no extra capital is needed. The two full stops mark the telling sentences’ endings.

Common mistakes

“Editing means every word was wrong.” Keep useful parts and make a chosen improvement.

“Neat copying fixes meaning.” Read the whole message and compare it with the intended idea.

“Every word here is CVC.” Cat and sat fit that pattern. Other words need their own taught knowledge or support.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Say, write, read back, improve

Choose an event from the supplied miniature or a safe imagined event. Say the message, draw or write it with support, then read it back. Choose one helpful edit together and explain what it improves.

Adjust the challenge Starter: arrange known word cards and add a full-stop card. Core: contribute a short sentence and one edit. Stretch: explain a meaning repair as well as a sentence-boundary or CVC-spelling check.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. “the dog ran.” already has a full stop. What could improve its beginning?

Change the first t to capital T: “The dog ran.” Keep the full stop.

2. The complete telling sentence “I can hop” has no ending mark. What can we add?

A full stop: “I can hop.” I is already a capital.

3. Does “My seed grew.” give us an initial short-vowel word with one letter for each of three sounds?

No. Use cat for this initial single-letter, short-vowel model. Seed has three sounds, /s/ /ee/ /d/, but ee is a two-letter vowel spelling. Grew has a /g/ /r/ start and the vowel spelling ew. Teach those patterns separately rather than treating them like cat.

FAQ

Should every correction happen at once?

No. Keep the child’s intended message, then choose one useful check at a time.

Can an adult scribe?

Yes. The child can contribute the idea, words, sound–letter choices or editing decisions. Record support honestly rather than claiming independent writing.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • draft — a first version that can be improved
  • edit — check and improve a text
  • sentence — words that work together to express a complete idea
  • capital letter — a large letter used at a sentence beginning or for a name
  • full stop — a mark that can show a telling sentence has ended
  • spelling — choosing and ordering letters to write words
  • CVC word — a short word with a consonant sound, a vowel sound and a final consonant sound, such as cat
Key concept

I can create a short text and help edit capitals, punctuation and simple spellings.

Learning goal: I can create a short text and help edit capitals, punctuation and simple spellings.

I am ready when I can

  • I can say one intended idea.
  • I can draft a complete short sentence.
  • I can spell a CVC word by sound position.
  • I can reread for meaning.
  • I can edit a capital, full stop or spelling choice and explain why.

See the skill in action

Worked-model method: say, draft, spell a CVC word, reread and edit.

1. The group wants to report that Sam fed the class cat; the visible draft ‘sam fed the cat’ begins the name with lower-case s and has no ending mark.2. Say cat slowly, segment it into three sound positions, spell one taught letter for each position and blend the written CVC word back to check it.3. Read the whole draft aloud, edit the name to begin with upper-case S and add a full stop: ‘Sam fed the cat.’

Model answer: The edited sentence clearly reports the event; cat is checked as a CVC spelling, upper-case S begins the sentence and the full stop marks its end.

Worked example: spell a CVC word inside a short observation

Worked-model method: say, draft, spell a CVC word, reread and edit.

1. Say the observation first: ‘We saw a cat.’2. Say cat slowly, segment its three sound positions, spell the CVC word and blend it back to check that the written word says cat.3. Reread the visible sentence ‘We saw a cat.’ and explicitly check that upper-case W begins it, the full stop ends it and the words carry the intended meaning.

Model answer: The sentence records one clear observation, uses checked CVC spelling for cat and has a clear capital-letter beginning and full-stop ending.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Use beginning writing knowledge to create a text

A short text grows from a spoken idea through known words, taught sound–letter relationships and basic sentence punctuation.

Think: Say, 'My seed grew.' Write known words and use taught sound–letter knowledge for the remaining word. Add a capital at the beginning and a full stop at the end, then reread.

Try: What idea are you recording, and which writing choices help another person read it?

A strong answer: Say the intended sentence, draft it, use taught spelling knowledge, then add the sentence boundary marks and reread the complete text. For a separate CVC model, say “My cat sat.” and segment cat as /k/ /a/ /t/.

Check: The learner writes or contributes to one meaningful sentence using known vocabulary, plausible spelling and sentence-boundary punctuation.

If you are stuck: Provide the known words on cards, stretch one decodable word orally and let the learner place a capital and full-stop card.

E2: Respond to a text through drawing and writing

Drawing, labels, early sentences and digital tools can work together to communicate a personal response to a text idea or event.

Think: Choose one meaningful event from the shared text. Draw or select an image that clearly represents it. Add a label, dictated sentence or typed beginning that shares the response.

Try: Which text event are you showing, and what do you want another person to understand about your response?

A strong answer: Choose one text event, draw or select an image for it and add a connected label, dictated sentence or typed response.

Check: The learner's drawing and words represent a recognisable text event and communicate a connected thought or feeling.

If you are stuck: Place the event picture beside the response page and model a complete response: 'I thought Niko felt worried because the boot was missing.'

E3: Read back a self-created text

Reading a draft back to an experienced writer lets the learner check whether the written marks still carry the intended message.

Think: Place the learner's draft where both writers can see it. Invite the learner to point and read the intended sentence aloud. Compare spoken meaning with the marks and choose one useful edit.

Try: What does your text say, and is there any place where the writing does not yet show your intended idea?

A strong answer: Point to and read the self-created draft, compare its marks with the intended message and choose one edit that improves clarity.

Check: The learner reads or explains the draft, tracks its parts and identifies or accepts one edit that improves message clarity.

If you are stuck: Let the learner dictate the sentence again while the experienced writer points to the corresponding written parts.

E4: Find capital letters during shared editing

Circling capital letters at sentence beginnings makes one sentence-boundary feature visible during shared editing.

Think: Reread the shared text and listen for where each sentence begins. Point to the first printed letter of each sentence. Circle the capital and explain its sentence-beginning job.

Try: Which capital begins this sentence, and how do you know this is the beginning?

A strong answer: Reread each sentence, locate its ending, circle the upper-case letter at its beginning and explain the capital letter's job.

Check: The learner locates and circles sentence-opening capital letters and links each one to a complete sentence boundary.

If you are stuck: Show one sentence at a time, mark its ending first and then return to circle the capital at its beginning.

Use paper/word cards and drawing tools, or an accessible digital tool with adult support.

Common misconceptions
  • Editing means copying the same draft neatly — Compare draft and edited sentence, then name the change and why it helps.
  • Every possible change should happen at once — Choose one check at a time: meaning first, then sentence boundary and spelling.
  • A correct full stop fixes unclear meaning — Read the whole sentence aloud and repair the words or order before checking punctuation.
Explain and apply

Say the idea first

Before writing, say the whole idea aloud: My seed grew. Does it tell one complete message?

Answer and teaching guidance

A clear spoken idea gives the draft a target. If the idea is unclear, repair meaning before checking capitals or punctuation.

Edit for meaning

Read sam the cat fed. What does the writer mean? Move the words so the event makes sense.

Answer and teaching guidance

Meaning comes before neat copying. The repaired sentence could be Sam fed the cat. Then check the capital name and full stop.

Check sentence boundaries

In “Sam fed the cat.”, point to the capital beginning and the ending mark. Explain how the same S also starts the name Sam.

Answer and teaching guidance

This connects shared editing to visible sentence features without turning it into a handwriting task only.

Spell and reread

Say cat slowly, map each sound to a letter, write it, then reread the whole sentence.

Answer and teaching guidance

Support: use counters for the three sounds. Extend: replace cat with cot and explain how the meaning changes.
