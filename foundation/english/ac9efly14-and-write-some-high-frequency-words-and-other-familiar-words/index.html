Read and write familiar words AC9EFLY14

Learning intention: I can read useful common words, check the whole word and write a word I have learnt.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Connect sounds, meaning and the exact spelling

Read “The dog is on the rug.” Point to the. Adult: say it naturally and teach the th team and the vowel as pronounced in this sentence; the vowel may be the weak “uh” sound rather than a usual short-e sound. Notice the exact spelling t-h-e. Cover the model, write the word, uncover and compare every letter. Familiarity grows through meaningful reading and accurate sound–spelling attention, not outline guessing.

Learn the skill

1. Read a taught common word

Look through the complete word and use the taught sound–spelling links. Some common words have an unusual or not-yet-taught part. An adult explains that part instead of asking for a guess.

Printed choices: and, my, is. Adult: read the sentence “The dog is here”, pointing to is, then revisit the word alone.

Try it · Which printed word was is? What did you check?

Is is i-s. Notice its vowel and the voiced final /z/ sound represented by s here. Compare the complete word with and and my, rather than choosing by its length alone.

2. Check the word in a sentence

Read the actual printed word, then check meaning and grammar in its sentence. Context helps confirm a reading; it does not replace looking at the letters.

Printed sentence: The cat is on my mat. Target cards: my / me.

Try it · Which card matches the word before mat? How does it fit the sentence?

My matches the printed m-y and tells whose mat it is. Me has a different final letter and does not fit this sentence position.

3. Write and compare a familiar word

Read a taught word and discuss its useful spelling features. Cover it, write it and check the full sequence. Personally familiar names are useful too, with their own exact spelling and capitalisation.

Printed model: go. Adult: read it, explain g and the o pronounced like its name here, then cover the model. Provide blank paper.

Try it · Write go, uncover the model and compare.

The word is g-o. Check both letters in order and reread it. Record whether the learner wrote from memory, copied or needed a prompt; those show different levels of support.

Common mistakes

“The first letter tells the whole word.” Track to the end and compare every letter.

“Common words cannot use sound knowledge.” Use known correspondences and explicitly teach unusual or new parts.

“Context lets me ignore print.” Read the printed sequence and use context to confirm it.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One common word and one meaningful name

Choose a taught common word such as my and a preferred name important to the learner. Supply their exact printed models, read them, discuss letters and sounds, then cover for a writing attempt. Uncover, compare and use each in a meaningful spoken sentence.

Adjust the challenge Starter: select and read one word beside a model. Core: read it in a sentence and write it after covering. Stretch: distinguish my from me in two supplied sentences: “This is my bag” and “Come with me.” Explain the final-letter difference.

Use only some taught words at a time; no speed gate or exhaustive list is required. Names may have spellings from many languages. Check the preferred spelling with the learner or family rather than inventing a pronunciation rule.

Three tricky questions

1. Is is spelt i-z because its last sound is /z/?

No. Its conventional spelling is i-s. Teach how s represents /z/ in this word.

2. Does the outside shape of the prove its spelling?

No. Check t-h-e in sequence and connect those letters with the spoken word.

3. If a child copied a name correctly, have they written it from memory?

No. Accurate copying is useful supported practice. Check recall separately after covering the model.

FAQ

Which words should we teach?

Choose useful high-frequency and personally familiar words that appear in current reading and writing. Revisit them meaningfully and teach their sound–spelling details.

What if the word has an unusual part?

Identify the known parts and explicitly model the new or unusual part. Return to the whole word in a clear sentence.

Key vocabulary
  • high-frequency word — a word that appears very often in texts
  • familiar word — a word known from everyday life or repeated reading
  • recognise — know something when you see or hear it
  • memory — what the mind keeps and can recall
  • context — the sentence or situation around a word
  • check — compare a response carefully with a model
Key concept

I can read and write some common words and words that are very familiar to me.

Learning goal: I can read and write some common words and words that are very familiar to me.

I am ready when I can

  • I can recognise taught high-frequency words.
  • I can read a familiar word in context.
  • I can write a taught word from memory.
  • I can check every letter in order.
  • I can use the word meaningfully in a sentence.

Model answer: The word the is read as a whole in context and written accurately after every letter is checked.

Worked example: learn a personally familiar word

Worked-model method: look, say, cover, write and check every letter.

1. Choose the learner’s printed name from a small set of classroom name cards.2. Say the name, trace its letters with a finger and notice the upper-case first letter.3. Cover, write the name from memory, then uncover and compare every letter in order.

Model answer: A familiar name becomes easier to read and write when the learner connects its meaning with its complete letter sequence.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Read and write high-frequency words in texts

High-frequency words become readily available when learners meet them repeatedly in meaningful shared and independent reading, then practise writing the complete sequence.

Think: Find the printed word the in 'The dog is on the rug.' Read it in the sentence and notice all three letters. Cover, write the and compare every letter before rereading the sentence.

Try: Which whole word appears often here, and how will you check every letter after writing it?

A strong answer: Find the target high-frequency word in a sentence, read it, use look, say, cover and write, then check every printed letter.

Check: The learner recognises a taught high-frequency word in connected text, writes it from memory and checks its complete letter sequence.

If you are stuck: Keep the model visible for the first copy, say the word in a sentence and cover it for only a brief memory attempt.

E2: Read and write personally familiar words

Names of people, characters, places and school contexts become familiar through meaningful repeated use.

Think: Select the learner's name or a familiar character name. Read it, trace the complete sequence and notice its opening capital where appropriate. Cover, write and compare each letter in order.

Try: What makes this word familiar to you, and which letters confirm that it is the right word?

A strong answer: Choose a personally familiar name or label, read the complete word, cover and write it, then compare every letter in order.

Check: The learner reads a personally familiar word, writes or copies it accurately and verifies the full sequence.

If you are stuck: Use a photo or character picture beside the name card, begin with letter tiles and move to writing when the order is secure.

Common misconceptions
  • Only the first letter needs to look right — Track from first letter to last and compare the complete written word with the model.
  • Remembering the outside shape is enough — Name or point to the letters in order so a change inside the word is noticed.
  • A memorised word never needs context — Read the word in a complete sentence and check that its meaning and grammar fit.
Explain and apply

Read the whole word

Point to the in The dog is on the rug and read it as one familiar word.

Answer and teaching guidance

High-frequency word practice should use the complete word, not only the first letter.

Look, cover, write, check

Look at my, cover it, write it, then compare every letter with the model.

Answer and teaching guidance

This builds accurate memory for short familiar words without relying on guessing.

Use sentence context

Choose the word that fits: The dog ____ here.

Answer and teaching guidance

Context checks meaning and grammar. The best word is is because it completes the sentence.

Repair familiar words

Fix Teh dog si on the rug. Which two words changed?

Answer and teaching guidance

The repaired sentence is The dog is on the rug. Learners should identify the and is as complete word repairs.

A complete sentence choice

Use the exact alternatives is / and / my in “The dog ____ here.” Is completes this supplied sentence. Context supports meaning, but inspect i-s rather than guess. For the repair example, show “Teh dog si on the rug” and compare t-h-e and i-s with the intended complete sentence.

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Use four different taught targets for the four repeated common-word prompts: (1) and / ant; (2) my / mud; (3) is / ink; (4) the / ten. An adult reads the named target and the child selects it, then reads or communicates the chosen word. The first option matches each target. All alternatives are real words: frequency is not a property a child can infer from an unexplained list. Follow with genuine reading and covered-model writing of some taught words.
