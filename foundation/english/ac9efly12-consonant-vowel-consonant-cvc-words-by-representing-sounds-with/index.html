FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Read and write CVC words AC9EFLY12

Learning intention: I can use taught letter sounds to read a CVC word and write each sound in order.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Hear hen; write hen; read it back

Adult: say “hen” without showing the spelling. The learner repeats it and moves three sound counters. Write h for the first sound, e for the short middle vowel and n for the final sound. Then show the completed word, point under each letter and blend hen. For reading, begin with the printed letters; for spelling, begin with the spoken word.

Learn the skill

1. Blend the printed word

Point from left to right. Say the taught sound linked to each letter and join the sounds smoothly. Check that the whole word makes sense; do not guess from a picture or the first letter.

Printed word: map. Taught cues: moon beginning for m, apple beginning for a, pig beginning for p.

Try it · Read the word. How did you check all three positions?

/m/ /æ/ /p/ blend to map. Touch each printed letter in order. The word names something that shows places; meaning confirms the blended reading.

2. Map a spoken word into letters

Listen to the whole word, segment its sounds and choose a taught letter for every position. The middle vowel must be represented as well as the consonants.

Adult: say “bed” without displaying it. Supply blank paper and taught letter cards b, e, d, a, t.

Try it · Write the word you heard, then read it back.

Bed: b, e, d in order. Use three counters if needed. If a sound is missing, say the word again and locate the empty sound position before choosing its letter.

3. Change one position and reread

Change only the named letter, use its sound and blend the whole new word. Compare first, middle and final positions instead of guessing that the word stayed the same.

Printed word: sit. Replace i with a to make sat. Then replace s with m.

Try it · Read the last word. Which positions changed in each step?

The last word is mat. Sit to sat changes the middle vowel; sat to mat changes the first consonant. The other two positions stay fixed at each step. Read all three letters after each change.

Common mistakes

“The first letter is enough.” Read every position and blend the whole word.

“The vowel can be left out.” Stretch the word and listen to the middle sound.

“Letter names blend into the word.” Use the sounds associated with the printed letters.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Hear one, read another

Adult: say “dog” for writing without showing it. Then show the different word “cup” for reading. Ask the learner to point to all positions and explain any repair. Keep the two tasks separate so a displayed word does not give away the spelling answer.

Adjust the challenge Starter: two of three letter positions supplied with counters. Core: spell a spoken CVC word and blend a different printed word. Stretch: explain why hn cannot fully spell hen, then repair it and read the whole word.

Use previously taught correspondences and familiar vocabulary. An adult supplies actual dictated speech. Copying a visible spelling is supported practice, not independent spelling from sound.

Three tricky questions

1. A child writes hn for hen. Is the final sound missing?

No. The final n is present. The middle vowel e is missing.

2. Can the letters in map be rearranged freely?

No. Sound order matters. Map and pam have different sequences; use the intended whole word as your check.

3. Is every three-letter word a simple CVC word?

No. The pattern concerns consonant–vowel–consonant sounds and their taught spellings. Use simple examples such as cat, bed, sit, dog and sun here.

FAQ

Must a child name a picture before reading?

No. For reading, show the word and blend its letters first. Use a picture or explanation only to confirm meaning.

What should we revisit if reading is a guess?

Return to the uncertain letter–sound link, model blending in order and try a different taught CVC word.

Key vocabulary
  • CVC word — a word with a consonant sound, a vowel sound and another consonant sound
  • consonant — a speech sound made with some closing or narrowing in the mouth
  • vowel — an open speech sound that forms the centre of a syllable
  • segment — break a spoken word into separate speech sounds
  • blend — join letter-linked sounds to read a whole word
  • sound position — the first, middle or final place of a sound in a word
Key concept

I can use letters for sounds to read and write simple consonant-vowel-consonant words.

Learning goal: I can use letters for sounds to read and write simple consonant-vowel-consonant words.

I am ready when I can

  • I can hear three sound positions.
  • I can choose a letter for each sound.
  • I can keep letters in sound order.
  • I can blend a written CVC word.
  • I can use meaning to confirm the reading.

Say it, map it, read it

hen

Use letter sounds when blending. Use the picture to confirm meaning after reading.

Try a new word

Say it. Write the sounds. Read your word.

Show answer and teaching guidance

sun: s, u, n in order. Adult: name the picture if needed. If the vowel is missing, say the word again and focus on its middle sound.

Important questions and answers

A child writes “hn” for hen. Which sound and letter are missing?

Show answer and teaching guidance

The middle vowel sound, represented by e. Say hen, move three counters and put one letter under each counter.

Read “can”. Change c to m. Read the new word. What stayed the same?

Show answer and teaching guidance

man. The middle vowel a and final consonant n stay the same; only the first sound and letter change.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Adult: say “bed” without showing the spelling. Ask the child to write it. Then show “map” and ask the child to read it.

Review hint: say the sounds in order for spelling; point under the letters and blend for reading.

Show answer and teaching guidance

bed; map. Check spelling and reading separately. Use familiar, previously taught letter-sound correspondences.

Exit ticket

Adult: say “pen”. Child: write the word. Adult: show “sit”. Child: read the word.

Show answer and teaching guidance

pen; sit. Look for all three sound positions in order and blending without guessing. If needed, use counters and reteach the uncertain letter-sound correspondence.

A fresh middle-sound comparison

Printed words: hen / hot. Adult reads both after the learner tries. E in hen and o in hot are different middle-vowel spellings; the final consonants also differ, so this is not a one-position substitution. For a one-position change use hen → pen, changing only the initial h to p.

Model answer: Cat is a CVC word because its three letters represent a consonant sound, a vowel sound and a final consonant sound in order.

Worked example: build and read sun

Worked-model method: segment, map letters, blend and check the CVC word.

1. Say sun slowly and listen for three speech sounds.2. Choose printed lower-case s using sun as its own cue, lower-case u for the short middle sound in up, and lower-case n for the sound at the start of nest.3. Place s, u and n in order and blend the written word sun.

Model answer: Sun has all three sound positions represented, including the vowel letter in the middle.

Common misconceptions
  • The middle vowel can be skipped — Hold the middle counter and stretch the vowel sound before choosing its letter.
  • Letter names should be blended into the word — Use each printed letter’s taught sound, then blend the speech sounds smoothly.
  • A picture guess is enough — Point to and blend every printed letter before using the picture to confirm meaning.

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Adult: say cat and dog without displaying their spellings for the writing checks. Cat uses c-a-t; dog uses d-o-g. For the separate reading checks show c-a-t and m-a-p; the learner blends cat and map. Printed spelling choices, if using recognition support: cat / cta and dog / dgo. Selecting a spelling is supported recognition, not independent writing from dictation.
