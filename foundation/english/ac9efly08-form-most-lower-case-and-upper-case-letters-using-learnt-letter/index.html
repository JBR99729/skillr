FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Form letters with a taught movement AC9EFLY08

Learning intention: I can watch where a letter starts, follow its movement and check the letter I make.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Watch, move, compare

Adult: place your school’s approved lower-case c and upper-case T models on paper. Demonstrate each complete letter once, then slowly repeat its actual start and stroke direction. The learner sky-writes the movement, traces the model and makes a fresh copy. Compare the new copy with the demonstrated shape, size and orientation; the printed samples below identify letters but do not prescribe a school handwriting script.

Learn the skill

1. Watch the starting point and direction

Watch the whole letter first. Then notice where each stroke begins and the route it follows. A recognisable finished shape does not tell us which movement the writer used.

Adult: draw the school-approved lower-case c with a start dot and direction arrow. Demonstrate the movement aloud. Provide blank paper beside this actual model.

Try it · Point to the start, sky-write the movement, then make your own c.

Check the learner against the supplied school model: the same starting point and direction, a recognisable curved shape and an open right side. Demonstrate again if the movement is uncertain.

2. Place each form on writing guides

Use the writing guides taught at school. Compare a lower-case form with its upper-case partner; the capital may have a different shape, not just a larger size.

Adult: draw the school’s writing guides and model lower-case t and upper-case T on them, with their taught starts and stroke order. Leave a blank pair of guides.

Try it · Form t and T on the blank guides. What will you compare?

Compare each letter with its own model for start, movement, height, placement and orientation. Do not enlarge lower-case t and call it the capital; use the taught T form.

3. Write and check with a comfortable hand

A functional grip lets the pencil move with control and without pain or excessive effort. Try a short writing turn, pause and compare one letter before writing more. Formation is different from naming a letter.

Adult: demonstrate the school-approved n, then cover the model after a supported trace. Invite one fresh n on paper. Ask about hand comfort.

Try it · How can you check the letter and your hand?

Uncover the model and compare the start, direction and recognisable form. Ask whether the hand feels comfortable. Adjust paper angle, tool or support if needed; do not force one identical grip for every learner.

Keep both cases in view

These samples help locate each letter. An adult supplies the approved writing model and demonstrates start and direction before formation practice. Revisit the alphabet in manageable sets.

Common mistakes

“If it looks close, the movement does not matter.” Follow the taught start and direction to build a reliable movement.

“Pressing harder improves every letter.” Use enough pressure to make a clear mark without strain.

“Naming a letter proves I can form it.” Watch the actual writing movement and inspect the new letter.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Three letters, three careful tries

An adult selects one curved, one straight and one slanting letter from the school model, demonstrates each and supplies a practice line. Sky-write, trace, make a fresh copy and compare. Return to another small set later so both cases across the alphabet are taught over time.

Adjust the challenge Starter: a large movement and one trace. Core: form a fresh letter on guides after the model. Stretch: form a taught upper/lower-case pair from a named prompt, then compare each with the model.

Provide accessible tools and short turns. Stop if writing hurts and seek suitable school support. A typed answer, selected glyph or static sample does not demonstrate pencil movement.

Three tricky questions

1. Must every child hold a pencil in exactly the same way?

No. Aim for a comfortable, functional grip that supports controlled movement.

2. Is a large a automatically an upper-case A?

No. A and a have distinct taught forms. Compare each with the correct case model.

3. Can you learn every letter by copying c’s movement?

No. Letters use different movements. Follow the actual taught model for each letter, revisiting both cases across sessions.

FAQ

Which handwriting style should we use?

Use the school’s approved style consistently. This page supports observing and following it; ordinary screen fonts are identification samples.

What counts as evidence of formation?

An observed writing turn and the learner’s fresh letters. Note any tracing, start dots, demonstrations or physical support supplied.

Key vocabulary
  • formation — the movements used to make a written letter
  • starting point — the place where a letter movement begins
  • direction — the way the pencil moves
  • lower-case — the smaller everyday form of a letter
  • upper-case — the capital form of a letter
  • functional grip — a comfortable pencil hold that allows controlled movement
Key concept

I can form most upper- and lower-case letters using the letter shapes I have learnt.

Learning goal: I can form most upper- and lower-case letters using the letter shapes I have learnt.

I am ready when I can

  • I can use the taught starting point.
  • I can move in the taught direction.
  • I can form recognisable lower-case letters.
  • I can form recognisable upper-case letters.
  • I can self-check shape, size and orientation.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Follow a demonstrated letter movement

A clear handwriting demonstration reveals where a letter begins, which direction the pencil travels and how the strokes create a recognisable form.

Think: Show the complete lower-case c once. Repeat slowly while naming its start near the top and curved direction. Ask the learner to sky-write, trace and then form it independently before comparing.

Try: Where does this taught movement begin, which way does it travel and what shape should remain?

A strong answer: Watch the demonstrated start and direction, sky-write and trace the movement, form the letter independently and compare it with the taught model.

Check: The learner follows the demonstrated start and direction and produces a recognisable form that can be compared with the model.

If you are stuck: Add a start dot and one direction arrow, enlarge the movement and guide sky-writing before returning to pencil and paper.

E2: Develop a comfortable functional pencil grip

A functional grip supports controlled movement without pain or unnecessary tension, and effective grips can look different across learners.

Think: Seat the learner with supported feet and a comfortably placed page. Invite a relaxed pencil hold near enough to the tip for control. Write a short pattern, pause and ask about comfort before adjusting only what is needed.

Try: Can you move the pencil smoothly and comfortably, or is any part of your hand working too hard?

A strong answer: Adjust the seat, paper and pencil hold for comfort, make a controlled letter movement, then explain whether the grip supports easy movement.

Check: The learner uses a comfortable grasp that permits controlled short movements without excessive pressure, pain or rapid fatigue.

If you are stuck: Try a thicker tool, shorter writing burst, page-angle change or supportive grip aid while keeping the same letter goal.

Common misconceptions
  • Any movement is fine if the final shape looks close — Slow down and copy the taught start and direction so the movement becomes reliable.
  • A tight grip gives more control — Relax the fingers, rest the hand and paper comfortably, then try a short smooth movement.
  • Upper-case means only making a lower-case letter larger — Compare the two taught forms and name the strokes or shape features that differ.

Start at the model

Before forming a new letter, point to its modelled starting point.

Answer and teaching guidance

Formation improves when learners notice where to begin and which direction to move before writing.

Describe the movement

Trace lower-case c, n and v. Which one curves, bumps or slants?

Answer and teaching guidance

This builds useful formation language without requiring every school to use the same handwriting script.

Compare with the model

After copying a letter, compare its shape, size and movement with the taught model.

Answer and teaching guidance

The check should focus on recognisable formation, not speed or decoration.

Write a word slowly

Copy one simple word and circle a letter whose starting point and movement you can explain.

Answer and teaching guidance

Support: copy one letter at a time. Extend: compare an upper-case letter with its lower-case partner.

Quick checks

  • Why does the starting point of a letter matter?
  • Which pair shows the same letter in upper- and lower-case?
  • Which pair matches correctly?

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Complete the checks with these alternatives. Before writing, look at “the taught start and movement” / “only the colour of the pencil”. The start matters because “it supports the taught movement” / “it makes every letter begin in the same place”. Same-letter case pair: A–a / A–b. Correct partner: M–m / M–n. The first option answers each check. These recognition questions do not replace observing formation.
