Key vocabulary
  • vocabulary — the words we understand and use
  • context — the situation or topic that helps a word make sense
  • meaning — the idea a word communicates
  • precise — exact and clear for what we mean
  • topic word — a word that is especially useful when learning or talking about one topic
Key concept

I can learn and use useful words for everyday and school topics.

Learning goal: I can learn and use useful words for everyday and school topics.

I am ready when I can

  • I can connect a familiar word to its meaning.
  • I can use context to choose a fitting word.
  • I can explain a topic word with an example.
  • I can use new vocabulary in a sentence.
  • I can choose a precise word instead of an unrelated word.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Use topic words in learning and imaginative play

Learning areas and imagined roles use precise vocabulary that helps people act, explain and solve problems together.

Think: Name the learning or play context. Choose a word that belongs to it. Use the word while doing, asking or explaining something.

Try: In a pretend shop, which words could a shopkeeper and customer use to buy an apple?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: uses relevant words such as price, dollar, customer or receipt in a meaningful shop exchange rather than merely repeating them.

Check: The learner uses relevant words such as price, dollar, customer or receipt in a meaningful shop exchange rather than merely repeating them.

If you are stuck: Give labelled props and two oral models: How much is the apple? and Here is your receipt.

E2: Build a word web for a school topic

Words studied together can form a topic network, with each word naming a person, relationship, place, action or idea connected to the topic.

Think: Name the topic in the centre. Add a word that belongs to the topic. Explain its connection with a picture, example or short sentence.

Try: How could caregiver, sibling, grandparent and home connect to a class topic called Families?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: explains a relevant connection for a topic word while respecting that different learners' family experiences vary.

Check: The learner explains a relevant connection for a topic word while respecting that different learners' family experiences vary.

If you are stuck: Offer pictures and blank cards so children can point, name, draw or contribute a word used by their family or community.

E3: Grow vocabulary through people, texts, images and objects

A new word becomes more usable when learners hear it in interaction, see it in a text or image, connect it to an object or action and try it themselves.

Think: Hear the word in a meaningful sentence. Connect it to an image, action or object. Say the meaning and use the word in a new context.

Try: You hear the word fragile, see a picture of a cracked shell and carefully hold an empty eggshell. What does fragile mean?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: explains fragile as easy to break or damage and uses it accurately for another suitable object.

Check: The learner explains fragile as easy to break or damage and uses it accurately for another suitable object.

If you are stuck: Contrast a sturdy block and an empty eggshell, then model the sentence The eggshell is fragile because it can break easily.

Common misconceptions
  • A long word is always a better word — Compare long and short choices and select the word whose meaning fits the context most precisely.
  • Repeating a word means understanding it — Ask the learner to point to an example, explain the meaning simply and use the word in a new familiar sentence.
  • A word has exactly the same meaning in every context — Place the word in two familiar sentences and use surrounding clues to discuss how its meaning or use changes.
Explain and apply

Choose a word in context

A cup can break easily. Would fragile or sturdy describe it? Explain.

Answer and teaching guidance

Fragile means easily broken or damaged; sturdy means strong and solid. The clue can break easily supports fragile. Ask for another familiar example.

Similar does not mean identical

Replace small in “We saw a small insect” with tiny. Is the main idea similar?

Answer and teaching guidance

Both describe a small size, but tiny suggests very small. Words with similar meanings may add different detail; choose the word that fits what you mean.

Give a topic word a job

In a pretend shop, use price in a question and receipt in a reply.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example: What is the price of this apple? Here is your receipt. Explain that price tells the cost and a receipt records a purchase. Accept other meaningful exchanges.

Use a new word again

Choose a word from today’s school topic or a personal interest. Explain it, give an example and use it in a new sentence.

Answer and teaching guidance

Judge meaning and use, not spelling or word length. Support: offer a familiar object or two meanings to choose from. Extend: give an example where the word would not fit and explain why.

Try these
  1. Word detective — Read a familiar sentence and use the picture or situation to infer one new word's meaning.
    sentence + context clue → word meaning
  2. Topic basket — Sort picture-word cards into home, personal interest and school-topic contexts, allowing words to fit more than one.
    home • interests • school topics
  3. Use it clearly — Students choose one word, explain it with a familiar example and use it in a short sentence.
    word → meaning → example sentence
