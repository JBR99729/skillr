FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Use sounds and letters to spell AC9EFLY13

Learning intention: I can hear the sounds in a word, choose taught spellings and read back what I wrote.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One sound can use a letter team

Adult: say “fish”. Hear three sounds, then map f | i | sh. The two letters sh work together for the final sound, so the word has four letters but three phonemes. Read the spelling back to check that it says fish. Sound positions and written letters do not always have the same count.

Learn the skill

1. Hear the whole word in order

Say the intended word naturally, then segment it. Keep every heard sound in order before writing. Supply familiar spoken meanings so vocabulary uncertainty does not become a spelling error.

Adult: say “bag”, meaning a container for carrying things. Keep its spelling hidden.

Try it · Say its sounds in order and choose the letters you know.

Bag uses b, a, g for the beginning, short middle vowel and final sound. A first-letter response alone does not spell the whole word.

2. Represent the vowel and any taught team

Choose the spelling taught for each sound position. A vowel can be easy to overlook; a known letter team can represent one sound. Use a fresh word after the model.

Adult: say “shop” without showing it. The learner already knows sh, o and p. Provide blank paper.

Try it · Write the word, then explain any team you used.

Shop: sh | o | p. The initial sh is one sound represented by two letters; the middle vowel is o. Read back the complete sequence to check.

3. Read back and keep meaningful parts

Read your own spelling as written and compare it with the intended word. Repair omissions or order errors. A familiar base can help when a meaningful ending is added.

Adult says “dogs”, meaning more than one dog. The learner writes dog. Show base dog and ending s after the attempt.

Try it · What needs to be added, and what meaning stays?

Add s to make dogs. The base dog keeps the animal meaning; the ending shows more than one here. In ordinary speech this s is voiced /z/. Check both the sounds represented and the familiar meaning part.

Common mistakes

“One sound always needs one letter.” Sh in fish represents one sound with two letters.

“The right letters work in any order.” Compare the written sequence with the intended spoken sequence.

“Only perfect adult spelling shows useful knowledge.” Notice plausible use of taught correspondences, then explicitly teach the next convention.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Say, map, write, repair

Adult: dictate “map”, then “fish”, one at a time without displaying them. The learner maps sounds, writes, and blends back. Ask what they would do if a sound were missing. Finally discuss dog and dogs with the printed base and ending.

Adjust the challenge Starter: supplied counters and two choices for one sound. Core: represent all sounds with taught letters or teams. Stretch: repair plan written as pan by locating the missing /l/, then explain a familiar plural ending.

Dictate real speech and record which spellings have been taught. A plausible attempt is evidence of developing knowledge; distinguish it from fully conventional spelling and from copying a displayed answer.

Three tricky questions

1. Does fish need four sound counters because it has four letters?

No. It has three phonemes; sh represents the last one.

2. For the intended word plan, is pan complete?

No. It misses /l/ after /p/. Put that sound and its l back before reading the word again.

3. Can s in dogs contribute meaning even though it is not a whole word?

Yes. It adds the more-than-one meaning in this example. A meaningful part need not stand alone as a word.

FAQ

Should adults correct every spelling in one turn?

Focus on the taught relationships and one useful next step. Ask the child to explain their choices before modelling a convention they do not yet know.

How can we check independent spelling fairly?

Say a fresh familiar word without revealing its spelling. Provide agreed access supports, record them and inspect every represented sound and any taught meaningful part.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • spell — choose and order letters to write a word
  • sound–letter relationship — a connection between a speech sound and its written letter or letters
  • letter team — two or more letters working together to represent a sound
  • segment — break a spoken word into speech sounds
  • blend — join the sounds represented by letters to read the word
  • plausible spelling — a spelling that sensibly represents the sounds a learner knows
Key concept

I can listen to a word and use what I know about letters and sounds to make a sensible spelling.

Learning goal: I can listen to a word and use what I know about letters and sounds to make a sensible spelling.

I am ready when I can

  • I can segment a spoken word.
  • I can choose taught letters for sounds.
  • I can use a known letter team.
  • I can keep sound order.
  • I can blend back and repair a spelling.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Make a plausible spelling and use a meaningful part

A plausible early spelling represents taught speech sounds in order and can also preserve a familiar meaningful part such as the base dog in dogs.

Think: Say dogs and listen from the beginning to the end. Write the familiar base word dog using known sound–letter relationships. Add the familiar plural ending s, blend back and explain the meaning.

Try: Which sounds and meaningful parts did you represent, and how does your spelling say the word you intended?

A strong answer: Say the intended word, map each heard sound to a taught letter or team, keep any familiar meaningful part, then blend and explain the spelling.

Check: The learner makes a defensible spelling choice using taught sound–letter relationships and, where relevant, a familiar meaningful part.

If you are stuck: Use counters for heard sounds, supply the known base-word card and let the learner choose between two possible ending cards.

Common misconceptions
  • Every speech sound must use exactly one letter — Show the sh letter team in fish and explain that its two letters work together.
  • All the right letters can be in any order — Touch the counters from first to last and place each spelling choice in the same sequence.
  • An early spelling must already use every adult convention — Check whether the learner has plausibly represented taught sound–letter relationships before teaching the next pattern.
Explain and apply

Say and map sounds

Say map slowly. What sound do you hear first, in the middle and at the end?

Answer and teaching guidance

The learner should map heard sounds to taught letters in order before writing the word.

Use every sound

Compare plan and pan. Which sound is missing in pan?

Answer and teaching guidance

This checks whether the spelling represents all heard sounds, including a consonant after the first sound.

Check a letter team

Spell fish. Which two letters work together for the final sound?

Answer and teaching guidance

The final sound in fish is represented by sh. The learner should explain the team as one sound, not two separate ending sounds.

Blend back to check

After writing drum, blend the spelling from left to right. Does it say the intended word?

Answer and teaching guidance

Reading the spelling back catches omitted, reordered or replaced sounds.

First, middle and final repair

Adult says bag. Compare written bg, ag and ba only after an independent attempt. Bg misses the vowel a; ag misses initial b; ba misses final g. Rebuild bag and read back the whole word after each repair.

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • You want to spell ‘map’. Which choice best matches the sounds?
  • You hear ‘sun’. Which spelling is the best choice?
  • Which spelling best matches ‘fish’ using known sounds?
  • You are unsure how to spell a word. What should you do first?

Mastery evidence

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Supply the choices omitted from the quick prompts: map / mp / pam for map; sun / sn / nus for sun; fish / fih / fsh for fish. The first spelling in each set completely represents the intended taught sound sequence. When unsure, first say and segment the whole word, rather than guess its first letter. For independent spelling, dictate a fresh word without displaying these choices or its answer.
