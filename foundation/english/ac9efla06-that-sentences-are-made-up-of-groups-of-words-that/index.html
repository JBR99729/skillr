FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Hear how groups of words work together AC9EFLA06

Learning intention: I can keep related words together, notice what they add and change a group to change the message.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the words that belong together

In “The little dog rests under the tree,” The little dog names the animal. Rests tells what it does. Under the tree tells where. We can begin with two broad parts, but that is a helpful model rather than a rule that every sentence has exactly two groups. Read the whole sentence whenever a group changes.

Learn the skill

1. Keep a naming group together

Some words work together to name who or what we mean. In The little dog, little describes dog and The belongs with that naming group. Do not split the message into random pieces.

Sentence: “The spotted dog carried a stick.”

Try it · Which whole group names the dog?

The spotted dog. Dog names the animal and spotted adds a description. Carried tells the action; a stick names what was carried.

2. Notice a word’s contribution

A word can tell what happens, what something is like or where it is. Explain its job in this sentence using everyday words. Formal word-class names are not required.

Compare “The bird rests.” with “The bird rests in a tree.”

Try it · What does in a tree add?

It tells where the bird rests. The bird and its resting stay the same. The added group makes the place clearer.

3. Swap a group and hear the change

Keep some words the same while replacing a meaningful group. Check that the new whole sentence makes sense, then say which part of the meaning changed.

A: “My bag is beside the door.” B: “My bag is under the table.”

Try it · What changes, and what stays the same?

The bag stays the same. The place changes from beside the door to under the table. Both whole sentences make sense.

Common mistakes

“I can cut a word group anywhere.” Listen for words that belong together in meaning.

“Every sentence has exactly two groups.” A two-part model is a starting aid. A sentence can add other meaningful groups.

“A word always has the same job.” Read it in its whole sentence and explain what it contributes here.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Keep, swap and say

Arrange “The small bird” and “sang.” Say the whole message. Keep the bird group and swap in “rested in a nest.” Explain what changed. Then try your own sensible group.

Adjust the challenge Support: the adult reads each complete group. Core: explain the changed happening. Extend: keep the happening and change the naming group or place.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. In “The black cat slept,” does black tell what the cat did?

No. Black describes the cat. Slept tells what happened.

2. Can I swap under the table for beside the door without changing the place?

No. Those groups describe different places. Read the new complete message to check the change.

3. Do “The little dog sleeps” and “Sleeps dog the little” give the same clear ordinary statement?

The first is a clear ordinary statement. The second mixes the words into a confusing order. Knowing the individual words is not enough.

FAQ

Is this a test of formal parsing?

No. Children explain how words work together through clear examples and everyday language.

Can pictures help?

Yes. Pair a naming group with a clear picture, then show or explain what the other group adds. Read the whole sentence together.

Key vocabulary
  • word group — words that belong together to add one part of a sentence's meaning
  • sentence — words arranged to communicate a whole idea
  • meaning — the idea the words communicate
  • action — what someone or something does
  • detail — extra information about who, what, where or how

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Connect words by the jobs they do together

Words in a sentence relate to one another: a naming word can connect to an action word, and a describing word can add information to a naming word.

Think: Find the naming word or group. Connect it to the action word or group. Attach a describing word to the person, animal, place or thing it describes.

Try: In “The black cat ate,” which words belong together, and how does each one help the meaning?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: connects cat to ate and black to cat, explaining the naming, action and describing relationships in child-friendly language.

Check: The learner connects cat to ate and black to cat, explaining the naming, action and describing relationships in child-friendly language.

If you are stuck: Use a cat picture as the centre, place black beside it and draw an action arrow from cat to ate.

Common misconceptions
  • A word group can be cut anywhere — Read the sentence aloud and keep together the words that answer the same meaning question, such as who or what happened.
  • Every sentence must have exactly two groups — Explain that the two-part model helps us begin, but a sentence can include more meaningful detail groups.
  • Moving one group never changes the message — Swap or move one group, read both sentences aloud and compare what each one now means.
Explain and apply

Keep related words together

Read “The spotted dog carried a stick.” Which words name the dog? Which word tells its action?

Answer and teaching guidance

The spotted dog names the animal; carried tells the action. Spotted describes dog. Use these everyday explanations without requiring formal grammar labels.

Change a description

Change spotted to muddy in “The spotted dog carried a stick.” What changes? What stays the same?

Answer and teaching guidance

The description of the dog changes. The animal still carries a stick. A describing word helps build the naming group rather than telling the action.

Change a place group

Compare “My bag is beside the door.” and “My bag is under the table.”

Answer and teaching guidance

My bag stays the same. Beside the door changes to under the table, so the location changes. Read each complete sentence to check that its groups work together.

Build, then explain

Make a sentence about a bird. Include what it does and where. Point to the words doing each job.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, The bird rests in a tree. The bird names what it is about; rests tells what happens; in a tree tells where. Support: supply word cards. Extend: change one group and explain the new meaning.

Try these
  1. Human sentence — Students hold word cards and stand close to the words that belong in the who group and action group.

Worked example and reasoning checks

Words work together to make a message

Hear how groups of words tell who or what, and what happens.

Worked example

Compare ‘The little dog sleeps’ with ‘Sleeps dog the little’. Which sounds like a complete message?

  1. Say ‘the little dog’ together. These words tell who the message is about.
  2. Add ‘sleeps’ to tell what the dog does. Read the whole message aloud.
  3. Moving the words randomly makes the message difficult to understand. Word order helps us make meaning.

Watch for this mistake: A row of familiar words does not automatically make a meaningful sentence. Say the complete message and check that the words work together.

Try it, then explain

  1. Use these words to tell a message: bird / sings / The.

    Hint

    Begin with the words naming the animal.

    Answer and explanation

    The bird sings.

  2. Change ‘The dog sleeps’ to tell about a cat. Which word changes?

    Hint

    Keep the action the same.

    Answer and explanation

    Change dog to cat: The cat sleeps.
