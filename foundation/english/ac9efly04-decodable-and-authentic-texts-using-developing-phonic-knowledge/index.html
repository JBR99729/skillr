Read the letters, then check the message AC9EFLY04

WORKED EXAMPLE

Decoding uses all the letters

The supplied microtext reads “Sam sat. / A cat ran.” Read left to right, blend taught sounds and sweep back for the second line. In sat, /s/ /a/ /t/ blend to sat. The word is not sun: the middle and final letters differ. Reread the complete sentence to check meaning after looking through the word.

Learn the skill

1. Track the print and blend taught sounds

Begin at the first word and match one spoken word to each written word. Move back to the start of the next line. Stretch continuous sounds without adding an extra uh; model stop sounds briefly.

Microtext: “Sam sat.” on line one; “A cat ran.” on line two.

Try it · After sat, where does the next line begin?

At A on the left of line two. Blend the taught sounds in cat and ran in order. The capital names/letters and the word A need prior teaching or adult support.

2. Read words you can work through and words you know

A text is decodable for a learner when its sound–letter patterns have been taught. Recognise and verify familiar high-frequency words too. An adult may support untaught parts of an authentic note. Do not substitute a plausible word for the printed one.

Printed target: mat. Compare map. Adult-supported real-life-style note: “Please bring a hat.”

Try it · What tells you that mat is not map?

The final letter t represents /t/, while p represents /p/. Look through every letter. In the note, an adult can support Please and bring if their patterns have not been taught.

3. Pause, repair and reread

If the reading does not match print, grammar or meaning, pause. Look again, blend and reread; ask for help when needed. Context is a check, not permission to guess from the picture or first letter.

Printed sentence: “A fox hid in a den.” The adult deliberately says din for den.

Try it · What should you check before rereading?

Check the middle letter e and its taught short sound, then blend den. Reread the whole sentence. A den is a place an animal can shelter; the meaning helps confirm the decoded word.

Common mistakes

“A picture can replace the letters.” Use all printed letters first. Pictures may help check meaning.

“Stopping means I failed.” Pausing to repair or ask is part of reading.

“Any word that makes sense is the printed word.” A word must also match the actual letters and taught sound patterns.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Point, blend, reread

Use the two-line microtext. Point once for each word, blend an unfamiliar taught word and reread the sentence. Ask an adult to model an untaught pattern. Use the note for supported authentic reading.

Adjust the challenge Starter: blend one CVC word with a model. Core: read and reread the short text. Stretch: identify a deliberate close-word misread and explain the letter evidence.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. Would seeing a cat picture prove that the printed word is cat?

No. Read the actual letters. A cat picture could accompany a different sentence.

2. You blend /m/ /a/ /p/. What word do you read?

Map, with the short vowel sound represented by a. The final /p/ matters.

3. You read “The cat sun” for “The cat sat.” What could you do?

Pause, compare the printed a and t with what you said, blend sat, then reread. Ask for help if the mismatch remains.

FAQ

Must every Foundation child read this same text independently?

No. Match the text to taught phonics and prior word knowledge. Adult support is appropriate for untaught parts.

Can comprehension support be separated from decoding?

Yes. An adult can read richer authentic texts aloud for comprehension while the child practises reading appropriately decodable print.

Key vocabulary
  • decodable — able to be read using letter–sound knowledge already learnt
  • blend — join speech sounds smoothly to say a word
  • context — the words, pictures and ideas around a word
  • grammar — the way words work together in a sentence
  • reread — read again to check or improve understanding
Key concept

I can use letter sounds, familiar words and meaning clues to read a text and notice when it stops making sense.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Navigate print and match voice to words

Fluent early reading begins at the agreed starting place, travels in the correct direction, returns to the next line and matches one spoken word with one written word.

Think: Point to the first word of a two-line sentence. Move once under each written word as it is spoken. At the line end, sweep back to the start of the next line.

Try: Where will your voice begin, and where will your pointer move after the final word on this line?

A strong answer: Point once under each printed word while reading aloud, then sweep back to the first word on the next line without losing the match.

Check: The learner begins correctly, tracks in the text direction, makes a return sweep and maintains one-to-one word matching.

If you are stuck: Use word cards with generous spaces in one line, then add a second line after matching is stable.

E2: Work through an unknown word

Readers use taught letter–sound knowledge to blend an unknown decodable word and recognise known high-frequency words as complete familiar words.

Think: Pause at the printed word mat and look through every letter. Blend the taught sounds from left to right. Read the known word the in the sentence and reread the whole phrase.

Try: Which word will you blend, which word do you already know, and how will you check both?

A strong answer: Look through and blend the unfamiliar printed word, recognise the taught high-frequency word, then reread the complete sentence for meaning.

Check: The learner uses complete letter evidence for the decodable word, recognises the taught high-frequency word and rereads for meaning.

If you are stuck: Reveal the unknown word one letter at a time, keep a familiar word card beside it and model the final whole-sentence check.

E3: Notice and repair a meaning breakdown

A reader monitors understanding and pauses, rereads or asks for support when a word or sentence stops making sense.

Think: Read 'A fox hid in a den' but deliberately say din. Pause and state that the sentence does not make sense. Look again, blend den, reread and ask for help if the mismatch remains.

Try: What told you meaning had broken down, and which repair will you try first?

A strong answer: Pause when the sentence does not make sense, look through the printed word again, reblend it and reread before asking for support if needed.

Check: The learner notices a mismatch, pauses and uses rereading, letter evidence or an appropriate request for support.

If you are stuck: Provide a stop card the learner can raise, then offer the choice: reread, look through the word or ask for a model.

Common misconceptions
  • The picture can replace decoding — Cover the picture briefly and use every printed letter before checking the picture as context.
  • A blended word never needs checking — Reread the sentence and ask whether the word fits its grammar and meaning.
  • Context means guessing from the first letter — Look through the whole word first, then use context only to confirm or repair the reading.
Explain and apply

Blend through the word

Point under mat from left to right and say each taught sound, then blend the whole word.

Answer and teaching guidance

The learner should use all printed letters in order. If they guess from the picture, ask them to return to the letters.

Reread to check meaning

After blending den in A fox hid in a den, reread the sentence. Does the word fit?

Answer and teaching guidance

Den fits because it is a place a fox can hide. Rereading checks both sentence meaning and grammar.

Compare close words

The printed sentence is “The pig sat in the mud.” An adult says mad instead of mud. Which letter shows the mismatch?

Answer and teaching guidance

The printed middle letter is u, not a. Blend mud using the taught sounds, then reread the sentence to check its meaning. Context checks the reading; it does not replace looking through the printed word.

Repair a misread word

If hen is read as hat, what should the reader check?

Answer and teaching guidance

Look through every letter, blend again, reread the sentence and check whether the idea makes sense.
