responding to stories

I can say how a story event or character makes me feel or think and point to the story clue that shaped my response.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can say how a story event or character makes me feel or think and point to the story clue that shaped my response.

Learning intention: I can say how a story event or character makes me feel or think and point to the story clue that shaped my response.

Success criteria

  • I can name an important event and the character involved.
  • I can share my own feeling or thought about that event.
  • I can support my response with a word, action or image clue from the story.
  • I can respond through talk, drawing or beginning writing.
  • I can listen respectfully when another reader has a different supported response.
Key vocabulary
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a story
  • event — something that happens in a story
  • response — what you feel, think, say, draw or write after a text
  • feeling — an emotion such as happy, worried, surprised or relieved
  • thought — an idea or opinion in your mind
  • story clue — a story word, action or picture detail that supports a response
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: respond to a story event with evidence

In a story, Niko loses a red boot in the rain. A neighbour finds it and returns it.

Worked example 2: explain a thought about a character

A character gives the last piece of fruit to a hungry friend.

The reader may speak, draw or begin to write this response. The meaning matters more than the response mode.

Learning activities
  1. Pause and respond — Pause after an important event. Children choose a feeling face, then point to the event that caused that response.
    Feeling faces beside one pictured story event
  2. Character clue detective — Show two thoughts about a character. Children choose the thought supported by the character's action and explain the clue.
    Character action card linked to a thought bubble
  3. Draw, say, explain — Children draw one moment that made them think or feel something, then dictate or say a complete reason.
    Drawing box with a complete response: I felt relieved because the boot came back
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Express a response through drawing or early writing

A drawing, label, mark or beginning sentence can show a personal response to a story, poem or film.

Teacher: Choose one meaningful moment. Draw the character and action clearly. Add or dictate a complete response: I felt relieved because the boot came back.

Ask: Which part of your drawing shows the moment, and what was your response?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: The learner's drawing or early writing represents a recognisable text moment and a connected response.

Look for: The learner's drawing or early writing represents a recognisable text moment and a connected response.

If incorrect: Offer the event picture to trace or point to, then scribe the child's own words beside it.

E2: Discuss characters and events through personal connections

Talking about a character or event becomes clearer when the reader links it to something they know or have experienced.

Teacher: Name the character and event. Share a connected personal experience. Explain how the connection shapes the response.

Ask: Has something like this happened to you, and how does that help you understand the character?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: names both the story event and a relevant personal connection.

Look for: The learner names both the story event and a relevant personal connection.

If incorrect: Use two mats labelled Story and My life so the child places each event in the right place.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Naming the character's feeling instead of the reader's response — Use two speech bubbles labelled Character may feel and I felt or thought, then answer in the correct bubble.
  • Giving a feeling with no story clue — Return to the pictured event and model a complete link, such as: I felt worried when the boot went missing.
  • Believing a response only counts when it is written — Accept drawing, speaking, gesture or beginning writing, then ask the child to explain the link to the story.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • When Niko loses the boot, what do you feel and which event causes that response?
  • What action shows that the fruit-sharing character is caring?
  • How is the reader's feeling different from a character's feeling?
  • Which part of your drawing shows the event you are responding to?

Mastery evidence

  • When Niko loses the boot, what do you feel and which event causes that response?
