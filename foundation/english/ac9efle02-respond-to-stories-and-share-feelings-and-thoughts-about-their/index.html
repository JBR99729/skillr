FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Share what a story makes you think and feel AC9EFLE02

Learning intention: I can notice a story moment, share my response and listen to a different response. There is no one feeling that every reader must have.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One event can lead to different responses

In the supplied story, Niko’s boot goes missing in the rain and a neighbour returns it. One reader says, “I felt worried when the boot was missing.” Another says, “I felt pleased when the neighbour helped.” Each response connects with an actual event. Neither reader has to copy the other’s feeling.

Learn the skill

1. Find the moment you are responding to

First say who is involved and what happens. Then share your thought or feeling. Keeping those parts separate helps a listener understand your response.

Niko’s boot is missing. Later, the neighbour returns it.

Try it · Which moment could connect with feeling relieved?

The boot being returned could bring relief. Other feelings are welcome when the reader explains their connection with a real story moment.

2. Show a response in words or a drawing

You can say, draw, sign or begin to write your response. Point out which part represents the story and which part shows what you think or feel. An adult can write your own words for you.

Another short story: Jo has one pear. Jo gives the pear to a hungry friend.

Try it · What might you think about Jo’s action?

For example, “I think Jo is generous because Jo gives food to the hungry friend.” Generous means willing to share. Other supported thoughts are possible; the action is the clue, not a rule about what Jo always does.

3. Make a connection without changing the story

A story may remind you of an experience. Say what is similar and what is different. You may use an imagined situation or pass on a private memory. Your experience does not prove an unstated story fact.

Story: Niko loses a boot. Possible connection: a pretend missing toy that is later found.

Try it · Could a lost toy be a useful connection even though it is not a boot?

Yes. Losing and finding something can be the connection. Keep the objects distinct: the story has a boot; the imagined example has a toy.

Common mistakes

“The character and I must feel the same.” Your response can differ. Name the event and explain your own response.

“A neat drawing is the goal.” The drawing helps communicate a response; drawing skill is not the literature skill.

“One action proves everything about a character.” Use the action to support a careful thought about this moment.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Story, response, clue

Hear the Niko story. Choose one moment. Draw, say or show a response and identify the clue. Listen to a partner’s different response and repeat the moment they chose.

Adjust the challenge Starter: point to the missing or returned boot moment. Core: share a response with its clue. Stretch: compare two supported responses to the same moment.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. A puppy finds its lost owner. “I felt relieved when they met again.” Does this connect feeling and event?

Yes. It names the reader’s feeling and the reunion that prompted it. A different supported feeling can also fit.

2. A bear loses a hat. Must every reader feel sad?

No. A reader might be worried, curious or feel something else. Ask what in the story prompted the response.

3. A character keeps trying after falling in a pretend climbing story. Can I think they are determined?

Yes. Continuing to try supports that thought. It does not show that real unsafe climbing should be copied.

FAQ

Does a child have to tell a private experience?

No. Use an imagined connection, another text or a simple response to the event.

Does the response need the word because?

No. A spoken explanation, pointing with a clear message or an adult-scribed sentence can show the link.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a story
  • event — something that happens in a story
  • response — what you feel, think, say, draw or write after a text
  • feeling — an emotion such as happy, worried, surprised or relieved
  • thought — an idea or opinion in your mind
  • story clue — a story word, action or picture detail that supports a response
Key concept

I can say what I think or feel about story events and characters.

Learning goal: I can say what I think or feel about story events and characters.

I am ready when I can

  • I can name an important event and the character involved.
  • I can share my own feeling or thought about that event.
  • I can support my response with a word, action or image clue from the story.
  • I can respond through talk, drawing or beginning writing.
  • I can listen respectfully when another reader has a different supported response.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Express a response through drawing or early writing

A drawing, label, mark or beginning sentence can show a personal response to a story, poem or film.

Think: Choose one meaningful moment. Draw the character and action clearly. Add or dictate a complete response: I felt relieved because the boot came back.

Try: Which part of your drawing shows the moment, and what was your response?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: The learner's drawing or early writing represents a recognisable text moment and a connected response.

Check: The learner's drawing or early writing represents a recognisable text moment and a connected response.

If you are stuck: Offer the event picture to trace or point to, then scribe the child's own words beside it.

E2: Discuss characters and events through personal connections

Talking about a character or event becomes clearer when the reader links it to something they know or have experienced.

Think: Name the character and event. Share a connected personal experience. Explain how the connection shapes the response.

Try: Has something like this happened to you, and how does that help you understand the character?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: names both the story event and a relevant personal connection.

Check: The learner names both the story event and a relevant personal connection.

If you are stuck: Use two mats labelled Story and My life so the child places each event in the right place.

Common misconceptions
  • Naming the character's feeling instead of the reader's response — Use two speech bubbles labelled Character may feel and I felt or thought, then answer in the correct bubble.
  • Giving a feeling with no story clue — Return to the pictured event and model a complete link, such as: I felt worried when the boot went missing.
  • Believing a response only counts when it is written — Accept drawing, speaking, gesture or beginning writing, then ask the child to explain the link to the story.
Explain and apply

Name the moment

Choose one event from a story before saying a feeling or thought. For example: The fox finds the gate open.

Answer and teaching guidance

Young learners often jump straight to a feeling. Naming the event first makes the response traceable to the text.

Feeling plus clue

Say: I felt ____ because ____. The because part must use a story clue, not a guess about a different story.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept a range of feelings when the evidence fits. Two readers can respond differently if each uses a relevant clue.

Thought about a character

Finish: I think the character is ____ because they ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The second blank should be an action from the story. This separates a supported character thought from a simple preference.

Respond in more than one way

Draw, act or tell the same response. Make sure the event, feeling or thought, and clue are all included.

Answer and teaching guidance

This makes the task Foundation-friendly while still requiring evidence. The response can be oral if writing load would hide the literature thinking.
