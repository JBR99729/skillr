FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Find meaning in both words and pictures AC9EFLA07

Learning intention: I can point to what the words tell, notice what a picture adds and use both clues. I can do this in a story and in an information text.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The picture can add something the words do not say

The words say “Rae found a box.” The actual drawing shows an open box with three shells. Rae’s name and the finding event come from the words. The visible contents come from the picture. Together they give more information. The name Rae cannot be proved by looking at an unlabelled picture alone.

Learn the skill

1. Find a clue in the words

Listen to the exact sentence. Say what it tells before adding something from the drawing. An image can show appearance, but words may tell a name or an event that is not directly visible.

Story words: “Rae found a box.” Use the worked-example drawing.

Try it · Which clue tells us the character’s name?

The words tell us Rae. An unlabelled drawing alone would not establish that name. The words also state that Rae found the box.

2. Find an added picture clue

Look closely at what is actually drawn. Name a visible detail. Keep a careful guess separate from something directly shown. Use the four labelled scene panels supplied with the earlier checks too.

The worked drawing shows the box open, with shells inside. The sentence does not name the contents.

Try it · What extra detail can you get from the picture?

The box contains three shells. That visible detail is not stated in the sentence. Do not invent where the shells came from or who owns them.

3. Use both in an information text

An information picture can show where parts are. A label names a part, and a sentence can tell a fact we cannot see happening. Link each word to the correct visible part.

Supplied plant diagram: stem above the soil line and roots below it. Sentence: “Roots take in water.”

Try it · What does the diagram show, and what does the sentence add?

The diagram shows where the labelled roots are in this example. The sentence tells that roots take in water. A still drawing does not by itself show the water moving.

Common mistakes

“A picture tells the character’s name.” Find a naming word or label; appearance alone is not enough.

“The picture only repeats the words.” Look for a visible detail that the words leave out.

“Every guess is a visible fact.” Point to the actual clue, and say may or might when making an interpretation.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Cover, reveal and explain

Use the actual box drawing and sentence. First cover the words and say one visible detail. Then cover the drawing and listen to the words. Reveal both and tell what each adds. Try the same routine with the plant diagram.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose between a visible shell and an invented hidden toy. Core: give one word clue and one picture clue. Extend: explain what information would disappear if either were removed.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. Does the picture alone prove that Rae found the box yesterday?

No. The image does not establish the name, the finding event’s date or an unseen earlier event.

2. The words say “The hen watched her babies.” The supplied panel shows three chicks. What does the picture add?

It shows three chicks. The words call them the hen’s babies but do not state the number.

3. In the supplied Sam panel, which colour comes only from the picture?

The raincoat is yellow. The accompanying words say that Sam wore boots, without naming the coat’s colour.

FAQ

Can a written picture description replace the picture for an image-reading task?

No. Use the actual supplied drawing or another available page. Alt text supports access, but should not be reported as a child interpreting an image visually.

What answers should an adult accept?

Accept ideas supported by the shown image and words. Ask the child to identify the clue rather than guessing what is in the adult’s mind.

Key vocabulary
  • image — a picture, photograph, drawing or diagram
  • word clue — meaning communicated by the written or spoken words
  • picture clue — meaning communicated by what an image shows
  • detail — a small piece of information that adds meaning
  • combine — put clues together to make a fuller meaning
Key concept

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Compare meaning from words only and images only

A reader may build different interpretations when only the words or only the images are available because each mode can contribute different evidence.

Think: Use the words only and say what they suggest. Use the image only and say what it suggests. Combine both and explain what became clearer or changed.

Try: The words say, “It was finally here.” The image shows a child beside a birthday cake. What can each part make you think?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: gives a text-based idea, an image-based idea and a combined interpretation that remains open to reasonable alternatives.

Check: The learner gives a text-based idea, an image-based idea and a combined interpretation that remains open to reasonable alternatives.

If you are stuck: Use three mats labelled Words, Image and Together and record one clue on each.

E2: Match written details with image details

Some written details are reflected directly in an accompanying image, while other written details such as a name, thought, time or unseen event may not be visible.

Think: Name one detail from the words. Check whether the image shows that detail. Find one detail supplied by only one mode.

Try: The sentence says, “Lina quietly waited until night.” The picture shows a child sitting beside a tent under stars. Which details match, and which detail is not visible?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: matches waiting or night with image evidence and identifies Lina's name or quietly as information not securely visible in the image.

Check: The learner matches waiting or night with image evidence and identifies Lina's name or quietly as information not securely visible in the image.

If you are stuck: Cover one phrase at a time and ask Can I still know this from the picture?

Response guidance for this packet

E1: The words alone leave it unclear what arrived; the cake and candles may suggest a birthday or celebration. The combined meaning is a supported interpretation, not the only possible event. E2: Stars and the dark sky support night; a seated figure can suggest waiting but does not prove a duration or silence. Lina’s name and quietly come from the words. Common misconceptions
  • The picture tells every part of the message — Ask what the words tell that cannot be seen, such as a character's name, time or thought.
  • The words are enough, so the image can be ignored — Cover the image, notice what information disappears, then restore it and combine both clues.
  • Every image only repeats the words — Find one added detail in the image and explain how it extends the sentence or fact.
Explain and apply

Use a real page

Choose an illustrated story page already available at home or school. Adult: cover the words and ask what the image shows; then read the words.

Answer and teaching guidance

Record one visible detail and one detail stated in words. This activity needs the actual page: a written picture description alone cannot show how a child interprets an image.

Find the evidence

On that story page, name something known from the words, something seen in the picture, and something both tell.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept answers supported by the selected page. Ask the child to point to each clue. A character name may come from words while clothing or position may come from the image; do not assume every page has all three kinds.

Try an information page

Use an existing labelled photograph or diagram from an information book. Read the caption or labels, then ask what the image adds.

Answer and teaching guidance

The image might show appearance, position or parts; words may name a part or give a fact not visible. Check the actual page rather than accepting a guessed fact.

Check a guess

Ask: “Does this picture tell us the character’s name?” How could we check?

Answer and teaching guidance

A picture of a person alone does not establish their name. Look for words or labels that name them. Support: compare two clear clues. Extend: explain how removing either words or image would change understanding.

Try these
  1. Cover and reveal — Show an image without its sentence, then reveal the words. Students name what each part contributes.
    image clue → word clue → combined meaning
  2. Missing clue — Read a sentence while hiding the image. Students predict one useful detail the picture might add.
    words + ? picture detail
  3. Story and information — Compare a story illustration and labelled diagram. Identify how each image helps its text.
    illustration ↔ story • diagram ↔ information

Quick checks

  • The words say, ‘The puppy hid.’ The picture shows the puppy under a bed. What does the picture add?
  • The words say, ‘A storm is coming.’ The image shows dark clouds and bending trees. What do they do together?
  • A picture shows three chicks, but the words say, ‘The hen watched her babies.’ What does the picture add?
  • The words say, ‘Sam wore boots.’ The picture also shows a yellow raincoat. What does only the picture tell?
