FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Listen, take a turn and choose a clear voice AC9EFLY02

Learning intention: I can listen to another person, respond to their message and use a comfortable volume for the listeners.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

An answer can show that you listened

Ari says, “My seed has two leaves.” Mia waits, then asks, “Are the leaves big or small?” Mia’s question connects with the leaves Ari mentioned. A quiet body or eye contact alone would not show that Mia understood. Listening can be shown in several comfortable ways.

Learn the skill

1. Hear the message before adding your idea

Give the speaker time to finish, then recall a detail or ask a connected question. Agree on an accessible turn signal. Movement, AAC and comfortable attention do not make a listener less able to understand.

Ari: “My seed has two leaves.” Possible replies: “How big are the leaves?” / “My lunch is cold.”

Try it · Which reply connects with Ari’s idea?

How big are the leaves? It builds on the stated leaves. The lunch reply changes the topic.

2. Adjust volume for the real listeners

Name who needs to hear you and how far away they are. Speak clearly at a comfortable volume and ask whether they can hear. Outdoors does not always mean shouting, and a bigger audience does not mean straining your voice.

Say “Our plant has grown” to a nearby partner, then to a small group a little farther away.

Try it · What should guide the change?

Whether the intended listeners can hear comfortably. Adjust distance or volume as needed. There is no one loudness level that fits every room or person.

3. Use listening in play and structured tasks

In a pretend shop, respond to the customer’s actual request. In an instruction task, hear the whole short direction before acting. Ask for a repeat if needed. These are different reasons to listen.

Adult says: “Pick up the blue card, then put it beside the book.” Use a safe blue card and a book.

Try it · What happens first and next?

First pick up the blue card. Next put it beside the book. Repeating the direction shows recall; observe the action separately if checking that it was carried out.

Common mistakes

“Still hands prove listening.” Ask for a relevant response or detail; do not require one body position.

“The loudest voice is best.” Choose a comfortable volume that reaches these listeners.

“Any reply continues the topic.” Use a detail from the message when responding.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Speak, listen, respond

Use one pretend-shop exchange and one plant discussion. Partners take turns, repeat a detail and add a connected response. Try the blue-card instruction, with repeat or chunking support when needed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: use one short message and two reply choices. Core: exchange a relevant turn. Stretch: ask for clarification or adjust the message when a listener cannot hear.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. A child looks away but accurately recalls the message. Must we say they were not listening?

No. Eye contact is not compulsory evidence. Consider the actual understanding shown and cultural or access needs.

2. Is shouting always needed for an outdoor partner?

No. Distance, surrounding sound and listener comfort matter. Move closer or adjust gently.

3. If I do not understand an instruction, should I guess silently?

Ask for a repeat or explanation, or use an agreed signal. Then check what to do.

FAQ

Is choosing a reply the same as a live conversation?

No. It is useful preparation. Observe the real exchange separately.

Can AAC count as an interaction turn?

Yes. Provide enough time and an agreed accessible way to listen, contribute and respond.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • interaction — people listening and speaking with one another
  • turn — one person’s time to speak or act
  • listener — a person paying attention to what is being said
  • volume — how loud or quiet a voice is
  • on topic — about the same idea being discussed
Key concept

I can listen when others speak and use a voice volume that suits the situation.

Learning goal: I can listen when others speak and use a voice volume that suits the situation.

I am ready when I can

  • I can listen through a whole short turn.
  • I can wait before responding.
  • I can respond on topic.
  • I can use suitable volume.
  • I can explain why the voice suits the listeners.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Exchange and build ideas in a group

Pair, group and class talk work when learners listen to an idea, contribute a connected idea and share the speaking space.

Think: Give one child a seed picture and ask for one observation. Ask a partner to listen until the observation is complete. Prompt the partner to add a question or idea about that same seed.

Try: What did your partner say, and what connected idea can you add?

A strong answer: Listen until a peer finishes, recall the peer's idea, then add one connected question or detail after waiting for a turn.

Check: The learner recalls a peer's idea and contributes a relevant question, example or detail after waiting for a turn.

If you are stuck: Use a speaker card and a listener card, then offer two pictured responses and ask which one stays on the topic.

E2: Show listening in a respectful way

Appropriate listening means not interrupting and showing attention in a way that is comfortable and culturally appropriate for the speaker and listener.

Think: Explain the short listening purpose. Let the speaker finish without interruption. Ask the listener to show understanding by recalling one exact detail.

Try: Which detail can you repeat to show that you heard the whole message?

A strong answer: Let the speaker finish, show attention in a comfortable and culturally appropriate way, then repeat one exact message detail.

Check: The learner waits, attends in an appropriate way and accurately recalls or responds to one part of the message.

If you are stuck: Shorten the message to one sentence and let the learner point to the matching picture before answering orally.

E3: Adjust volume for place and audience

Voice volume changes with distance, setting and audience size so listeners can hear comfortably without being overwhelmed.

Think: Name the place and intended listeners. Model a clear volume for that distance. Ask listeners whether every word was comfortable to hear and adjust once.

Try: Who needs to hear you here, and how will your voice reach them comfortably?

A strong answer: Name the place and listeners, deliver the same line at a suitable volume, ask whether it was comfortable to hear and adjust once.

Check: The learner selects and adjusts volume according to the named audience and setting while keeping speech clear.

If you are stuck: Move the speaker closer, reduce the audience to one partner and rehearse the sentence before changing the distance again.

E4: Use spoken language in imaginative play

Imaginative play creates informal reasons to ask, explain, negotiate roles and respond in character.

Think: Choose roles in a pretend fruit shop. Model a customer asking for an apple and a shopkeeper responding. Invite children to continue the exchange and solve a simple play problem.

Try: What could your character say next to keep the play going?

A strong answer: Take a pretend-shop role, make or answer a connected request, then adapt the next spoken turn when the play situation changes.

Check: The learner uses understandable spoken language that suits the pretend role and responds to another player's contribution.

If you are stuck: Offer role pictures and a complete oral model such as, 'May I have two apples, please?' or 'We have one apple left.' before fading the prompt.

E5: Listen to and follow instructions

Following an oral instruction requires listening to the whole direction, keeping its order and completing the named action.

Think: Say, 'Pick up the blue card, then place it beside the book.' Ask the learner to repeat the two actions in order. Let the learner act and compare the result with the direction.

Try: What will you do first, and what will you do next?

A strong answer: Listen to the complete two-step direction, repeat what happens first and next, then carry out both actions in order.

Check: The learner repeats or demonstrates both named actions in the stated order without adding a different action.

If you are stuck: Give one instruction at a time with matching object pictures, then reconnect the two steps with first and next.

Texts and materials for these examples

Prepare: a safe blue card and book for the two-step instruction; role cards or AAC choices for speaker/listener. For the seed-picture exchange, use an actual safe seed/plant picture prepared by an adult. If using reply pictures, put the actual choices where both partners can see them. Comfortable attention and movement are allowed.

Common misconceptions
  • Waiting silently means listening — Ask the learner to repeat one idea the speaker shared before responding.
  • The loudest voice is always the best voice — Move closer to the listener and adjust volume until every word is comfortable to hear.
  • Any response counts as a turn — Name the topic first, then make the response connect to that same topic.
Explain and apply

Listen for the whole message

A partner tells how their seed grew. What should you do before speaking?

Answer and teaching guidance

Wait until the speaker finishes, attend in a comfortable way and listen for one detail you can respond to. This protects the speaker's turn.

Match voice to audience

Practise the same sentence for one partner, a small group and the whole class. What changes?

Answer and teaching guidance

The words can stay similar, but the volume changes so the intended audience can hear without shouting.

Stay on topic

A classmate says, I found a smooth shell at the beach. Give one connected reply or question.

Answer and teaching guidance

The response should reuse a detail from the message, such as shell or beach, and add a related comment or question.

Repair an interaction

Sam interrupts and then talks about lunch when the classmate is explaining a pet. What two things should Sam fix?

Answer and teaching guidance

He should wait for his turn and respond to the pet topic. This checks turn-taking and relevance together.

Try these

    Speaker card → wait card → listener response card
  3. Volume match — Say the same greeting to one partner and then to a small group, changing only the volume needed.
    One partner: quiet and clear • small group: clear enough for all
