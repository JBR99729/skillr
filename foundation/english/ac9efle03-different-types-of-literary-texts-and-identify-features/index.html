Key vocabulary
  • literary text — a creative text such as a story, poem or play
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a literary text
  • event — something that happens in a literary text
  • beginning — the part where the text and its first event start
  • ending — the part where the final event finishes the text
  • fairytale — a traditional kind of imaginative story that often has magical events or a lesson
  • poem — a literary text arranged to make meaning through words, lines, sounds or rhythm
Key concept

I can recognise literary texts and find characters, events, beginnings and endings.

Learning goal: I can recognise literary texts and find characters, events, beginnings and endings.

I am ready when I can

  • I can recognise a story, fairytale or story poem as a kind of literary text.
  • I can identify a character by answering who is in the text.
  • I can identify an event by explaining what happens.
  • I can locate the first story event and the final story event.
  • I can use text or picture evidence to explain each feature.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Recognise familiar story beginnings

Story openings can use familiar phrases or introduce a character, place or first event.

Think: Read: Once upon a time, a fox found a red hat. Read: A girl called Amira found a tiny key. Ask what each opening prepares the listener to meet.

Try: What tells your ears that the story is beginning?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies an opening phrase or character introduction as a beginning feature.

Check: The learner identifies an opening phrase or character introduction as a beginning feature.

If you are stuck: Place a Beginning card before each opening and contrast it with an ending sentence.

E2: Explore cultural patterns in an attributed First Nations story

A selected First Nations Australian literary text may organise storytelling through patterns particular to its creator, community, language or mode.

Think: Name the creator and source. Notice an approved repeated image, place, sound or event pattern. Explain how that pattern helps tell this story.

Try: What pattern do you notice in this storyteller's work, and where does it appear again?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: points to a repeated feature in the selected text without presenting it as a rule for all First Nations stories.

Check: The learner points to a repeated feature in the selected text without presenting it as a rule for all First Nations stories.

If you are stuck: Revisit two approved moments side by side and let the child point to what repeats.

E3: Identify how a poem can tell a story

A poem can include a character and ordered events while also using lines, rhythm and sound.

Think: Name who appears in the poem. Place two or three events in order. Mark the first and final event.

Try: Who is in this poem, and what happens first and last?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a character and at least two ordered events in the poem.

Check: The learner identifies a character and at least two ordered events in the poem.

If you are stuck: Turn each line into a simple picture card and sequence the cards.

E4: Notice familiar fairytale features

Fairytales often include recognisable character types, magical events, repeated patterns or a lesson, although not every fairytale uses every feature.

Think: Name the fairytale. Find a character or magical event. Discuss what a character learns, if the text gives a lesson.

Try: Which feature helps you recognise this as a fairytale, and where is the evidence?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies one present fairytale feature and locates its text or image evidence.

Check: The learner identifies one present fairytale feature and locates its text or image evidence.

If you are stuck: Offer two cards, a magical event and a page number, and ask which helps identify the literary type.

Common misconceptions
  • Every text with pictures is a story — Compare an illustrated information page with a literary text and look for characters and connected events.
  • A character is the same as an event — Ask who for the character and what happened for the event, then sort one example of each.
  • The title or cover is automatically the story beginning — Open the text and locate the first story event; explain that the cover helps us enter the text but is not usually an event.
Explain and apply

Sort the literary type

Compare a story, poem and rhyme. Which one has characters and events? Which one uses lines or repeated sound?

Answer and teaching guidance

The learner is identifying features, not memorising labels. A poem can also tell events, so ask what evidence in the selected text supports the choice.

Find who and what happened

On a short story page, point to the character and say one event.

Answer and teaching guidance

Character answers name who or what takes part. Event answers tell what happens. Keep these two jobs separate.

Beginning and ending

Retell the first important event and the final important event. Explain how they work like bookends.

Answer and teaching guidance

The beginning starts the action or situation; the ending shows where the selected text finishes. Use picture cards if needed.

Picture-about check

Look at a literature picture and answer: What is this picture mostly about? Which clue shows that?

Answer and teaching guidance

This closely matches the IXL-style picture-comprehension move while keeping it tied to literary features and evidence.
