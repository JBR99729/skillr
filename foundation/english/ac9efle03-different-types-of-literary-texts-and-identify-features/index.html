literary text types and features

I can name a kind of literary text and identify its characters, events, beginning and ending.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can name a kind of literary text and identify its characters, events, beginning and ending.

Learning intention: I can name a kind of literary text and identify its characters, events, beginning and ending.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise a story, fairytale or story poem as a kind of literary text.
  • I can identify a character by answering who is in the text.
  • I can identify an event by explaining what happens.
  • I can locate the first story event and the final story event.
  • I can use text or picture evidence to explain each feature.
Key vocabulary
  • literary text — a creative text such as a story, poem or play
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a literary text
  • event — something that happens in a literary text
  • beginning — the part where the text and its first event start
  • ending — the part where the final event finishes the text
  • fairytale — a traditional kind of imaginative story that often has magical events or a lesson
  • poem — a literary text arranged to make meaning through words, lines, sounds or rhythm
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: map the features of a short story

A short story begins, “A girl called Mina found a shining shell.” Mina takes the shell home, listens to its ocean sound and returns it to the beach.

Worked example 2: find a story inside a poem

A four-line poem tells of a sleepy bird that wakes, stretches, flies and reaches a tree.

Learning activities

  2. Story bookends — Place a Beginning marker before the first event and an Ending marker after the final event, then retell the middle events.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Recognise familiar story beginnings

Story openings can use familiar phrases or introduce a character, place or first event.

Teacher: Read: Once upon a time, a fox found a red hat. Read: A girl called Amira found a tiny key. Ask what each opening prepares the listener to meet.

Ask: What tells your ears that the story is beginning?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies an opening phrase or character introduction as a beginning feature.

Look for: The learner identifies an opening phrase or character introduction as a beginning feature.

If incorrect: Place a Beginning card before each opening and contrast it with an ending sentence.

E2: Explore cultural patterns in an attributed First Nations story

A selected First Nations Australian literary text may organise storytelling through patterns particular to its creator, community, language or mode.

Teacher: Name the creator and source. Notice an approved repeated image, place, sound or event pattern. Explain how that pattern helps tell this story.

Ask: What pattern do you notice in this storyteller's work, and where does it appear again?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: points to a repeated feature in the selected text without presenting it as a rule for all First Nations stories.

Look for: The learner points to a repeated feature in the selected text without presenting it as a rule for all First Nations stories.

If incorrect: Revisit two approved moments side by side and let the child point to what repeats.

E3: Identify how a poem can tell a story

A poem can include a character and ordered events while also using lines, rhythm and sound.

Teacher: Name who appears in the poem. Place two or three events in order. Mark the first and final event.

Ask: Who is in this poem, and what happens first and last?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a character and at least two ordered events in the poem.

Look for: The learner identifies a character and at least two ordered events in the poem.

If incorrect: Turn each line into a simple picture card and sequence the cards.

E4: Notice familiar fairytale features

Fairytales often include recognisable character types, magical events, repeated patterns or a lesson, although not every fairytale uses every feature.

Teacher: Name the fairytale. Find a character or magical event. Discuss what a character learns, if the text gives a lesson.

Ask: Which feature helps you recognise this as a fairytale, and where is the evidence?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies one present fairytale feature and locates its text or image evidence.

Look for: The learner identifies one present fairytale feature and locates its text or image evidence.

If incorrect: Offer two cards, a magical event and a page number, and ask which helps identify the literary type.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Every text with pictures is a story — Compare an illustrated information page with a literary text and look for characters and connected events.
  • A character is the same as an event — Ask who for the character and what happened for the event, then sort one example of each.
  • The title or cover is automatically the story beginning — Open the text and locate the first story event; explain that the cover helps us enter the text but is not usually an event.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Who is the character in Mina's shell story, and what is one event?
  • Which event begins the sleepy-bird poem and which event ends it?
  • What features would help you recognise a fairytale rather than a sign?
  • How can a poem use characters and events to tell a story?
