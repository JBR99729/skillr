Key vocabulary
  • preference — the choice someone likes more
  • like — to enjoy or feel good about something
  • dislike — to not enjoy something
  • reason — words that explain why you think or choose something
  • because — a word that introduces a reason
Key concept

I can show and say what I like, dislike or prefer.

Learning goal: I can show and say what I like, dislike or prefer.

I am ready when I can

  • I can name a like, dislike or preference.
  • I can use because to give a reason.
  • I can tell a preference from a fact.
  • I can listen to a different preference respectfully.
  • I can use more than one clear preference frame.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Notice preferences in speech and gesture

A person can communicate a feeling or preference through spoken words, tone, face and body movement, and these clues are clearest when considered together.

Think: Listen to the words. Notice the voice, face or gesture. Combine the clues and check by asking the speaker.

Try: Sam says, “I would like the apple,” and points to it with a smile. Which clues show the preference?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

Check: The learner names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

If you are stuck: Replay the message in two parts: hear the words with eyes closed, then view the gesture without words before combining both.

E2: Read feelings in visual and media texts

Creators use visible and audible choices such as facial expression, movement, colour, framing, music and voice to suggest feelings in advertisements and animations.

Think: Name one thing you can see or hear. Say which feeling it may suggest. Explain how that choice guides the viewer.

Try: An animated character jumps, grins and speaks quickly beside a new toy. What feeling is suggested, and what evidence supports your idea?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

Check: The learner names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

If you are stuck: Offer a complete evidence model, such as: I notice a grin and lively movement; this may show excitement.

Common misconceptions
  • A preference is a fact — Place two different valid choices side by side and explain that people can prefer different things.
  • Saying one word gives a clear reason — Recast the response as a complete sentence, such as: I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  • Disagreeing means the other choice is bad — Model both views kindly without judging either person: You prefer running; I prefer skipping.
Explain and apply

Name the choice

Choose between reading and drawing. Start with I prefer and name one choice.

Answer and teaching guidance

A preference needs a clear choice. The learner can answer orally if writing load gets in the way.

Add because

Finish: I prefer drawing because ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The reason should explain why the speaker likes that choice. Because links the preference to its reason.

Separate fact and preference

Compare: The cup is blue. I like the blue cup best. Which one tells a personal choice?

Answer and teaching guidance

The second sentence is a preference because another person may choose differently.

Disagree politely

A friend likes apples best. You prefer pears. What could you say?

Answer and teaching guidance

For example: I prefer pears because they are juicy. This shares a preference without dismissing the friend.

Try these
  1. Choose and tell — Offer two familiar picture choices. Students point, then complete the oral frame I prefer this one because.
    two picture choices → one preference
  2. Add the reason — Students extend the preference with because and one true reason.
    I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  3. Kind disagreement — Partners share different choices and reply with a complete comparison, such as: You like running; I prefer skipping.
    listen → name both choices → respond
