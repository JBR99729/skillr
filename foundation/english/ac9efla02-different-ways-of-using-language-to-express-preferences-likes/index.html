Sharing Likes, Dislikes and Preferences

I can tell what I like, do not like or prefer, and I can give a simple reason. A preference is a personal choice, not a fact everyone must share. We can disagree and still respond kindly.

What students learn

I can tell what I like, do not like or prefer, and I can give a simple reason. A preference is a personal choice, not a fact everyone must share. We can disagree and still respond kindly.

Learning intention: I can tell what I like, do not like or prefer, and I can give a simple reason. A preference is a personal choice, not a fact everyone must share. We can disagree and still respond kindly.

Success criteria

  • I can name a like, dislike or preference.
  • I can use because to give a reason.
  • I can tell a preference from a fact.
  • I can listen to a different preference respectfully.
  • I can use more than one clear preference frame.
Key vocabulary
  • preference — the choice someone likes more
  • like — to enjoy or feel good about something
  • dislike — to not enjoy something
  • reason — words that explain why you think or choose something
  • because — a word that introduces a reason

Learning activities
  1. Choose and tell — Offer two familiar picture choices. Students point, then complete the oral frame I prefer this one because.
    two picture choices → one preference
  2. Add the reason — Students extend the preference with because and one true reason.
    I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  3. Kind disagreement — Partners share different choices and reply with a complete comparison, such as: You like running; I prefer skipping.
    listen → name both choices → respond
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Notice preferences in speech and gesture

A person can communicate a feeling or preference through spoken words, tone, face and body movement, and these clues are clearest when considered together.

Teacher: Listen to the words. Notice the voice, face or gesture. Combine the clues and check by asking the speaker.

Ask: Sam says, “I would like the apple,” and points to it with a smile. Which clues show the preference?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

Look for: The learner names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

If incorrect: Replay the message in two parts: hear the words with eyes closed, then view the gesture without words before combining both.

E2: Read feelings in visual and media texts

Creators use visible and audible choices such as facial expression, movement, colour, framing, music and voice to suggest feelings in advertisements and animations.

Teacher: Name one thing you can see or hear. Say which feeling it may suggest. Explain how that choice guides the viewer.

Ask: An animated character jumps, grins and speaks quickly beside a new toy. What feeling is suggested, and what evidence supports your idea?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

Look for: The learner names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

If incorrect: Offer a complete evidence model, such as: I notice a grin and lively movement; this may show excitement.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • A preference is a fact — Place two different valid choices side by side and explain that people can prefer different things.
  • Saying one word gives a clear reason — Recast the response as a complete sentence, such as: I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  • Disagreeing means the other choice is bad — Model both views kindly without judging either person: You prefer running; I prefer skipping.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Choose drawing or building and state your preference with because.
  • Which part of ‘I prefer mango because it is sweet’ gives the reason?
  • Two classmates prefer different games. Can both preferences be valid? Why?
  • Turn ‘Blue!’ into a clear preference sentence with a reason.
