FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Show and share what you like AC9EFLA02

Learning intention: I can tell or show a like, a dislike or a choice. Another person may choose something different. Both of us can be heard.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two people can choose differently

The picture shows an apple and a pear. Ari says, “I like the apple.” Mia says, “I prefer the pear.” The words tell us each person’s choice. We can also point to a chosen picture after agreeing what the pointing means. A reason can add information, but a clear preference does not need a because sentence.

Learn the skill

1. Listen for whose choice it is

Name the speaker before naming the preference. Notice not: liking drawing is different from not liking drawing. We are finding the message, not deciding which activity everyone should like.

The puppet says, “I like puzzles. I do not like painting today.”

Try it · Which activity does this puppet say it likes?

Puzzles. Painting is the activity it says it does not like today. This does not tell us every other person’s choice.

2. Make your own choice clear

You may speak, sign, point or use AAC. Choose freely; you do not have to copy an adult. You can choose neither. If you want to, add a reason that fits your choice.

Pretend choices: drawing, building or neither. Frame: “I prefer ___.” Optional: “because ___.”

Try it · What would you choose, and how could you show it?

Any freely chosen clear preference is valid. An adult can check the intended choice: “You chose building; is that right?” A reason is optional and has no single key.

3. Listen to a different preference

A different choice does not make someone wrong. Repeat the other person’s message accurately, then share yours. A face or gesture alone may not tell a person’s exact private feeling; ask if you are unsure.

Ari: “I prefer apples.” Mia: “I prefer pears.”

Try it · How could Mia show that both choices were heard?

For example: “You prefer apples. I prefer pears.” Equivalent spoken, signed or AAC messages work. Neither fruit is the correct choice for everyone.

Common mistakes

“Everyone must like my choice.” People can prefer different things.

“A grin proves a person’s exact feeling.” Use the person’s message and context, and check what they mean.

“I need a long reason to have a preference.” A clear choice is enough. A reason is an optional next step.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Choose, show and listen

Use the two fruit pictures, then a new pair of familiar activities. Each person freely chooses one, both or neither. Show the choice in an agreed way. Repeat a partner’s choice before sharing your own.

Adjust the challenge Support: offer two clear pictures and check the intended message. Core: exchange two preferences. Extend: add an optional reason or describe how the preferences differ.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. The puppet says, “I do not like running today.” Does it say it likes running?

No. Do not changes the message. It also does not tell us every activity the puppet likes.

2. Mia chooses neither drawing nor building. Is that automatically incorrect?

No. Neither is a valid personal choice if Mia communicates it clearly.

3. A cartoon jumps and says “Again!” What might it suggest?

It may suggest that the character wants the activity repeated. The supplied words and movement support that interpretation; do not treat one real person’s movement as proof of a hidden feeling.

FAQ

Must a child say because?

No. Model reasons as an extension, while accepting clear preferences without a reason.

Is pointing enough?

It can be, when the available choices and the meaning of the pointing are clear. Check the child’s intended message.

Key vocabulary
  • preference — the choice someone likes more
  • like — to enjoy or feel good about something
  • dislike — to not enjoy something
  • reason — words that explain why you think or choose something
  • because — a word that introduces a reason

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Notice preferences in speech and gesture

A person can communicate a feeling or preference through spoken words, tone, face and body movement, and these clues are clearest when considered together.

Think: Listen to the words. Notice the voice, face or gesture. Combine the clues and check by asking the speaker.

Try: Sam says, “I would like the apple,” and points to it with a smile. Which clues show the preference?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

Check: The learner names a speech clue and a gesture or expression clue, then states the likely preference without treating the body clue as certain proof.

If you are stuck: Replay the message in two parts: hear the words with eyes closed, then view the gesture without words before combining both.

E2: Read feelings in visual and media texts

Creators use visible and audible choices such as facial expression, movement, colour, framing, music and voice to suggest feelings in advertisements and animations.

Think: Name one thing you can see or hear. Say which feeling it may suggest. Explain how that choice guides the viewer.

Try: An animated character jumps, grins and speaks quickly beside a new toy. What feeling is suggested, and what evidence supports your idea?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

Check: The learner names a plausible feeling and supports it with at least one observable media clue.

If you are stuck: Offer a complete evidence model, such as: I notice a grin and lively movement; this may show excitement.

Common misconceptions
  • A preference is a fact — Place two different valid choices side by side and explain that people can prefer different things.
  • Saying one word gives a clear reason — Recast the response as a complete sentence, such as: I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  • Disagreeing means the other choice is bad — Model both views kindly without judging either person: You prefer running; I prefer skipping.
Explain and apply

Name the choice

Choose between reading and drawing. Start with I prefer and name one choice.

Answer and teaching guidance

A preference needs a clear choice. The learner can answer orally if writing load gets in the way.

Add because

Finish: I prefer drawing because ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The reason should explain why the speaker likes that choice. Because links the preference to its reason.

Separate fact and preference

Compare: The cup is blue. I like the blue cup best. Which one tells a personal choice?

Answer and teaching guidance

The second sentence is a preference because another person may choose differently.

Disagree politely

A friend likes apples best. You prefer pears. What could you say?

Answer and teaching guidance

For example: I prefer pears because they are juicy. This shares a preference without dismissing the friend.

Try these
  1. Choose and tell — Offer two familiar picture choices. Students point, then complete the oral frame I prefer this one because.
    two picture choices → one preference
  2. Add the reason — Students extend the preference with because and one true reason.
    I prefer drawing because I like making pictures.
  3. Kind disagreement — Partners share different choices and reply with a complete comparison, such as: You like running; I prefer skipping.
    listen → name both choices → respond

Four supplied preference checks

  1. Which message tells a like for drawing: “I like drawing” or “The paper is white”?
  2. Mia likes apples more than pears. Show or say that preference.
  3. Choose a clear dislike: “I do not like this game today” or “You are bad for liking it”.
  4. In a pretend scene, a child smiles, claps and says “Again!” What might the message suggest?
