FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Make a sentence that shares a whole idea AC9EFLA05

Learning intention: I can hear whether words give a whole message, add missing meaning and check what changes when words move.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A short sentence can give a whole idea

“The small pup” tells us which animal, but leaves us waiting for something about it. “The pup naps.” tells a whole event. “The bag is red.” tells a whole state rather than an action. A full stop cannot supply missing meaning. First make a clear message, then notice its written beginning and end.

Learn the skill

1. Hear the whole intended idea

Listen to all the words. In these telling sentences, ask who or what the message is about and what is said about it. Do not decide by length. A question or a command can also be a sentence.

Compare “Rain falls.” with “The very tall green tree”.

Try it · Which gives a whole telling idea on its own?

Rain falls. The longer group names a tree but does not finish what is being said about it. Length is not the test.

2. Build a complete message

Keep a useful beginning, then add the meaning it needs. Say the whole message aloud and ask whether a listener can understand your intended idea. There can be several good completions.

Beginning: “Our bus”. Possible endings: “stopped.” / “is yellow.”

Try it · Make two whole sentences using the beginning.

Our bus stopped. Our bus is yellow. The first tells what happened; the second tells what the bus is like. Accept another sensible complete message.

3. Move words and check meaning

The same words can make different messages when their order changes. Imagine or act out the stated event and check who is doing what.

A: “The cat follows the dog.” B: “The dog follows the cat.”

Try it · Who follows in A, and who follows in B?

In A the cat follows the dog. In B the dog follows the cat. Both are complete, but swapping the animals changes who follows whom. Following does not necessarily mean chasing.

Common mistakes

“Long means complete.” A short sentence can give a whole idea; a long group can still be unfinished.

“A full stop fixes missing meaning.” Add the needed message first.

“Only action messages are sentences.” A sentence can also tell a state, ask a question or give a command.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build it and change it

Say a whole message beginning “The little bird”. Ask a partner what it tells. Change one word or group to make a different clear message. Compare the meanings.

Adjust the challenge Support: offer “sings.” and “is quiet.” as endings. Core: make an original complete message. Extend: swap two participants and explain who now does the action.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. Does “The green frog.” become complete just because it has a full stop?

As a stand-alone telling sentence here, no. It names the frog but leaves the intended message unfinished. It could be an answer in a conversation, which is a different context.

2. Is “Sit.” always an incomplete message because it does not name a person?

No. It can be a complete command to the listener. The examples above mainly compare telling sentences.

3. Are “The dog follows the cat” and “The cat follows the dog” the same event?

No. The follower changes when the participant order changes.

FAQ

Should an adult insist on grammar names?

No. At Foundation, model whole messages and discuss meaning with everyday language.

What if handwriting gets in the way?

Let the child say, point to, sign or arrange the sentence. An adult can scribe the intended message.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Change word order and change the meaning

Word order matters because rearranging the same words can change who does an action, who receives it or whether the sentence makes sense.

Think: Read or hear the first sentence in order. Act out or picture its meaning. Reorder the words, read again and compare what changed.

Try: How are “The dog chased the boy” and “The boy chased the dog” different?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: explains that the first named participant performs the chasing in each sentence and that changing order changes the event.

Check: The learner explains that the first named participant performs the chasing in each sentence and that changing order changes the event.

If you are stuck: Give each participant a coloured card and move the cards physically as the sentence order changes.

E2: Tell a sentence fragment from a complete sentence

A sentence communicates a complete intended idea, while a fragment such as after school gives only one piece and leaves the listener waiting for more.

Think: Listen to all the words. Ask whether they communicate the whole intended idea. Add the missing who, what happens or other needed meaning.

Try: Do the words “after school” tell a whole idea? How can you complete them?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies the fragment and expands it into a meaningful sentence such as “Dad will pick me up after school.”

Check: The learner identifies the fragment and expands it into a meaningful sentence such as “Dad will pick me up after school.”

If you are stuck: Use two oral frames: Who will do something after school? and What will happen after school?

Common misconceptions
  • Any group of words is a sentence — Say the words aloud and ask what whole idea they communicate; add or rearrange words if the thought is unclear.
  • A long group is more complete than a short group — Compare a short complete sentence, such as Birds fly, with a longer unfinished group and check meaning rather than length.
  • An ending mark can fix words that do not make sense — Repair the word meaning and order first, then add the sentence boundary marks.
Explain and apply

Meaning comes first

Adult: read “The wet dog” and “The wet dog shook.” Which tells a whole idea?

Answer and teaching guidance

The second tells what the dog did. The first names the dog but leaves the intended idea unfinished. Adding a full stop alone does not supply the missing meaning.

Same words, different event

Compare “The girl follows the duck.” and “The duck follows the girl.” Act out each sentence.

Answer and teaching guidance

Both are complete sentences, but changing the word order changes who follows whom. Check meaning by pointing to the one doing the following.

Short can be complete

Compare “Rain falls.” with “The very tall green tree”. Which is complete?

Answer and teaching guidance

Rain falls is complete even though it is shorter. Sentence length does not decide completeness; the longer group still needs something said about the tree.

Make and check

Finish “Our bus” with a whole idea. Ask a partner what your sentence tells.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, Our bus stopped at school. Accept another sensible completion. Support: offer two possible endings. Extend: change the event while keeping Our bus.

Four complete-message choices

  1. Choose the whole telling sentence: “The ant walks.” / “The tiny ant”.
  2. Choose the unfinished stand-alone group: “After school” / “Dad arrived after school.”
  3. Choose the clear ordinary telling sentence: “The cup is full.” / “Full cup the is.”
  4. Which gives a whole telling idea: “Birds fly.” / “The very large brown bird”?
Response guidance for this packet

1 The ant walks. 2 After school is unfinished as a stand-alone telling sentence here; a short response can work differently in conversation. 3 The cup is full. 4 Birds fly. Meaning, not length or punctuation alone, decides these choices.
