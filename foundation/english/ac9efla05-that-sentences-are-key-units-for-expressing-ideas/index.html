A Sentence Shares One Idea

I can recognise a sentence as a group of words that communicates one whole idea. The words need to work together so a listener or reader can understand the thought. In writing, a capital and an ending mark help show the sentence boundary.

What students learn

I can recognise a sentence as a group of words that communicates one whole idea. The words need to work together so a listener or reader can understand the thought. In writing, a capital and an ending mark help show the sentence boundary.

Learning intention: I can recognise a sentence as a group of words that communicates one whole idea. The words need to work together so a listener or reader can understand the thought. In writing, a capital and an ending mark help show the sentence boundary.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise a sentence that communicates a whole idea.
  • I can tell a complete idea from an unfinished group.
  • I can complete an oral sentence meaningfully.
  • I can check whether word order makes sense.
  • I can show a written sentence boundary.
Key vocabulary
  • sentence — words arranged to communicate a whole idea
  • idea — a thought or message someone can understand
  • sense — meaning that can be understood
  • boundary — where a sentence begins or ends
  • complete — having all the words needed for the intended idea

Learning activities
  1. Sentence or not yet? — Say one complete sentence and one unfinished group. Students signal whole idea or not yet.
    whole idea ✓ • not yet
  2. Finish the thought — Give an unfinished beginning such as The tiny ant and invite students to complete the idea orally.
    The tiny ant (not complete) → add an action or state
  3. Boundary walk — Place word cards on the floor. Students stand at the beginning and ending of the complete sentence.
    beginning capital; words with meaning; ending mark
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Change word order and change the meaning

Word order matters because rearranging the same words can change who does an action, who receives it or whether the sentence makes sense.

Teacher: Read or hear the first sentence in order. Act out or picture its meaning. Reorder the words, read again and compare what changed.

Ask: How are “The dog chased the boy” and “The boy chased the dog” different?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: explains that the first named participant performs the chasing in each sentence and that changing order changes the event.

Look for: The learner explains that the first named participant performs the chasing in each sentence and that changing order changes the event.

If incorrect: Give each participant a coloured card and move the cards physically as the sentence order changes.

E2: Tell a sentence fragment from a complete sentence

A sentence communicates a complete intended idea, while a fragment such as after school gives only one piece and leaves the listener waiting for more.

Teacher: Listen to all the words. Ask whether they communicate the whole intended idea. Add the missing who, what happens or other needed meaning.

Ask: Do the words “after school” tell a whole idea? How can you complete them?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies the fragment and expands it into a meaningful sentence such as “Dad will pick me up after school.”

Look for: The learner identifies the fragment and expands it into a meaningful sentence such as “Dad will pick me up after school.”

If incorrect: Use two oral frames: Who will do something after school? and What will happen after school?

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Any group of words is a sentence — Say the words aloud and ask what whole idea they communicate; add or rearrange words if the thought is unclear.
  • A long group is more complete than a short group — Compare a short complete sentence, such as Birds fly, with a longer unfinished group and check meaning rather than length.
  • An ending mark can fix words that do not make sense — Repair the word meaning and order first, then add the sentence boundary marks.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Do the words ‘The sleepy koala’ communicate a whole idea yet? Explain.
  • Complete the words ‘Our class’ so the listener understands one idea.
  • Why is ‘Birds fly.’ a sentence even though it is short?
  • Repair ‘blue under runs’ so it communicates a clear idea.
