FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Share an idea from a story, poem or picture AC9EFLE01

Learning intention: I can use a clue from a text and connect it with something I know. My experience can be similar or different. Another reader may have a different thoughtful response.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

Use a text clue and explain your connection

Short readings and an actual illustration

Reading A · a story by Helen Milroy

Wombat and Mother Earth, from Wombat, Mudlark and Other Stories, by Helen Milroy, a Palyku author. In this selected scene, Mother Earth is lonely. Wombat offers help and shares stories with her. This is a brief scene note, not the whole story.

“let me see if I can help you”

That is Wombat’s offer in the publisher’s extract. What idea does it give you about his response? Connect it with helping or receiving help if you wish.

Read the publisher’s classroom extract. The publisher places the source in its ages six-to-nine section. Here an adult supports a short benign scene for Foundation. This named work is not a rule about all First Nations people or stories.

Reading B · a poem by C. J. Dennis

Opening of The Ant Explorer, in A Book for Kids (1921), by Australian poet C. J. Dennis:

Once a little sugar ant made up his mind to roam—
To fare away far away, far away from home.

Roam means travel or wander. How does this opening make you think about going somewhere? You can use an imagined safe outing rather than a private memory. This is the opening only.

Reading C · words and a picture by Beatrix Potter

From the opening of The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), written and illustrated by Beatrix Potter, a British author and illustrator:

They lived with their Mother in a sand-bank, underneath the root of a very big fir-tree.

Point to a visible family or place clue. What does it remind you of, or how is it different from a familiar home? Do not invent a detail that neither the words nor picture supports.

Learn the skill

1. Share an idea using a text clue

After an adult reads the supplied Milroy scene note and quotation, say what you notice. Keep your response connected to the named story. A personal idea can be different from a partner’s if both use a relevant clue.

Use Reading A in the worked example: Helen Milroy (Palyku), Wombat and Mother Earth, offered by the publisher as a classroom extract.

Try it · What might you say about Wombat’s response?

For example: “I think Wombat is trying to help.” Point to the supplied offer of help as the clue. Other supported responses are possible. This is a response to Milroy’s story, not a claim about every First Nations story.

2. Connect an experience without judging it

Use the short poem opening in Reading B. The adult explains roam as travel or wander. Say what the character does, then connect it with a familiar experience. Similar in one way does not mean identical in every way.

Reading B: the ant decides to go away from home. Think of a short outing, or use an imagined walk with a trusted adult.

Try it · How could your example be similar and different?

For example: “Both involve leaving home. My walk is with an adult, while this poem begins with the ant deciding to roam.” Accept another fitting connection; do not require a real personal journey or assume the whole poem’s events from its opening.

3. Compare responses to words and a picture

Read the short home sentence and view Potter’s actual opening illustration in Reading C. Point to a visible detail, then say what it makes you think about. Pictures and words can invite different supported connections.

The illustration shows the rabbit family beside tree roots. The supplied sentence describes their home.

Try it · Could two readers make different connections to the same home clue?

Yes. One might think of living with family; another might notice that a home under tree roots differs from their own. Accept a specific clue and a relevant connection. Do not decide that one kind of home is normal or better.

Common mistakes

“We must all give the same personal answer.” Different responses can be thoughtful when connected to the text.

“Different means worse.” Name the difference respectfully, without ranking people’s lives.

“One story tells us about every culture.” Name the creator and say in this story. Do not turn one text into a rule about a group.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Clue, connection and listening

An adult reads one short selection from the packet. Point to or repeat one clue. Say or show a connection, using a personal or imagined example. Listen to a partner’s different connection and identify its text clue.

Adjust the challenge Support: offer two details from one reading. Core: link one detail with one experience. Extend: compare a story, poem and picture while keeping each creator and clue clear.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. Can two readers disagree about which experience feels familiar?

Yes. Their experiences may differ. Check that each response uses a real clue from the selected text.

2. Does a rabbit home in one story tell us where all people from the author’s country live?

No. The selected text is about a fictional rabbit family. Do not generalise it to real people or a whole country.

3. Must a child reveal a private family experience to answer?

No. They may use a familiar imagined example, another known story or choose not to share. Keep the comparison respectful.

FAQ

Do the brief scene notes replace reading literature?

No. They make the discussion givens clear. Use the attributed selections and the linked lawful source, and extend with suitable books by a range of First Nations Australian, other Australian and world authors and illustrators.

What does an adult assess?

Notice a text clue, a relevant connection and respectful listening. Do not mark a personal life, feeling or preference as the one correct answer, or claim independent reading from an adult-read task.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • literature — stories, poems and other creative texts that invite us to imagine and respond
  • experience — something that happens to a person or that a person does
  • connection — a link between something in a text and something you know or have experienced
  • similar — alike in at least one clear way
  • different — not the same in at least one clear way
  • culture — shared ways of living, knowing, creating and belonging within a group
  • text clue — a word, event or image detail that supports an idea about a text
Key concept

I can share an idea about a story, poem or picture and connect it to my own experience.

Learning goal: I can share an idea about a story, poem or picture and connect it to my own experience.

I am ready when I can

  • I can name one experience shown in a story, poem or literature image.
  • I can connect a text experience with one experience from my own life.
  • I can say whether the two experiences are similar or different and explain why.
  • I can use an exact text clue when I share an idea.
  • I can talk about cultural details as evidence from this text rather than as a rule about everyone.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Recognise a familiar social or cultural experience

A literary text can show routines, places and relationships that feel familiar to a reader.

Think: Read an authentic text selected for the class. Point to one event, object or relationship. Model a complete connection: This feels familiar to me because I also greet my grandparent.

Try: What detail feels familiar to you, and what in your life does it remind you of?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: names a specific text detail and a relevant personal experience.

Check: The learner names a specific text detail and a relevant personal experience.

If you are stuck: Offer two pictured experiences and let the child point before saying a complete connection.

E2: Compare an experience from a world story

A story from another place can show an experience that is similar to or different from the reader's life.

Think: Notice where and how the event happens. Recall the reader's own version of that event. State similar or different and give a reason.

Try: The character travels through snow to school; how is your trip to school similar or different?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: compares the story journey with their own journey using one accurate reason.

Check: The learner compares the story journey with their own journey using one accurate reason.

If you are stuck: Use a two-column picture chart labelled In the story and In my life.

E3: Respond to an attributed First Nations Australian story

Children can listen to, view and respond to a story by a First Nations Australian storyteller in its named source and context.

Think: Introduce the creator and source accurately. Listen or view without copying restricted or sacred material. Share one idea using an exact story or image clue.

Try: What did you notice in this storyteller's work, and which part made you think that?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: shares a response grounded in the selected text and refers respectfully to its named creator or source.

Check: The learner shares a response grounded in the selected text and refers respectfully to its named creator or source.

If you are stuck: Replay one approved image and model a complete response, such as: I noticed the journey image; it made me think about travelling with family.

E4: Compare Australian family life across settings

Australian stories can portray family life in suburban, regional, remote and other settings.

Think: Name the setting shown in each text. Find one family event in each setting. Compare the experiences using evidence.

Try: What changes because of the setting, and what family experience stays similar?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a setting clue and makes a supported comparison between family experiences.

Check: The learner identifies a setting clue and makes a supported comparison between family experiences.

If you are stuck: Place one landscape clue and one family-action clue under each story image.

E5: Notice cultural details revealed in a story

Characters and events may reveal food, dress, celebrations, languages and daily routines within the particular story.

Think: Locate a visible or stated detail. Explain what the character does with it. Frame the observation as evidence from this story.

Try: What feature of daily life do you notice, and how does the story show it?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a cultural feature and links it to a character or event without overgeneralising.

Check: The learner identifies a cultural feature and links it to a character or event without overgeneralising.

If you are stuck: Model a complete sentence: In this story, the character shares a meal during a celebration.

Two complete original family scenes for E4

Story A, original SkillrHub microfiction: In a town, Lee and a caregiver walk along their street to post a card to a relative. They talk about what to draw next time.

Story B, original SkillrHub microfiction: On a farm, Jo and a caregiver travel along a long driveway to collect a relative’s letter from the roadside box. They read it together at home.

These invented scenes illustrate different settings; they are not First Nations stories or a description of every town or farm family. Extend with actual Australian picture books selected and named by an adult.

Response guidance for this packet

Both involve a caregiver and child connecting with a relative through mail. One scene is a town street and posting; the other is a farm driveway and collecting. Name only the supported differences. No precise journey distance or travel vehicle is supplied.

Common misconceptions
  • There is only one correct personal response — Place two different supported responses beside the same text clue and show that both can be thoughtful.
  • Similar means every detail is identical — Circle one shared feature and say that experiences can be similar in one way and different in another.
  • One story feature describes every person in a cultural group — Use the words in this text, point to exact evidence and avoid claims about all families or communities.
Explain and apply

Notice one experience

After hearing a story or poem, name one experience in the text: travelling, sharing food, playing a game, caring for Country or spending time with family.

Answer and teaching guidance

Keep the first answer close to the text. The learner should point to words or images before adding a personal connection. Accept oral, drawn or acted responses.

Connect without judging

Say whether the text experience is similar to or different from your own life. Use respectful words such as In this story or In my family.

Answer and teaching guidance

The value is in the comparison, not in deciding which experience is better. Encourage neutral wording when the text comes from a culture or community different from the learner's own.

Use a literature image

Look at an illustration by a named author or illustrator. What idea can you share from one visible clue?

Answer and teaching guidance

A valid idea must be supported by an observable detail such as a place, object, person, action, colour or repeated image. This keeps image talk evidence based.

Build a two-part response

Complete: The text shows ____. That is similar to or different from my experience because ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

Support: offer two event cards from the text. Extend: include both one similarity and one difference while staying respectful and specific.

Try these

Complete situations for the four earlier checks

  1. Original short situation: “Rae felt nervous on the first school day.” Which response makes a connection: “I felt nervous on a first day too” or “The word has four letters”?
  2. Original short situation: “The story family eats outside.” Your chosen example has people eating inside. Compare the places respectfully.
  3. After the supplied Ant Explorer opening, choose a poem response: “The repeated away makes me think of going far” or “This proves every ant has the same life”.
  4. Original short situation: “The child visits a grandparent.” Give a similar experience if you wish, or use an imagined example.
Response guidance for this packet

1 The first connects the named experience; a child need not claim it happened personally. 2 For example, both groups eat, but their places differ. 3 The first uses the poem’s language; the second overgeneralises. 4 Accept a relevant visit connection with a clue, including an imagined or known-story example.
