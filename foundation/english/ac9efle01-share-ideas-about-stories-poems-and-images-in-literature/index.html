sharing ideas about literature

I can share an idea about a literary text and compare one text experience with my own experience.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can share an idea about a literary text and compare one text experience with my own experience.

Learning intention: I can share an idea about a literary text and compare one text experience with my own experience.

Success criteria

  • I can name one experience shown in a story, poem or literature image.
  • I can connect a text experience with one experience from my own life.
  • I can say whether the two experiences are similar or different and explain why.
  • I can use an exact text clue when I share an idea.
  • I can talk about cultural details as evidence from this text rather than as a rule about everyone.
Key vocabulary
  • literature — stories, poems and other creative texts that invite us to imagine and respond
  • experience — something that happens to a person or that a person does
  • connection — a link between something in a text and something you know or have experienced
  • similar — alike in at least one clear way
  • different — not the same in at least one clear way
  • culture — shared ways of living, knowing, creating and belonging within a group
  • text clue — a word, event or image detail that supports an idea about a text
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: make a respectful text-to-self connection

A picture story shows a child cooking flatbread with an aunt before a family picnic.

Think aloud: I name what happens in the text. I name one experience from my life. I compare only those two experiences and explain the clue.

Worked example 2: explain a difference without judging it

In another story, the character travels a long way to collect the family mail. The reader walks to a letterbox beside their home.

Step by step: Notice the event, recall your own experience, choose similar or different, then explain why.

Learning activities
  1. Same or different corners — Read one story experience aloud. Children move or point to Similar or Different, then name the text event and their connected life experience in complete phrases.
    Two clearly labelled response places: Similar and Different
  2. Clue and connection — Show a text image and three possible connections. Children choose the response that uses an actual image or event clue.
    Text clue card joined by an arrow to a personal experience card
  3. Many lives, many responses — Invite two children to share different supported connections to the same event. Model listening without deciding that one life is the normal one.
    One text event in the centre with two different, equally respected response bubbles
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Recognise a familiar social or cultural experience

A literary text can show routines, places and relationships that feel familiar to a reader.

Teacher: Read an authentic text selected for the class. Point to one event, object or relationship. Model a complete connection: This feels familiar to me because I also greet my grandparent.

Ask: What detail feels familiar to you, and what in your life does it remind you of?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: names a specific text detail and a relevant personal experience.

Look for: The learner names a specific text detail and a relevant personal experience.

If incorrect: Offer two pictured experiences and let the child point before saying a complete connection.

E2: Compare an experience from a world story

A story from another place can show an experience that is similar to or different from the reader's life.

Teacher: Notice where and how the event happens. Recall the reader's own version of that event. State similar or different and give a reason.

Ask: The character travels through snow to school; how is your trip to school similar or different?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: compares the story journey with their own journey using one accurate reason.

Look for: The learner compares the story journey with their own journey using one accurate reason.

If incorrect: Use a two-column picture chart labelled In the story and In my life.

E3: Respond to an attributed First Nations Australian story

Children can listen to, view and respond to a story by a First Nations Australian storyteller in its named source and context.

Teacher: Introduce the creator and source accurately. Listen or view without copying restricted or sacred material. Share one idea using an exact story or image clue.

Ask: What did you notice in this storyteller's work, and which part made you think that?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: shares a response grounded in the selected text and refers respectfully to its named creator or source.

Look for: The learner shares a response grounded in the selected text and refers respectfully to its named creator or source.

If incorrect: Replay one approved image and model a complete response, such as: I noticed the journey image; it made me think about travelling with family.

E4: Compare Australian family life across settings

Australian stories can portray family life in suburban, regional, remote and other settings.

Teacher: Name the setting shown in each text. Find one family event in each setting. Compare the experiences using evidence.

Ask: What changes because of the setting, and what family experience stays similar?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a setting clue and makes a supported comparison between family experiences.

Look for: The learner identifies a setting clue and makes a supported comparison between family experiences.

If incorrect: Place one landscape clue and one family-action clue under each story image.

E5: Notice cultural details revealed in a story

Characters and events may reveal food, dress, celebrations, languages and daily routines within the particular story.

Teacher: Locate a visible or stated detail. Explain what the character does with it. Frame the observation as evidence from this story.

Ask: What feature of daily life do you notice, and how does the story show it?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: identifies a cultural feature and links it to a character or event without overgeneralising.

Look for: The learner identifies a cultural feature and links it to a character or event without overgeneralising.

If incorrect: Model a complete sentence: In this story, the character shares a meal during a celebration.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • There is only one correct personal response — Place two different supported responses beside the same text clue and show that both can be thoughtful.
  • Similar means every detail is identical — Circle one shared feature and say that experiences can be similar in one way and different in another.
  • One story feature describes every person in a cultural group — Use the words in this text, point to exact evidence and avoid claims about all families or communities.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • The character eats outside with family; what text-to-self connection could you share?
  • Which word fits your comparison, similar or different, and what clue proves it?
  • How can two readers give different thoughtful responses to the same story event?
  • What respectful words keep a comment about culture tied to this particular text?
