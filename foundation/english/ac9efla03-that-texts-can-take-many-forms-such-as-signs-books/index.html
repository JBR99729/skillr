Texts Come in Many Forms

I can recognise that a text is something people make to communicate a message. Texts can be printed, spoken, visual or digital. Their form helps the message reach its audience and purpose.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can recognise that a text is something people make to communicate a message. Texts can be printed, spoken, visual or digital. Their form helps the message reach its audience and purpose.

Learning intention: I can recognise that a text is something people make to communicate a message. Texts can be printed, spoken, visual or digital. Their form helps the message reach its audience and purpose.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise several forms of text.
  • I can find the message a text communicates.
  • I can explain a familiar text purpose.
  • I can tell text form from text purpose.
  • I can represent one message in another text form.
Key vocabulary
  • text — something made to communicate a message
  • form — the kind or shape a text takes, such as a sign, book or screen page
  • message — the idea or information being communicated
  • purpose — the job a text is made to do
  • audience — the people a text is made for

Learning activities
  1. Text hunt — Find three classroom texts, such as a label, book and screen notice. Name each form.
    label • book • screen notice
  2. Message match — Match each found text to the message it communicates.
    text form → message
  3. Change the form — Turn a spoken pack-away reminder into a small sign or picture message.
    spoken reminder → visual sign
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Compare images in informative and imaginative texts

Images in informative and imaginative texts can show the same subject, but they may do different jobs: one may explain real features while another helps invent a story world.

Teacher: Name what both images show. Find one feature that is the same. Find one difference and connect it to information or imagination.

Ask: A labelled whale diagram and a story picture of a whale flying above a town both show a whale. What is one similarity and one difference?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: gives an observable similarity and difference, then links the diagram to explaining or the story image to imagining.

Look for: The learner gives an observable similarity and difference, then links the diagram to explaining or the story image to imagining.

If incorrect: Use two hoops labelled Same and Different and place one picture clue in each before discussing purpose.

E2: Compare a sign with a set of instructions

The same topic can be presented in different text forms because a quick sign and a step-by-step instruction text serve different reading needs.

Teacher: Name the information both texts share. Notice how each form organises it. Explain when each form would be useful.

Ask: What does a one-picture Wash hands sign do quickly, and what do numbered handwashing instructions add?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies the shared handwashing message and explains that the instructions add an ordered process.

Look for: The learner identifies the shared handwashing message and explains that the instructions add an ordered process.

If incorrect: Act out the sign as one reminder, then walk through the numbered cards one at a time.

E3: Find text forms at school, home and in the community

Signs, labels and directions are texts when people use them to communicate a message in a place.

Teacher: Notice a made message in the place. Name its form, such as sign, label or direction. Explain what it helps people know or do.

Ask: A red pedestrian symbol stands near a crossing, a name label is on a lunchbox and arrows point to the library. What message does each text communicate?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies at least two forms and explains a place-specific message for each.

Look for: The learner identifies at least two forms and explains a place-specific message for each.

If incorrect: Use a three-column mat labelled Place, Text form and Message, and sort one example together.

E4: Listen to and view an attributed First Nations story

Many First Nations Australian stories and cultural accounts are shared through oral narration and may also use authorised images; oral and visual forms carry meaning and are not incomplete versions of a written book.

Teacher: Name the storyteller, creator or approved source. Listen for meaning in the oral narration. Notice how authorised images add to or support that meaning.

Ask: In this named, approved story, what do you learn from listening, and what does one authorised image help you notice?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: refers to the selected source, distinguishes an oral clue from a visual clue and avoids treating one story as a rule for all First Nations cultures.

Look for: The learner refers to the selected source, distinguishes an oral clue from a visual clue and avoids treating one story as a rule for all First Nations cultures.

If incorrect: Replay one permitted short section, pause at one approved image and use the frames I heard this clue and I noticed this image clue.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Only books are texts — Compare a book, sign and digital message and identify the message communicated by each one.
  • Any object is automatically a text — Ask whether someone made or used the object to communicate a message; a plain object and a labelled object are different.
  • Text form and purpose mean the same thing — Name the form first, such as sign, then name its job, such as warning or informing.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • A door card says ‘Quiet, baby sleeping’. What is the text form and what is its message?
  • How could a teacher share the same excursion reminder as print, speech and a digital message?
  • Is a plain lunchbox a text? What could make it communicate a message?
  • Name the form, message and purpose of one classroom text.
