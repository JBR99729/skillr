FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Find messages in signs, books and screens AC9EFLA03

Learning intention: I can notice different forms of text and find what they communicate. Texts can use words, pictures or speech.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One message can take different forms

Look at the printed sign and the pictured screen notice. Both say that the library is closed today. An adult can also say the same message aloud. The form changes, but the main message stays the same. The pictured book cover is another form: it names an invented story, Lost Hat. A text’s form and its purpose are related ideas, but they are not the same thing.

Learn the skill

1. Recognise a form and its message

A sign gives a message in a place. A book can have a cover and pages. Some books tell stories; others give information. Look at the actual example before deciding its job.

Shown examples: the LIBRARY CLOSED TODAY sign and the Lost Hat story-book cover.

Try it · Which example is a sign, and what does it tell us?

The library notice is a sign. It tells readers the library is closed today. Lost Hat is the title on the story-book cover.

2. Notice screen and spoken texts

A screen can carry a message, just as a printed page can. Spoken words can also communicate a text. Listen to the actual words; the device alone does not tell us what the message is.

Adult says: “The library is closed today.” The screen picture carries the same notice. This is a static screen drawing, not an interactive app.

Try it · What message do both forms share?

Both tell us the library is closed today. One message is spoken and one appears on the pictured screen.

3. Compare forms for the same need

A short sign can remind us quickly. Numbered instructions can tell us steps in order. Explain what each form adds instead of saying one is always better.

Sign: “Wash hands.” Instructions: “1 Wet. 2 Soap. 3 Scrub. 4 Rinse. 5 Dry.” These are comparison examples, not a timed health procedure.

Try it · What do the numbered instructions add to the sign’s reminder?

They show a sequence of actions. The short sign gives a quick reminder. Both concern handwashing, but they organise the message differently.

Common mistakes

“Only books are texts.” A sign, screen message or spoken announcement can communicate meaning too.

“Every book is a story.” Books can also give information. Use the actual book’s clues.

“Form means the same as purpose.” Form names the kind of text; purpose tells what it is trying to do.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Keep the message; change the form

Say “Please put books here” aloud. Make a small sign that keeps the message, using words or an explained picture. Compare what stayed the same and how the form changed.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the supplied closed-library notice in two forms. Core: make one new sign from a spoken reminder. Extend: explain when a spoken reminder might be more useful.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a picture without written words be part of a text?

Yes. A deliberately made picture can communicate a message. Explain what this particular picture communicates.

2. Does a screen always show a game?

No. It may show a notice, story, information or another kind of text. Look at the content.

3. Is a plain pebble automatically a text?

No. Ask whether someone has made or used it to communicate a message. Its use and context matter.

FAQ

Can an adult read all the words?

Yes. The focus is recognising forms and meaning, rather than testing independent decoding.

What is a useful comparison?

Keep one message the same across two forms. Then name what changed and what each form helps the audience do.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • text — something made to communicate a message
  • form — the kind or shape a text takes, such as a sign, book or screen page
  • message — the idea or information being communicated
  • purpose — the job a text is made to do
  • audience — the people a text is made for

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Compare images in informative and imaginative texts

Images in informative and imaginative texts can show the same subject, but they may do different jobs: one may explain real features while another helps invent a story world.

Think: Name what both images show. Find one feature that is the same. Find one difference and connect it to information or imagination.

Try: A labelled whale diagram and a story picture of a whale flying above a town both show a whale. What is one similarity and one difference?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: gives an observable similarity and difference, then links the diagram to explaining or the story image to imagining.

Check: The learner gives an observable similarity and difference, then links the diagram to explaining or the story image to imagining.

If you are stuck: Use two hoops labelled Same and Different and place one picture clue in each before discussing purpose.

E2: Compare a sign with a set of instructions

The same topic can be presented in different text forms because a quick sign and a step-by-step instruction text serve different reading needs.

Think: Name the information both texts share. Notice how each form organises it. Explain when each form would be useful.

Try: What does a one-picture Wash hands sign do quickly, and what do numbered handwashing instructions add?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies the shared handwashing message and explains that the instructions add an ordered process.

Check: The learner identifies the shared handwashing message and explains that the instructions add an ordered process.

If you are stuck: Act out the sign as one reminder, then walk through the numbered cards one at a time.

E3: Find text forms at school, home and in the community

Signs, labels and directions are texts when people use them to communicate a message in a place.

Think: Notice a made message in the place. Name its form, such as sign, label or direction. Explain what it helps people know or do.

Try: A red pedestrian symbol stands near a crossing, a name label is on a lunchbox and arrows point to the library. What message does each text communicate?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies at least two forms and explains a place-specific message for each.

Check: The learner identifies at least two forms and explains a place-specific message for each.

If you are stuck: Use a three-column mat labelled Place, Text form and Message, and sort one example together.

E4: Listen to and view an attributed First Nations story

Many First Nations Australian stories and cultural accounts are shared through oral narration and may also use authorised images; oral and visual forms carry meaning and are not incomplete versions of a written book.

Think: Name the storyteller, creator or approved source. Listen for meaning in the oral narration. Notice how authorised images add to or support that meaning.

Try: In this named, approved story, what do you learn from listening, and what does one authorised image help you notice?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: refers to the selected source, distinguishes an oral clue from a visual clue and avoids treating one story as a rule for all First Nations cultures.

Check: The learner refers to the selected source, distinguishes an oral clue from a visual clue and avoids treating one story as a rule for all First Nations cultures.

If you are stuck: Replay one permitted short section, pause at one approved image and use the frames I heard this clue and I noticed this image clue.

Adult preparation for the optional oral-and-image activity

The E4 activity requires an actual lawfully available illustrated text by a named First Nations Australian creator. One suggested book is Little Bird’s Day, by Sally Morgan, illustrated by Johnny Warrkatja Malibirr. An adult must obtain the actual book or an authorised classroom version, check its supplied context and any access conditions, read the selected part aloud and show its actual image. Publisher book and teacher-note page supports preparation; teacher notes alone are not the illustrated book. If the text is unavailable, use another suitable lawful named selection before asking the child to answer E4.

Response guidance for this packet

Check the child’s idea against the actual selected words and image. Keep the creator/source named. An adult reading a written text aloud is not a claim to have heard the creator’s recorded oral performance. Do not invent a cultural story or infer permission from citation alone.

Common misconceptions
  • Only books are texts — Compare a book, sign and digital message and identify the message communicated by each one.
  • Any object is automatically a text — Ask whether someone made or used the object to communicate a message; a plain object and a labelled object are different.
  • Text form and purpose mean the same thing — Name the form first, such as sign, then name its job, such as warning or informing.
Explain and apply

Find the message

Look at a sign, food label, picture book or weather page. What message does it communicate?

Answer and teaching guidance

A text is made to communicate. The learner should name the message, not only the object.

Name the form

A red road sign says STOP. What form of text is it?

Answer and teaching guidance

It is a sign. The form helps readers know how to use the text quickly.

Match form and purpose

Match picture book, food label and weather page to the job each one usually does.

Answer and teaching guidance

Picture books can tell stories, food labels name or describe food, and weather pages share forecasts.

Change speech into print

Turn Pack away now into a simple printed classroom text.

Answer and teaching guidance

A valid answer keeps the message but changes the form, such as a classroom sign with words and a picture cue.

Try these
  1. Text hunt — Find three classroom texts, such as a label, book and screen notice. Name each form.
