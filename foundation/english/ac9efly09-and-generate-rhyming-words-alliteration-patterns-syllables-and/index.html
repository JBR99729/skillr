rhyme, alliteration, syllables and phonemes

I can listen to spoken language to hear and make rhymes, notice alliteration, clap syllables and isolate an individual speech sound.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can listen to spoken language to hear and make rhymes, notice alliteration, clap syllables and isolate an individual speech sound.

Learning intention: I can listen to spoken language to hear and make rhymes, notice alliteration, clap syllables and isolate an individual speech sound.

Success criteria

  • I can recognise a spoken rhyme.
  • I can generate a rhyming word.
  • I can recognise alliteration by sound.
  • I can clap spoken syllables.
  • I can isolate one speech sound using a cue word.
Key vocabulary
  • rhyme — words whose spoken endings sound the same
  • alliteration — nearby words beginning with the same speech sound
  • syllable — a spoken beat in a word
  • phoneme — the smallest speech sound that can change a word
  • spoken word — a word we hear or say
Model and guided application

Worked example: hear and make a rhyme

Worked-model method: listen, say or clap, then name the sound pattern.

1. Listen as the teacher says cake and lake without showing the spellings.2. Repeat the pair and stretch the ending of each word; the endings sound the same.3. Name the feature as rhyme and offer make as another spoken word with the same ending sound.

Model answer: Cake, lake and make rhyme because their spoken endings sound the same.

Worked example: compare alliteration, syllables and one sound

Worked-model method: listen, say or clap, then name the sound pattern.

1. Say ‘helpful Henry’ and notice that both words begin with the same gentle breathy sound.2. Say banana and clap three spoken beats: ba, na, na; each beat is a syllable.3. Say moon slowly and isolate the humming sound at its beginning as one speech sound.

Model answer: Helpful Henry shows alliteration, banana has three syllables and moon begins with one humming speech sound.

Learning activities
  1. Rhyme basket — Listen to three object names and place together the two whose endings rhyme.
    hear the whole ending; do not decide from spelling
  2. Beginning-sound parade — Choose two picture names that begin with the same sound and say them as an alliterative pair.
    silly snake • busy bee • helpful hen
  3. Clap and stretch — Clap the syllables in a familiar name, then say the first speech sound with a cue word.
    whole word → beats → first speech sound
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Hear and produce spoken rhymes

Rhyming words share the same or a very similar spoken ending, even when learners are not shown how the words are written.

Teacher: Say cake and lake while pictures are visible but print is hidden. Repeat the pair and compare their spoken endings. Invite make or another valid spoken rhyme and repeat the set.

Ask: Which word has the same spoken ending as cake, and what new rhyme can you make?

Students: Listen to and repeat the rhyme pair, compare the spoken endings, then say another word whose ending follows the same sound pattern.

Look for: The learner recognises a spoken rhyme and produces another word with the matching ending sound.

If incorrect: Offer two picture names, lake and fish, repeat both after cake and ask the learner to echo the matching pair.

E2: Notice repeated beginning sounds

Alliteration occurs when nearby spoken words begin with the same speech sound.

Teacher: Say 'helpful Henry' naturally. Repeat it slowly and notice the gentle breathy sound at both beginnings. Compare it with 'helpful Mia' and create another matching pair.

Ask: Which words begin with the same speech sound, and what do you hear at both beginnings?

Students: Say the alliterative phrase, identify its repeated beginning sound with a cue word, then create another two-word example.

Look for: The learner identifies the repeated beginning sound in a spoken phrase and generates another appropriate alliterative pairing.

If incorrect: Use two picture names beginning with the same cue sound and one contrasting picture, then say every name aloud.

E3: Clap syllables in spoken words

A syllable is a spoken beat in a word, and clapping can make each beat easier to notice.

Teacher: Say banana as a whole word. Repeat it slowly and clap the three beats ba, na and na. Count the claps, then compare with a one-beat word such as moon.

Ask: How many spoken beats do you hear in this word, and where will you clap?

Students: Say the whole word naturally, clap once for each spoken beat, count the claps and compare the count with a partner's pronunciation.

Look for: The learner coordinates one clap with each naturally spoken syllable and reports the matching count.

If incorrect: Say the word while placing one counter for each beat before adding the clap movement.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Rhyming words must look alike — Hide the print and compare the spoken endings with careful listening.
  • A syllable is the same as one speech sound — Clap the word beats first, then stretch one beat to hear smaller speech sounds inside it.
  • A letter name is the beginning sound — Say the whole cue word and imitate the sound heard at its beginning rather than reciting the letter name.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Which word rhymes with lake when you say the words aloud?
  • What repeated beginning sound do you hear in helpful Henry?
  • How many syllables do you clap in banana?
  • Say moon slowly and identify its first speech sound using the moon cue.

Mastery evidence

  • Which word rhymes with lake when you say the words aloud?
