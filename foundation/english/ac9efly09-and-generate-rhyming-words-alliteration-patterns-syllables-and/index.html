Listen for patterns in spoken words AC9EFLY09

Learning intention: I can hear and make rhymes, repeated beginnings and word beats, then notice a smaller speech sound.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Same ending or same beginning?

Adult: say “boat, goat” and “busy bee” naturally before showing print. Boat and goat rhyme because their spoken endings match. Busy bee repeats its beginning sound, so it shows alliteration. Now say “rabbit”: two claps mark its two syllables. Say “moon” and hold its first humming sound: that is one phoneme, smaller than a syllable.

Learn the skill

1. Hear and make a rhyme

Listen from the vowel to the end of the spoken word. Rhymes share that ending sound. Print can help an adult remember the words, but the child should decide by listening.

Adult: say “hen, pen, dog”. Repeat each whole word. Keep the written words out of the child’s view.

Try it · Which word does not rhyme with the other two? Say another rhyme for hen.

Dog is different. Hen and pen rhyme; ten or another matching spoken rhyme can join them. Accept a valid rhyme in the child’s variety of English.

2. Repeat the beginning sound

Alliteration repeats the first speech sound of nearby words. It is not the same as sharing any letter or having a matching ending. Say each word aloud.

Adult: say “Sam sees seals” and “Sam likes jam”. Repeat the beginning of each word gently.

Try it · Which phrase repeats the same beginning sound? Make another matching pair.

Sam sees seals repeats the hissing beginning sound. “Silly snake” is another possible pair. Sam and jam rhyme, but their first sounds differ.

3. Hear beats, then isolate one sound

A syllable is a spoken beat. A phoneme is an individual speech sound. Clap the beats in a whole word, then listen for a specified smaller sound without reciting letter names.

Adult: say “sun”, then “rabbit”. Clap their beats. Say “sun” again and model its first hissing sound without an extra “uh”.

Try it · How many beats are in each word? What sound starts sun?

Sun has one syllable; rabbit usually has two. Sun begins with the hissing sound /s/. Its one syllable contains three phonemes. The child imitates the heard sound; reading this notation is not required.

Common mistakes

“Rhyme means the first sounds match.” Compare the spoken endings; matching beginnings make alliteration.

“One clap always means one sound.” A clap marks a syllable, which may contain several speech sounds.

“The letter name is the sound I heard.” Say the cue word and imitate the specified speech sound.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Listen, make and sort

An adult says “cat, hat, fish” for a rhyme choice; asks for a new rhyme for cat; then says “Mia makes maps” for a beginning-sound pattern. Finish by clapping banana and isolating the first sound of fish. Keep the activity oral and let the child repeat words.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose a rhyme from two spoken words. Core: generate a rhyme and an alliterative pair, then clap beats. Stretch: explain why dog has one beat but three phonemes and isolate its final sound.

Use familiar words and the child’s natural pronunciation. Adults supply real speech; silent print is not an audio test. Accept a pointed choice for recognition while separately observing spoken generation if that is the goal.

Three tricky questions

1. Do blue and shoe rhyme even though their spellings differ?

Yes, their spoken endings match in usual Australian pronunciation. Listen rather than compare letters.

2. Do cat and kite show alliteration?

Yes, they begin with the same /k/ sound, although their first letters differ. They do not rhyme.

3. Is banana three words because it has three claps?

No. Banana is one word with three syllables. The claps do not separate it into three meaning units.

FAQ

Must every rhyme be a real word?

Playful invented rhymes can practise the sound pattern. Ask separately for a real word when that is the goal.

What if a word has a different beat count in a child’s pronunciation?

Listen to their natural spoken form and discuss it respectfully. Do not mark a dialect difference as a listening error.

Key vocabulary
  • rhyme — words whose spoken endings sound the same
  • alliteration — nearby words beginning with the same speech sound
  • syllable — a spoken beat in a word
  • phoneme — the smallest speech sound that can change a word
  • spoken word — a word we hear or say

Worked-model method: listen, say or clap, then name the sound pattern.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Hear and produce spoken rhymes

Rhyming words share the same or a very similar spoken ending, even when learners are not shown how the words are written.

Think: Say cake and lake while pictures are visible but print is hidden. Repeat the pair and compare their spoken endings. Invite make or another valid spoken rhyme and repeat the set.

Try: Which word has the same spoken ending as cake, and what new rhyme can you make?

A strong answer: Listen to and repeat the rhyme pair, compare the spoken endings, then say another word whose ending follows the same sound pattern.

Check: The learner recognises a spoken rhyme and produces another word with the matching ending sound.

If you are stuck: Offer two picture names, lake and fish, repeat both after cake and ask the learner to echo the matching pair.

E2: Notice repeated beginning sounds

Alliteration occurs when nearby spoken words begin with the same speech sound.

Think: Say 'helpful Henry' naturally. Repeat it slowly and notice the gentle breathy sound at both beginnings. Compare it with 'helpful Mia' and create another matching pair.

Try: Which words begin with the same speech sound, and what do you hear at both beginnings?

A strong answer: Say the alliterative phrase, identify its repeated beginning sound with a cue word, then create another two-word example.

Check: The learner identifies the repeated beginning sound in a spoken phrase and generates another appropriate alliterative pairing.

If you are stuck: Use two picture names beginning with the same cue sound and one contrasting picture, then say every name aloud.

E3: Clap syllables in spoken words

A syllable is a spoken beat in a word, and clapping can make each beat easier to notice.

Think: Say banana as a whole word. Repeat it slowly and clap the three beats ba, na and na. Count the claps, then compare with a one-beat word such as moon.

Try: How many spoken beats do you hear in this word, and where will you clap?

A strong answer: Say the whole word naturally, clap once for each spoken beat, count the claps and compare the count with a partner's pronunciation.

Check: The learner coordinates one clap with each naturally spoken syllable and reports the matching count.

If you are stuck: Say the word while placing one counter for each beat before adding the clap movement.

Common misconceptions
  • Rhyming words must look alike — Hide the print and compare the spoken endings with careful listening.
  • A syllable is the same as one speech sound — Clap the word beats first, then stretch one beat to hear smaller speech sounds inside it.
  • A letter name is the beginning sound — Say the whole cue word and imitate the sound heard at its beginning rather than reciting the letter name.
Listen, explain and apply

Listen before looking at print

Adult: say the words naturally and repeat if needed. For rhyme, alliteration and syllables, assess what the child hears and says. Reading or spelling the words is not required.

Find the word that does not rhyme

Adult: say “hat, sun, mat”. Which word does not rhyme with the other two? Say a new word that rhymes with hat.

Answer and teaching guidance

Sun is the odd one out; hat and mat share their spoken ending. Cat or bat is a possible new rhyme. Accept another spoken rhyme in the child’s variety of English.

Beginnings or endings?

Say “sun, sock” and “sun, run”. Which pair shares a beginning sound? Which pair rhymes?

Answer and teaching guidance

Sun and sock share the beginning sound. Sun and run rhyme. Listen to the requested part of the word instead of treating any matching sound as rhyme.

A beat is not a phoneme

Say “dog”. Clap its syllables, then slowly say its separate speech sounds. Are the counts the same?

Answer and teaching guidance

Dog has one syllable and three speech sounds. One clap marks a syllable; three counters can represent the smaller sounds. Keep letter names out of this listening task.

Ready to move on

Adult: ask for a rhyme for “hen”, a pair of words beginning like “moon”, and the syllables in “rabbit”.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, pen; moon and map; two beats in rabbit. Accept valid alternatives and natural pronunciation. Check each skill separately; reteach the one that needs support.

Fresh oral sound check

Adult says dog. Ask for its syllable count and final phoneme. It has one syllable and ends in /g/. Ask for a new rhyme and a phrase beginning like dog; log and dancing dog are possible responses. Accept locally valid alternatives.

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Adult: say each set aloud. Rhyming pair: cake–lake / cake–fish. Rhyme for cat: hat / dog. Alliteration: helpful Henry / helpful Mia. Repeated /b/ beginning: busy bee / happy bee. The first choice fits each stated pattern. Also ask the child for a new rhyme for hen, an alliterative pair beginning like moon, the beats in rabbit, and the first sound of sun. Possible answers: pen; moon/map; two; /s/.
