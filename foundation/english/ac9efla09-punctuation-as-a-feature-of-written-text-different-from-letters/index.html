Capital Letters and Ending Punctuation

I can tell that letters make words while punctuation marks organise written text. A capital letter can show the beginning of a sentence or the name of a particular person or place. A full stop, question mark or exclamation mark can signal that a sentence has ended.

What students learn

I can tell that letters make words while punctuation marks organise written text. A capital letter can show the beginning of a sentence or the name of a particular person or place. A full stop, question mark or exclamation mark can signal that a sentence has ended.

Learning intention: I can tell that letters make words while punctuation marks organise written text. A capital letter can show the beginning of a sentence or the name of a particular person or place. A full stop, question mark or exclamation mark can signal that a sentence has ended.

Success criteria

  • I can tell letters from punctuation marks.
  • I can use a capital at a sentence beginning.
  • I can use a capital for a familiar name.
  • I can choose an ending mark that fits the message.
  • I can explain how capitals and punctuation show sentence boundaries.
Key vocabulary
  • capital letter — the large form of a letter, used at sentence beginnings and for names
  • punctuation — marks that help organise written meaning
  • full stop — a dot that signals the end of a telling sentence
  • question mark — a mark that signals the end of a question
  • sentence boundary — the visible beginning or ending of a written sentence

Learning activities
  1. Letter or mark? — Sort large cards into letters that build words and punctuation that organises the sentence.
    A m t → letters • . ? ! → punctuation
  2. Capital hunt — Find the first word and familiar names in three short sentences. Circle only the capitals that are needed.
    sentence start • person name • place name
  3. Read the ending — Match a telling, asking or strong-warning voice to its full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.
    telling . • asking ? • strong warning !
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Notice capital letters in everyday names

Capital letters appear in everyday texts to begin the names of particular people, families, places and other named things, and a child can connect a capital with the matching lower-case letter.

Teacher: Find the name in the everyday text. Point to its first capital letter. Explain whose or which name the capital begins.

Ask: A library card says “Noah,” and a map label says “Darwin.” What can you say about the capitals N and D?

Students: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies each capital as the first letter of a particular name and can match it with its lower-case form.

Look for: The learner identifies each capital as the first letter of a particular name and can match it with its lower-case form.

If incorrect: Place N beside n and D beside d, then model complete statements: That capital starts the name Noah; that capital starts the name Darwin.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Every large-looking mark is a capital letter — Sort examples by job: capital letters are letter forms that help make words; punctuation marks are not letters.
  • A capital belongs only at the start of a line — Follow the sentence, not the line, and mark its actual beginning plus any names.
  • Any ending mark can finish any message — Say the sentence aloud as telling, asking or a strong feeling, then choose the mark that fits.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • A written sentence has capital A at the start of the name Ava and a full stop after hops. Which feature is punctuation, and which capital begins the name?
  • A sentence is written with lower-case w at its beginning, lower-case t at the start of the name Tom, and no ending mark. Say the repaired sentence and name each change.
  • The words ‘Can I play’ ask a question. Name the ending mark that fits and explain why.
  • Explain why a full stop is not a letter even though it appears beside letters.
