FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Notice capital letters and punctuation AC9EFLA09

Learning intention: I can tell letters from punctuation and notice how a sentence begins and ends. Names use capital letters too.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The same sentence shows two jobs for capitals

Look at “We met Ava.” W begins the sentence. A begins the person’s name Ava, even though the name is later in the sentence. The full stop ends this telling sentence. W and A are letters; the full stop is punctuation. They do different jobs in the written message.

Learn the skill

1. Tell a letter from a punctuation mark

Letters help spell words. A capital and its lower-case form are both letters. Punctuation marks help organise written meaning; a mark can be large without being a capital letter.

Cards: m / M / . / ?

Try it · Which two cards are letters?

m and M are letters. The full stop and question mark are punctuation marks, not letters used to spell a word.

2. Find the beginning and the name

In these examples, a capital begins the sentence and another capital begins a person’s name. A name keeps its capital wherever it appears. Do not put a capital at every new line just because it is a new line.

The writer means a calm telling sentence: “we saw ben”.

Try it · What capitals are needed?

Change we to We because it starts the sentence, and ben to Ben because it is a person’s name. The complete written example is We saw Ben.

3. Notice the sentence ending

A full stop can finish an ordinary telling sentence. A question mark finishes a written question. Read what the message means, then notice its ending mark.

Telling: “The dog is here.” Asking: “Where is the dog?”

Try it · Which mark finishes the question, and what does it show?

The question mark finishes Where is the dog? It shows that the written sentence is asking a question. The full stop ends the supplied calm telling sentence.

Common mistakes

“A large question mark is a capital.” It is still punctuation. Capitals are letter forms.

“Only the first word can have a capital.” Names need capitals even in the middle or end of a sentence.

“A line break ends every sentence.” Follow the sentence and its meaning; it can continue onto another line.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Sort, find and repair

Sort m, M, . and ? into letters and punctuation. In “We met Ava.” point to each capital and say its job. Then repair the calm telling sentence “we saw ben”.

Adjust the challenge Support: an adult reads the whole message and offers two marks. Core: explain both capitals and add the ending. Extend: find a name later in a new sentence and explain why it still has a capital.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. In “We met Ava.” is A wrong because it is not the first letter in the sentence?

No. It starts the name Ava, so it needs a capital too.

2. If a sentence continues on the next printed line, must its next word begin with a capital?

No. A line break alone is not a new sentence. A name may still need its own capital.

3. Does a full stop make any mixed-up words a clear sentence?

No. The words must first communicate the intended message. Punctuation does not add missing meaning.

FAQ

Must Foundation learners master every punctuation mark?

No. Start with noticing letters, capitals, names and sentence boundaries in short familiar examples. Other marks can be discussed when encountered.

Can a child explain by pointing?

Yes. An adult can read or scribe while the child points to the capital or punctuation and explains its job in an accessible way.

Key vocabulary
  • capital letter — the large form of a letter, used at sentence beginnings and for names
  • punctuation — marks that help organise written meaning
  • full stop — a dot that signals the end of a telling sentence
  • question mark — a mark that signals the end of a question
  • sentence boundary — the visible beginning or ending of a written sentence

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Notice capital letters in everyday names

Capital letters appear in everyday texts to begin the names of particular people, families, places and other named things, and a child can connect a capital with the matching lower-case letter.

Think: Find the name in the everyday text. Point to its first capital letter. Explain whose or which name the capital begins.

Try: A library card says “Noah,” and a map label says “Darwin.” What can you say about the capitals N and D?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: identifies each capital as the first letter of a particular name and can match it with its lower-case form.

Check: The learner identifies each capital as the first letter of a particular name and can match it with its lower-case form.

If you are stuck: Place N beside n and D beside d, then model complete statements: That capital starts the name Noah; that capital starts the name Darwin.

Common misconceptions
  • Every large-looking mark is a capital letter — Sort examples by job: capital letters are letter forms that help make words; punctuation marks are not letters.
  • A capital belongs only at the start of a line — Follow the sentence, not the line, and mark its actual beginning plus any names.
  • Any ending mark can finish any message — Say the sentence aloud as telling, asking or a strong feeling, then choose the mark that fits.
Explain and apply

Show the writing

Read “We met Ava.” Find the capital at the sentence beginning, the capital in the name and the ending mark.

Answer and teaching guidance

W begins the sentence; A begins Ava’s name; the full stop ends the telling sentence. Ava needs its capital even though the name is not the first word.

Repair the visible example

The writer means a telling sentence: “we saw ben”. Write it with the capitals and ending mark it needs.

Answer and teaching guidance

We saw Ben. Change w to W, change b to B and add a full stop. Adult: let the learner point out the three repairs before writing if handwriting is a barrier.

Letters and marks have different jobs

In “Is it Mia?” point to a capital letter and a punctuation mark. What does each do?

Answer and teaching guidance

I begins the sentence, M begins a name, and the question mark ends the question. Capitals are letter forms; the question mark is not a letter used to spell a word.

Choose for the message

Write “The dog is here” as a calm statement and “Where is the dog” as a question.

Answer and teaching guidance

The dog is here. Where is the dog? Support: offer a full stop and question mark to choose from. Extend: explain why a new printed line does not automatically begin a new sentence.
