retell and adapt familiar literary texts

I can retell the main events of a familiar literary text in order and adapt one part while the text stays recognisable.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can retell the main events of a familiar literary text in order and adapt one part while the text stays recognisable.

Learning intention: I can retell the main events of a familiar literary text in order and adapt one part while the text stays recognisable.

Success criteria

  • I can choose the main events rather than every small detail.
  • I can sequence story pictures by what happens first, next and finally.
  • I can retell the familiar text so a listener can follow it.
  • I can adapt one character, setting, event or ending through play, images or words.
  • I can explain what stayed the same and what changed.
Key vocabulary
  • retell — tell a text again using its important events
  • sequence — the order in which events happen
  • adapt — change part of a familiar text to make a new version
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a literary text
  • event — something that happens in a text
  • beginning — the first part of a text or retell
  • ending — the final part that finishes a text or retell
  • performance — showing a text to others through voice, movement or acting
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: retell the main events in sequence

In a familiar class story, Tama plants a seed, waters it each day and sees a yellow flower open.

Use the pictures as memory clues. Say a complete event for each picture rather than naming single objects.

Worked example 2: adapt one part of the known story

Keep Tama, the planting and the daily care. Change the final plant from a yellow flower to a tall bean vine.

Tell listeners what stayed the same and what changed. The change can be shown through play, performance, images or writing.

Learning activities
  1. Picture-path retell — Children place three main-event pictures from left to right, then retell using first, next and finally.
    Three numbered event frames connected by arrows
  2. Freeze-frame performance — Small groups make a still pose for one main event. The audience identifies where the event belongs in the sequence.
    Beginning, middle and ending floor markers
  3. Change one card — Replace one character, setting or ending card. Children retell the adapted version and name exactly what changed.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Retell through drawing and role-play

Drawing and role-play can represent characters and main events from a familiar literary text.

Teacher: Choose one main event. Draw it or make a freeze-frame pose. Say who is present and what is happening.

Ask: Which story event are you showing, and what clue will help the audience recognise it?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: represents a recognisable character and event and explains where it belongs in the story.

Look for: The learner represents a recognisable character and event and explains where it belongs in the story.

If incorrect: Place the original event image beside the child's drawing or pose and match one feature at a time.

E2: Sequence pictures to retell a story

Ordered pictures can hold the main event sequence and support an oral, written or digitally recorded retell.

Teacher: Select the main-event images. Arrange them from beginning to ending. Retell each image in order and check the sequence.

Ask: What happened before this picture, and what must happen after it?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: orders the pictures from story evidence and gives a connected retell.

Look for: The learner orders the pictures from story evidence and gives a connected retell.

If incorrect: Begin with the first and final event fixed, then decide where the remaining picture belongs.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • A retell includes every tiny detail — Limit the picture path to the main events needed for the story to make sense.
  • An adaptation can become an unrelated new story — Keep two or more anchor features visible and change only one clear part first.
  • Pictures are sequenced by colour or appearance instead of events — Ask what happened first, next and finally, then use story cause and time clues to reorder the images.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • What are the three main events in Tama's seed story?
  • Which details stay the same when the flower becomes a bean vine?
  • How could you show the middle event through a freeze-frame performance?
  • Why is changing every character and event no longer a clear adaptation of the familiar text?

Mastery evidence
