Retell a story and make a new version AC9EFLE05

Learning intention: I can show the important events in order, then change a clear part to make an adaptation.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the story anchors when changing an ending

In the complete Tama story, Tama plants a seed, waters the growing shoot and sees a yellow flower open. The three actual event drawings support a retell. A new version keeps planting and watering but changes the flower to a tall bean vine. Name the change so a listener can recognise both versions. Any climbing scene is pretend play, not a real climbing task.

Learn the skill

1. Choose and order important events

Read or hear the whole short story first. Select events needed to follow it. Check first, next and finally against the story, not the colours or sizes of the cards.

Tama plants a seed. Tama waters the shoot each day. A yellow flower opens.

Try it · Which event must begin this retell?

Planting the seed. Watering the growing shoot follows; the flower opening is the ending. This is the order stated in the story.

2. Show the same event in another way

A drawing, puppet scene, oral retell or freeze-frame can represent an event. Tell your listener who is present and what happens. The medium can change while the important event stays the same.

Choose “Tama waters the shoot” from the supplied sequence.

Try it · How could you show that event without writing a long paragraph?

Draw Tama watering, use a puppet with a pretend watering can, or say the event. Explain which story moment you mean. Drawing quality and handwriting are not the retelling goal.

3. Adapt one part and check the new sequence

Keep enough familiar parts to recognise the source. Change one character, place, event or ending, then check that the rest still follows. Creative changes are welcome; the new version must remain understandable.

Keep Tama planting and watering. Replace the yellow flower ending with a tall bean vine.

Try it · What stayed the same and what changed?

Tama’s planting and care stay. The plant at the ending changes. Explain any new event as part of your adaptation rather than claiming it was in the original.

Common mistakes

“A retell needs every tiny detail.” Choose the main connected events.

“I can order the cards by colour.” Use what happened before and after.

“An adaptation has to copy every word.” Keep recognisable anchors and make a deliberate change.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Picture path, then a change

Hear the Tama story. Use the three supplied drawings to retell it. Draw a new ending or act it with safe pretend materials. A partner says what stayed the same and what changed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: fix the first and last cards and place the middle. Core: retell all three events. Stretch: change one part and explain how the new ending still connects.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. Is naming seed, water, flower a connected retell by itself?

It names useful objects. Add what happens: Tama plants the seed, waters the shoot and sees the flower open.

2. If I change every character, event and place, will a listener recognise this adaptation?

They may not. Keep some clear anchors and explain the intended link.

3. Does clicking an event picture show that I performed the story?

No. A choice can show recognition. An adult would need to observe the actual retell or performance separately.

FAQ

Must a child write to retell?

No. Speech, sign, pictures, AAC, puppets and supported performance can communicate the sequence.

Can an adaptation have a different ending?

Yes. Make the change clear and check that the new events fit together.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • retell — tell a text again using its important events
  • sequence — the order in which events happen
  • adapt — change part of a familiar text to make a new version
  • character — a person, animal or imagined being in a literary text
  • event — something that happens in a text
  • beginning — the first part of a text or retell
  • ending — the final part that finishes a text or retell
  • performance — showing a text to others through voice, movement or acting
Key concept

I can retell a familiar story in order and change part of it in a new version.

Learning goal: I can retell a familiar story in order and change part of it in a new version.

I am ready when I can

  • I can choose the main events rather than every small detail.
  • I can sequence story pictures by what happens first, next and finally.
  • I can retell the familiar text so a listener can follow it.
  • I can adapt one character, setting, event or ending through play, images or words.
  • I can explain what stayed the same and what changed.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Retell through drawing and role-play

Drawing and role-play can represent characters and main events from a familiar literary text.

Think: Choose one main event. Draw it or make a freeze-frame pose. Say who is present and what is happening.

Try: Which story event are you showing, and what clue will help the audience recognise it?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: represents a recognisable character and event and explains where it belongs in the story.

Check: The learner represents a recognisable character and event and explains where it belongs in the story.

If you are stuck: Place the original event image beside the child's drawing or pose and match one feature at a time.

E2: Sequence pictures to retell a story

Ordered pictures can hold the main event sequence and support an oral, written or digitally recorded retell.

Think: Select the main-event images. Arrange them from beginning to ending. Retell each image in order and check the sequence.

Try: What happened before this picture, and what must happen after it?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: orders the pictures from story evidence and gives a connected retell.

Check: The learner orders the pictures from story evidence and gives a connected retell.

If you are stuck: Begin with the first and final event fixed, then decide where the remaining picture belongs.

Common misconceptions
  • A retell includes every tiny detail — Limit the picture path to the main events needed for the story to make sense.
  • An adaptation can become an unrelated new story — Keep two or more anchor features visible and change only one clear part first.
  • Pictures are sequenced by colour or appearance instead of events — Ask what happened first, next and finally, then use story cause and time clues to reorder the images.
Explain and apply

Retell before adapting

Tell the familiar text in three parts: first, next and finally.

Answer and teaching guidance

A clear adaptation starts with an accurate retell. Keep the main events in order so listeners recognise the original.

Change one clear part

Change the character, setting, object or ending, then say what stayed the same.

Answer and teaching guidance

The adaptation should keep enough anchors from the familiar text. Changing everything makes it a new story rather than an adapted version.

Use play or performance

Show one event as a freeze-frame, puppet scene or role-play. Ask a partner which event it shows.

Answer and teaching guidance

This lets learners demonstrate sequence through action. A partner's answer checks whether the event is clear.

Compare both versions

After the adaptation, complete: In both versions ____. In my new version ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The comparison makes the creative change purposeful and visible. Accept oral, image or writing responses.
