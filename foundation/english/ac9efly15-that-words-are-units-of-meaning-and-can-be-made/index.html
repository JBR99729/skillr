FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Find meaning inside words AC9EFLY15

Learning intention: I can explain a whole word and notice parts that add to its meaning.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A base word and a meaningful ending

Dog names an animal. Dogs keeps that base meaning and adds s to show more than one. Dog and s are two meaningful parts inside the one written word dogs. The s is not a separate word or a separate syllable here. Compare cup and cups to see the same more-than-one ending with a different base.

Learn the skill

1. Begin with the whole word’s meaning

Say a word and connect it with something familiar. A word is a unit of meaning; the letters inside it help spell it, but each letter is not usually a separate meaning part.

Adult: place one cup on the table. Show the word cup and say it.

Try it · What does cup name? Does c alone mean a smaller cup?

Cup names the object used for drinking. The letters c, u and p help spell the word; they do not each name a separate part of its meaning.

2. Keep the base and add more-than-one

Compare one and more than one. Keep the familiar base and notice the ending that changes the number meaning. The ending can sound different across words while keeping its meaning.

Adult: place one cup on one side and three cups on the other. Show cup and cups.

Try it · Which word matches each group? What does the added s tell us?

Cup matches one; cups matches three. Cup keeps the object meaning and s adds more than one. In cups it is /s/; in dogs it is /z/. Do not use sound alone to decide whether a part carries meaning.

3. Join two familiar word meanings

Some familiar words combine two words. Explain how both meanings help with the whole word. A split is useful when the parts contribute meaning, not just because a group of letters happens to look like a word.

Printed parts: rain + coat → raincoat. Adult: show or draw a coat worn in rain and name it.

Try it · What does each part contribute? Is raincoat one word or two in print?

Rain tells the weather it is used for; coat names the clothing. Raincoat is one printed word built from two meaningful words. Do not confuse its two syllables with a general rule for meaning parts.

Common mistakes

“Every letter is a meaning part.” Ask what meaning each proposed part contributes.

“Every word ending in s means more than one.” Compare meaning: bus names one vehicle; its final s is part of the base.

“A word can be split anywhere.” Choose a familiar base and ending or two words whose meanings explain the whole.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One, many and a word pair

Use one spoon and several spoons. An adult supplies the printed words spoon and spoons, reads them and asks the learner to match meaning. Then supply sun and hat cards, join sunhat and explain a hat used in sunny conditions. Read and explain the complete word each time.

Adjust the challenge Starter: match one object and a group to named words. Core: identify the base and more-than-one s in a familiar pair. Stretch: compare cats with cat, then explain why bus does not split into bu plus plural s.

An adult may read all words aloud; independent decoding is not required to explain meaning parts here. Use familiar objects or actual prepared pictures. Do not turn this introduction into a formal suffix taxonomy.

Three tricky questions

1. Is dogs two words because dog and s have meanings?

No. Dogs is one word with two meaningful parts in this example.

2. Do cat and hat belong together because they share the base cat?

No. They rhyme, but hat does not contain the base cat. A shared sound ending is different from shared meaning.

3. Must every word have more than one meaningful part?

No. Cup is a useful simple base here; cups adds a number meaning. Only separate parts that make sense for the word being studied.

FAQ

Does plural s always sound the same?

No. It is /s/ in cats and /z/ in dogs in usual speech. The more-than-one meaning stays.

What should an adult listen for?

Ask the learner to explain the whole meaning, identify a sensible part and tell what it contributes. Saying a label such as ending without its meaning is not enough.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • word — a spoken or written unit that carries meaning
  • meaningful part — a part of a word that contributes meaning
  • base word — the main word that carries the central meaning
  • plural — a word form that can show more than one
  • compound word — a word made by joining two complete words
  • ending — a meaningful part added at the end of a word
Key concept

I can understand that a word has meaning and can sometimes contain more than one meaningful part.

See the skill in action

Worked-model method: say the word, split meaningful parts and explain each meaning.

1. Compare a picture of one dog with the printed word dog and a picture of several dogs with the printed word dogs.2. Keep the meaningful part dog, which names the animal, and notice the added letter s at the end.3. Explain that the added part tells us there is more than one dog.

Model answer: Dogs has two meaningful parts: dog names the animal and the added s shows more than one.

Worked example: build the compound word sunhat

Worked-model method: say the word, split meaningful parts and explain each meaning.

1. Read two picture-word cards: sun and hat.2. Join the two complete meaning units to make the printed word sunhat.3. Explain that sun tells the condition or use and hat names the object worn on the head.

Model answer: Sunhat combines two meaningful words to name a hat worn for protection in sunny conditions.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Find meaningful parts in a word

A word can contain a base that carries the central meaning and an added part that contributes extra meaning, as dog and s work together in dogs.

Think: Match dog with a picture of one dog and dogs with a picture of several dogs. Cover the final s and identify the base word dog. Reveal s and explain that this added part signals more than one in this example.

Try: What meaning stays in the base word, and what extra meaning does the added part contribute?

A strong answer: Match the whole word to its picture meaning, separate the base and ending or two complete words, rebuild it and explain each part's meaning.

Check: The learner separates dog and s as two meaningful parts and explains the contribution of each to the whole word dogs.

If you are stuck: Use one-dog and several-dogs picture cards, then physically join the printed base card dog and ending card s.

Common misconceptions
  • Every letter is a separate meaningful part — Ask whether the part contributes a stable meaning, rather than splitting the word at every letter.
  • A word can be cut anywhere — Use picture or sentence meaning to find a base word or two complete words that make sense.
  • An added ending changes the whole base meaning — State the base meaning first, then explain only the extra meaning the ending adds.
Explain and apply

Find the base meaning

Compare dog and dogs. What meaning stays in both words?

Answer and teaching guidance

Dog remains the base meaning. The added s changes number, not the animal meaning.

Add more-than-one

Make cup mean more than one by adding the ending s.

Answer and teaching guidance

Cups keeps the base cup and adds an ending that means more than one.

Split a compound

Split raincoat into two complete words and explain how the meanings work together.

Answer and teaching guidance

Rain and coat are meaningful parts. Together they name a coat used for rainy weather.

Correct the split

A learner says every letter in dogs is a meaningful part. How would you fix that?

Answer and teaching guidance

Dogs is one whole word with the base dog and the ending s. Individual letters help spell the word but are not each separate meanings.

Meaning family or just rhyme?

Compare cat, cats and hat. Cat and cats share the base meaning cat; s adds more than one. Cat and hat rhyme, but hat is not built from the base cat. Compare bus and buses only orally if useful; do not claim the final s of bus is itself a plural ending.

Try these
  1. One or more — Match pictures of one object and several objects with a base word and its plural form.
    cat → cats • cup → cups
  2. Word-part build — Join picture-word cards to make a familiar compound word and explain both parts.
    sun + hat • rain + coat
  3. Meaning detective — Underline the base word, circle the added part and say what changed in the meaning.
    whole word → base meaning → added meaning

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • What does the word ‘dog’ do?
  • In ‘dogs’, what does the added s tell us?
  • How many meaningful parts are in ‘dogs’ for this example?
  • Which word means more than one cat?
