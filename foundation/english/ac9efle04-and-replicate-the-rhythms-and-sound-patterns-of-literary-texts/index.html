AC9EFLE04 • Foundation English

rhythms and sound patterns in literary texts

I can hear and copy the beat, rhyme and repeated sounds in a poem, rhyme or song.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What students learn

I can hear and copy the beat, rhyme and repeated sounds in a poem, rhyme or song.

Learning intention: I can hear and copy the beat, rhyme and repeated sounds in a poem, rhyme or song.

Success criteria

  • I can keep a steady beat while listening to a short literary line.
  • I can identify two spoken words that rhyme.
  • I can copy a short rhythm with my voice and body.
  • I can repeat words, sounds and actions in the modelled order.
  • I can describe the difference between a steady beat and a matching end sound.
Key vocabulary
  • rhythm — the pattern of long, short, strong and soft parts we hear in words or music
  • beat — the steady pulse we can tap while a rhyme, poem or song continues
  • rhyme — words that have the same or very similar ending sound
  • sound pattern — sounds that repeat or are arranged in a noticeable way
  • chant — words spoken together with a strong repeated rhythm
  • action — a body movement used to show or support a rhythm
Model and guided application

Worked example 1: keep the beat and hear the rhyme

Say: “Tap your knee, count to three. Clap your hands and sway with me.”

First listen. Next tap a steady pulse. Then repeat the words and actions without racing.

Worked example 2: copy a repeating sound-and-action pattern

Chant: “Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop. Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop.”

Listen for the whole repeating unit, copy it once, then join the second repetition.

Learning activities
  1. Echo the line — Teacher says one short line with a steady beat. Children echo the same words, pace and actions.
    Teacher speech bubble followed by a matching child echo bubble
  2. Rhyme ears — Say three words aloud. Children point to the two words with the same ending sound, even if the spelling is not shown.
    Three picture cards: moon, spoon and fish
  3. Action conductor — A child chooses tap, clap or stamp cards to match a repeated chant, then leads the class through two repetitions.
    Ordered action cards that repeat as one pattern
Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Use music and actions to feel literary rhythm

Movement and simple music can make the rhythm and sound pattern of a rhyme, poem, chant or song easier to notice.

Teacher: Listen once without moving. Add one steady tap or sway. Repeat the literary line with the action.

Ask: Which movement helps you keep the beat, and when does it repeat?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: coordinates an action with the modelled beat or sound pattern.

Look for: The learner coordinates an action with the modelled beat or sound pattern.

If incorrect: Remove the words, rehearse the action pattern slowly, then add the words again.

E2: Recite a rhyme with actions

Reciting means saying a known rhyme aloud so its wording, rhythm and actions can be shared with others.

Teacher: Teacher says and acts one short part. Children echo that part. Join the parts and perform the whole rhyme.

Ask: Can you copy my words, beat and action in the same order?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: recites the modelled section and coordinates the agreed actions with it.

Look for: The learner recites the modelled section and coordinates the agreed actions with it.

If incorrect: Reduce the echo to two or three words and one action before building back up.

E3: Explore rhythm in an attributed First Nations Australian poem

A school-approved poem by a First Nations Australian creator can be heard and appreciated through the rhythm present in that particular work.

Teacher: Introduce the attributed poem and context supplied by its source. Listen for an approved repeated rhythm or phrase. Respond by tapping or describing that feature as the source permits.

Ask: Where do you hear the rhythm return in this poem?

Students: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: locates an audible rhythmic feature in the selected poem and refers respectfully to the named work.

Look for: The learner locates an audible rhythmic feature in the selected poem and refers respectfully to the named work.

If incorrect: Replay one approved short section and offer two possible tap patterns.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Rhythm and rhyme mean the same thing — Tap the steady beat while saying two non-rhyming words, then compare the beat with the matching end sounds in a rhyme.
  • Copying the words but losing the beat — Slow the line, keep a quiet four-beat tap and add the words back one small part at a time.
  • Words rhyme only when their spellings look the same — Hide the written words and listen to the spoken endings, such as blue and you.
Quick checks and mastery

Quick checks

  • Which sound matches at the end of three and me?
  • Can you tap the beat while saying the first chant line?
  • What action comes after the two hop claps in the bird chant?
  • How can your ears decide whether blue and you rhyme?
