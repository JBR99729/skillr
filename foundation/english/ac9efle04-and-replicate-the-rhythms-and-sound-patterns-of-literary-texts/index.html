FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Listen, echo and play with literary sounds AC9EFLE04

Learning intention: I can hear and copy a rhythm or repeated sound in a short poem, rhyme or chant. I can join in in a comfortable way.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep a steady pulse; notice the words’ rhythm

An adult can say the supplied original rhyme slowly while tapping four even beats per line. The beat is the steady pulse; the spoken rhythm includes longer and shorter sounds and pauses. Three and me rhyme by sound. Copy a small part first, then join the two lines. A printed pattern is a reminder for an adult model, not recorded audio.

Learn the skill

1. Listen and echo a small pattern

An adult models “Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop.” Listen to the whole unit, then echo its words, timing and pause. Begin slowly and repeat the same unit. A comfortable tap, sway or AAC rhythm control can accompany the model.

Original chant: “Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop. Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop.”

Try it · Which words repeat as the whole unit?

Red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop repeats. The learner can hear the adult’s model and echo it; identifying the printed unit alone does not establish a rhythm performance.

2. Hear rhyme and repeated sounds

Rhyme concerns the spoken ending; repeated beginning sounds are another sound pattern. Say the words aloud. Letter appearance alone cannot settle whether they rhyme.

Say moon, spoon, fish. Then say “Busy bees buzz.”

Try it · Which two words rhyme, and which beginning sound repeats in the phrase?

Moon and spoon rhyme. The /b/ sound repeats in busy, bees and buzz. The spoken sound is the clue.

3. Join words with an agreed action or pause

Choose actions that help you follow the model, rather than race. Compare a steady pulse with a pattern that includes a pause. Adapt movement to the learner. There is no need to stand, stamp or clap loudly.

Adult models clap, clap, pause; clap, clap, pause. The pause lasts one agreed pulse.

Try it · What needs to stay in place when you copy it?

The two claps and the pause, in that order and timing. Use quiet taps or another comfortable signal. Removing the pause changes this pattern.

Common mistakes

“Rhythm and rhyme are the same.” Rhyme is a matching ending sound; rhythm is how sounds and pauses move through time.

“Going faster makes it better.” Keep the modelled order and pace first.

“Matching spelling proves a rhyme.” Listen to the endings. Blue and you can rhyme despite different spellings.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Echo, join, change one part

An adult models the original rhyme and chant. Echo a short part, then join a full repetition. Try a new quiet action while keeping the same words and timing. Use the separately prepared named poem activity to explore one First Nations poet’s work.

Adjust the challenge Starter: echo two or three words. Core: copy a complete short unit with its pause. Stretch: offer a new line ending with a fitting rhyming word and try it against the same modelled pace.

Adult: read and model when needed. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing or a drawing with explanation. Use short turns. A page answer does not prove an actual conversation, reading performance or physical action.

Three tricky questions

1. Do blue and you need the same spelling to rhyme?

No. Their spoken ending sounds match.

2. If I say the right words but miss the agreed pause, have I copied the whole pattern?

Not yet. The pause is part of this model. Slow down and echo it again.

3. Can I hear a rhythm in a poem without rhyming words?

Yes. Rhythm can come from timing, repeated phrases and stressed sounds without matching line endings.

FAQ

Does the page play a recording?

No. An adult supplies the live spoken model. Reading a transcript is different from hearing or performing its rhythm.

Must every child use the same body movement?

No. Choose an accessible way to join in, and observe the intended sound or timing rather than a particular movement.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • rhythm — the pattern of long, short, strong and soft parts we hear in words or music
  • beat — the steady pulse we can tap while a rhyme, poem or song continues
  • rhyme — words that have the same or very similar ending sound
  • sound pattern — sounds that repeat or are arranged in a noticeable way
  • chant — words spoken together with a strong repeated rhythm
  • action — a body movement used to show or support a rhythm
Key concept

I can hear, copy and make rhythm and sound patterns in poems, rhymes and songs.

Learning goal: I can hear, copy and make rhythm and sound patterns in poems, rhymes and songs.

I am ready when I can

  • I can keep a steady beat while listening to a short literary line.
  • I can identify two spoken words that rhyme.
  • I can copy a short rhythm with my voice and body.
  • I can repeat words, sounds and actions in the modelled order.
  • I can describe the difference between a steady beat and a matching end sound.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Use music and actions to feel literary rhythm

Movement and simple music can make the rhythm and sound pattern of a rhyme, poem, chant or song easier to notice.

Think: Listen once without moving. Add one steady tap or sway. Repeat the literary line with the action.

Try: Which movement helps you keep the beat, and when does it repeat?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: coordinates an action with the modelled beat or sound pattern.

Check: The learner coordinates an action with the modelled beat or sound pattern.

If you are stuck: Remove the words, rehearse the action pattern slowly, then add the words again.

E2: Recite a rhyme with actions

Reciting means saying a known rhyme aloud so its wording, rhythm and actions can be shared with others.

Think: Teacher says and acts one short part. Children echo that part. Join the parts and perform the whole rhyme.

Try: Can you copy my words, beat and action in the same order?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: recites the modelled section and coordinates the agreed actions with it.

Check: The learner recites the modelled section and coordinates the agreed actions with it.

If you are stuck: Reduce the echo to two or three words and one action before building back up.

E3: Explore rhythm in an attributed First Nations Australian poem

A school-approved poem by a First Nations Australian creator can be heard and appreciated through the rhythm present in that particular work.

Think: Introduce the attributed poem and context supplied by its source. Listen for an approved repeated rhythm or phrase. Respond by tapping or describing that feature as the source permits.

Try: Where do you hear the rhythm return in this poem?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and show this evidence in your own example: locates an audible rhythmic feature in the selected poem and refers respectfully to the named work.

Check: The learner locates an audible rhythmic feature in the selected poem and refers respectfully to the named work.

If you are stuck: Replay one approved short section and offer two possible tap patterns.

Common misconceptions
  • Rhythm and rhyme mean the same thing — Tap the steady beat while saying two non-rhyming words, then compare the beat with the matching end sounds in a rhyme.
  • Copying the words but losing the beat — Slow the line, keep a quiet four-beat tap and add the words back one small part at a time.
  • Words rhyme only when their spellings look the same — Hide the written words and listen to the spoken endings, such as blue and you.
Explain and apply

Listen for rhyme

Say moon, spoon and fish. Which two words have the same spoken ending?

Answer and teaching guidance

Rhyme is heard at the end of words. Do not judge by the last letter alone; say the words aloud.

Keep the beat

Tap a steady beat while saying a short rhyme or chant. What stays even under the words?

Answer and teaching guidance

The beat is the pulse. Learners can tap, clap or step so the rhythm is physical as well as spoken.

Copy the pattern

Repeat: red bird, blue bird, hop-hop-stop. Use the same action for each repeated part.

Answer and teaching guidance

The voice pattern and action pattern should match. If a word changes, the action can change in the same position.

Add one matching line

Add a new line that keeps the rhythm and ends with a rhyming word.

Answer and teaching guidance

Support: give two rhyming choices. Extend: ask the learner to explain why the final word rhymes by sound.
