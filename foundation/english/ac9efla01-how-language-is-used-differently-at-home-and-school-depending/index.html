FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Choose words for the person you are talking to AC9EFLA01

Learning intention: I can notice who is listening and choose a clear message that fits. Families and friends may use different words. More than one choice can work.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the need; think about the listener

Ari needs help opening a paint jar. The listener is named on each card. With a friend, Ari could say, “Can you help me open this?” With teacher Ms Lee, Ari could say, “Ms Lee, can you help me open this jar?” Both requests say what help is needed. The same clear words could also fit both listeners; adding a name can help get attention.

Learn the skill

1. Notice who is talking and listening

Start with the people in the situation. A relationship tells us how they know each other. Words such as please can be used with many people, so they do not prove who the listener is.

Tess tells her cousin, “Our cat has a new bed.”

Try it · Who is receiving Tess’s message?

Her cousin is listening. The situation tells us the relationship; we do not have to guess it from the words.

2. Choose a message for your purpose

A question can ask for information. A request can ask for help or an action. An opinion tells what someone thinks. Start with what you need the listener to understand.

The class cannot find the paint pots. Choices: “Where are the paint pots?” / “Please pass the paint pot.” / “I like blue paint.”

Try it · Which message asks for the missing information?

“Where are the paint pots?” asks where they are. The request asks for an action; the opinion shares a view. A request can also be phrased as a question, so consider what it is asking the listener to do.

3. Try the message with another listener

Keep the need the same while the listener changes. You might add a name, greeting or more detail. Do not make the message longer just to sound better. Listen to the reply.

You want to borrow a pencil. First ask a friend. Then ask a shopkeeper who does not know which pencil you mean.

Try it · What could you say each time?

For example: “Can I borrow your pencil?” and “Excuse me, could I borrow this pencil?” Accept other clear, respectful choices, including the same fitting request for both. The second listener may need more detail about which pencil.

Common mistakes

“Please tells me who is listening.” It can be used with a friend, teacher or another person. Read the role clue.

“Formal words are always better.” Choose words that are clear for this listener and setting. Home languages and dialects are not inferior.

“I must change every word.” Keep the purpose. Sometimes the same message fits both people.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One need, two listeners

Choose a pretend need: finding a hat or asking for a turn. Name two listeners. Say or show one clear message for each. Ask your partner what stayed the same and what changed.

Adjust the challenge Support: give two named role cards and model one message. Core: keep one purpose with two listeners. Extend: explain when the same words could fit both.

Adult: read the words aloud when needed. Accept speech, pointing, sign, AAC or a drawing with explanation. Split the activity into short turns. A page response does not show that a child has independently performed a real conversation or read a whole book.

Three tricky questions

1. A child says “Please help.” Can we tell whether the listener is a parent or a teacher?

No. Either could hear those words. We need a clue about the people or setting.

2. Your friend says, “I like red.” You like green. Must you agree?

No. You can say, “I like green.” Different preferences can be shared respectfully.

3. Is “Can you open this?” only a question asking for information?

In this situation it asks someone to open something, so it functions as a request. Meaning and context matter.

FAQ

Must a child use may instead of can?

No. Several expressions can make a clear, respectful request. Model choices without making one formula compulsory.

What should an adult notice?

Notice whether the child identifies the listener and communicates the intended need. Do not score accent, eye contact, handwriting or one preferred speaking style.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Key vocabulary
  • relationship — how people are connected, such as friends, family members or teacher and student
  • context — the place, people and situation around the talk
  • purpose — the reason we are speaking, such as asking, greeting or inviting
  • respectful — showing care for the other person through words, voice and listening
  • choice — one possible way to say something
Key concept

I can notice who I am talking with, where we are and why I am speaking, then choose words that fit. Families and communities may use different words. More than one choice can be kind, clear and correct for its setting.

Learning goal: I can choose words that fit who I am talking to.

I am ready when I can

  • I can name who is speaking and who is listening.
  • I can ask for the same thing with a named friend and teacher, using words that fit each situation.
  • I can explain what changed and what stayed the same.
  • I can match a phrase to a friend, teacher or shopkeeper.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Ask, request and share an opinion in a fitting way

A useful question, request or opinion matches what is happening and gives the listener enough information to respond.

Think: Use one familiar classroom event and model the difference between asking for information, requesting an action and sharing an opinion. Notice what is happening and what you need to communicate. Choose a question, request or opinion frame. Say it clearly, then listen for the response.

Try: You cannot find the paint pots. What relevant question could you ask in this classroom?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: gives a clear message that fits the classroom event and can name whether it is a question, request or opinion.

Check: The learner gives a clear message that fits the classroom event and can name whether it is a question, request or opinion.

If you are stuck: Offer three oral models: Where is the item? May I use the item? I think this choice fits because it is clear; let the learner adapt one.

E2: Choose language for the relationship

People may express the same purpose in different respectful ways because their relationships and shared experiences are different.

Think: Keep the purpose fixed, change the relationship card and rehearse language that is natural, kind and clear for that pair without treating one community's way as better. Name the two people and how they are connected. Keep the purpose the same. Try words, voice and listening that suit this relationship.

Try: You want to see an item. How might you ask a sibling or friend, and how might you ask a shopkeeper?

A strong answer: Complete the modelled response and use the example to show: changes at least one language feature for the relationship and explains that both versions can be respectful in their settings.

Check: The learner changes at least one language feature for the relationship and explains that both versions can be respectful in their settings.

If you are stuck: Role-play with two labelled puppets and model a complete response, such as: With my friend I could say, Can I see your new card?

Common misconceptions
  • One sentence must fit everyone — Show friend and teacher scenes side by side. Circle the same purpose and highlight the words that change.
  • Longer words are always better — Say, A short friendly request can fit a friend. The best choice fits the listener and purpose.
  • One way of speaking is wrong — Explain that people and families speak in many correct ways. Today we are choosing words for a particular listener and place.

Notice who is listening

A child needs help opening a paint jar. How would the words change for a teacher, friend or visiting adult?

Answer and teaching guidance

A strong answer keeps the same purpose but adjusts the words to fit the relationship and place.

Keep it respectful

Change Move! into words that could be used with a librarian.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example: Excuse me, may I please get past? The request stays clear and becomes more suitable for the listener.

Compare home and school talk

Name one phrase you might use at home and one you might use at school for asking for help.

Answer and teaching guidance

Both can be correct. The learner explains why each fits its context rather than ranking one as better.

Choose for purpose

Complete: I chose these words because I was talking to ____ and I needed ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

Support: offer person and purpose cards. Extend: ask how the words would change for a different listener.
