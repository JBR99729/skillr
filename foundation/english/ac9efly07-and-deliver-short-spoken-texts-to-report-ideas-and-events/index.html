FOUNDATION ENGLISH

Share a short spoken report AC9EFLY07

Learning intention: I can choose one topic, put my ideas in order and share them so my listeners can follow.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One discovery, two helpful details

Kai reports a discovery: “Our bean seed changed. First, a white root appeared. Next, two green leaves opened.” The first sentence names the topic. Both details explain the same discovery, and first and next show the order. Kai rehearses with a partner, faces the group and uses a voice they can hear comfortably.

Learn the skill

1. Choose a topic and relevant details

A report tells listeners about an idea or event. Name one topic first, then select details that explain it. A true fact can still be unrelated to this report.

Topic: our classroom snail. Possible details: it left a shiny trail; it has a shell; my shoes are blue.

Try it · Which two details help your classmates learn about the snail?

The shiny trail and shell describe the snail. Blue shoes do not explain this topic. An opening could be “We watched our classroom snail.”

2. Put the ideas in order

Use first and next when events have an order. A small prompt can remind you of an idea without containing every word. Tell a connected sentence for each prompt.

Adult: draw three simple cards now: an empty pot, a hand planting a seed, a pot with a shoot. Display them in that order. Say the names of the pictures together.

Try it · Use the three pictures to tell what happened.

For example: “First we had an empty pot. Next we planted a seed. Later a shoot grew.” Accept another accurate connected report. The actual drawings are memory cues; the child supplies the spoken sentences.

3. Rehearse for your listeners

Try the report with one person. Ask whether they heard it and understood the order. Face the group and adjust your volume for the room. Clear does not mean shouting.

Mina whispers her report and a partner cannot hear the last detail. The topic and order are clear.

Try it · What could Mina change before sharing with a small group?

Rehearse the final detail with a comfortably stronger voice while facing the listeners. Pause between ideas and check whether the group can hear. Keep the same relevant ideas.

Common mistakes

“A report needs every fact I know.” Choose details that explain the one topic.

“Louder is always clearer.” Use a comfortable volume that reaches the listeners.

“A prompt must show every word.” A small picture can remind you of an idea; say the sentence yourself.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

A discovery to share

Choose something you really noticed today. Name the topic, draw two detail prompts and put them in order. Rehearse with an adult, then share with a willing partner or small group. Ask what they learnt from your report.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose between two relevant details with an adult. Core: give an opening and two ordered details. Stretch: answer one on-topic listener question and add a useful detail.

Use speech, sign or AAC with suitable support. Record topic and order separately from actual voice volume; a written answer cannot demonstrate spoken delivery. Do not require a child to share private experiences.

Three tricky questions

1. “My seed grew. I like chips.” Is this a focused report?

The seed sentence fits the topic; chips do not explain the seed. Add a detail such as its new leaves.

2. Can a report share a response to a story?

Yes. First supply the text event: “Niko found his lost boot and could go home.” A report might be “I felt relieved when Niko found his boot because he could go home.” The reason connects the response to that event.

3. Does reading a script show the same skill as using picture prompts?

It can support sharing, but it does not show that the speaker can expand picture prompts into their own connected report. Notice the support used.

FAQ

How long should a report be?

Use a short, manageable turn with one topic and a few relevant ideas. There is no compulsory time limit here.

What should an adult notice?

Listen for a clear topic, relevant ordered details and communication that the actual audience can follow. Check voice volume through a real speaking turn where appropriate.

Key vocabulary
  • spoken report — a short talk that shares ideas, information or events
  • topic — what the whole report is about
  • detail — a piece of information that explains the topic
  • sequence — an order that helps ideas make sense
  • rehearse — practise before presenting
  • volume — how loud or quiet a voice is
Key concept

I can give a short spoken report about an idea or event using a voice my listeners can hear.

Learning goal: I can give a short spoken report about an idea or event using a voice my listeners can hear.

  • I can order ideas.
  • I can use a prompt without reading a script.
  • I can speak clearly at suitable volume.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Share a personal discovery with peers

A semi-formal peer report names one experience, interest or discovery and explains it with relevant details in a clear voice.

Think: Choose the discovery: a bean seed has sprouted. Plan an opening topic and two observable details. Rehearse, face the group and speak at a comfortable classroom volume.

Try: What did you discover, and which two details will help your peers understand it?

A strong answer: Choose one personal discovery, order two relevant details, rehearse the report and deliver it clearly to peers at a suitable volume.

Check: The learner states one topic, gives relevant details and delivers the report clearly to peers.

If you are stuck: Use three picture prompts for topic, detail one and detail two, then rehearse first with one partner.

E2: Share a personal response to a text

A spoken response reports what the learner thought or felt about a text idea or event and gives a story clue.

Think: Name the character action or event. State a thought or feeling about it. Explain the text clue and deliver the response to peers.

Try: What did you think or feel, and which event made you respond that way?

A strong answer: Name the text event, state a connected feeling or thought, give the text-based reason and share the complete response aloud.

Check: The learner shares a personal response and supports it with an accurate event, word or image clue from the text.

If you are stuck: Offer feeling pictures and model a complete response: 'I felt relieved when the boot was found because Niko could go home.'

E3: Use visual prompts to organise a report

A small sequence of visual prompts can help a speaker stay on one topic and recall ideas in order without reading a full script.

Think: Arrange three seed pictures from planting to sprouting. Say one connected sentence for each picture. Check that every sentence explains the report topic and that the order makes sense.

Try: Which picture comes next, and what sentence will you say without reading a script?

A strong answer: Order three visual prompts, point to each in turn and speak one connected report detail before moving to the next prompt.

Check: The learner follows the visual sequence, stays on topic and expands each prompt with a relevant spoken sentence.

If you are stuck: Fix the first and final pictures, rehearse one sentence at a time and let the learner point while speaking.

Common misconceptions
  • A report is a list of unrelated facts — State the topic first and keep only details that explain that topic.
  • Speaking quickly makes a report sound prepared — Pause between ideas and rehearse at a pace listeners can follow.
  • Visual prompts should contain every spoken word — Use one simple picture for each main idea and speak the details aloud.
Explain and apply

Choose one report topic

Pick one event or idea to report, such as how a bean seed changed. What is your opening sentence?

Answer and teaching guidance

The opening should name the topic so listeners know what the report is about.

Order two details

Use two picture prompts to tell what happened first and next.

Answer and teaching guidance

Picture prompts keep the report in order and reduce memory load while preserving the speaking skill.

Use a voice for the audience

Say the same report to a partner, group and whole class. What changes about your voice?

Answer and teaching guidance

Volume and pace should fit the audience. The report should be audible without shouting.

Rehearse and repair

If the report is rushed or hard to hear, choose two rehearsal changes.

Answer and teaching guidance

Useful changes include slowing down, pausing between details, facing listeners and using a clearer volume.

Quick checks

  • Which opening suits a short report to classmates?
  • You are speaking to the whole class. Which volume is best?
  • Which order makes a personal event easier to follow?

Complete check prompts and response guidance

Complete the checks with these supplied alternatives. Caterpillar topic: “its green body and tiny legs” / “my lunch”. Opening: “I found a caterpillar” / “I forgot my shoes”. Class volume: “comfortably audible” / “a whisper nobody hears” / “a shout beside someone’s ear”. Event order: “found it, looked closely, told the class” / “told the class, then found it yesterday”. The first option in each pair or set fits the given purpose. For open personal reports, accept accurate relevant alternatives and observe real delivery.
