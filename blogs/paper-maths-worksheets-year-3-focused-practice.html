Why Paper Maths Worksheets Still Matter in Year 3

A pencil, a clear question and a little time to think: sometimes a simple learning setup is exactly what a child needs.

By SkillrHub • 7 September 2026

Multiplication, division and fractions deserve regular attention. These skills appear when children share food, count equal groups, read recipes and later work with more complex numbers. A well-chosen worksheet gives them somewhere to draw, calculate, make a mistake and explain a better answer.

At SkillrHub, we see printable practice as a useful part of a lesson. Combine it with a clear explanation, conversation and feedback, and a few questions can become a productive learning session at home or in the classroom.

A quieter space to concentrate

Once a worksheet is printed and the device is put aside, there are no on-page notifications, extra browser tabs or buttons competing for attention. A child can keep the question, diagram and working visible together, and return to an earlier step without changing screens.

That setup can help adults create a focused practice routine. Paper alone cannot guarantee concentration: a crowded page, an unclear task or work that is too difficult can still get in the way. Choose a manageable section, clear a little desk space and tell the learner what they are practising.

The teaching matters as much as the worksheet

Good pedagogy means making thoughtful choices about how a child learns. For worksheet practice, that includes choosing the right starting point, showing a useful example and responding to the child's reasoning.

The Education Endowment Foundation's maths guidance recommends purposeful representations, worked examples and assessment that informs teaching. It also emphasises clear feedback and connections between mathematical ideas. These principles can guide how adults use a worksheet; the report does not evaluate SkillrHub products. See the EEF summary of recommendations.

Look for questions that ask children to represent an idea, calculate and explain. If a child is unsure what an equal group means, bring out counters before expecting independent written answers. If the idea is secure, a short set of calculations and a new problem can help you check how they apply it.

Try this: connect multiplication and division on paper

Four rows, five counters in each row

Ask the child to draw four rows of five dots. Together, connect the drawing to 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20 and 4 × 5 = 20.

Then ask: “If we share these 20 counters equally into four groups, how many go in each group?” Write 20 ÷ 4 = 5. Ask the child to point to what each number represents.

For an independent check, try three rows of five. The related equations are 3 × 5 = 15 and 15 ÷ 3 = 5.

The marks on the page give you something specific to discuss. An answer of 9 to 4 × 5 may suggest the child added the two numbers. Ask them to show four groups of five before deciding what help they need. An uneven drawing may call for a conversation about equal groups.

A tick tells a child that an answer is correct. “Your four equal rows show why the total is 20” connects the answer to the thinking behind it.

Use teaching slides, then let students do the thinking

A presentation can introduce a visual model and give everyone a shared example to discuss. After modelling a question, work through a similar one together. Then put the example aside and let students attempt a few worksheet questions independently.

A practical 10–15 minute routine

Use this as a flexible starting point, adjusting the time and number of questions to the learner:

  1. Choose one goal. For example, “I can connect the 4 times table to equal groups.”
  2. Model one question. Draw it and explain your steps aloud.
  3. Try one together. Ask the child to supply the next step.
  4. Practise independently. Select a few suitable questions rather than assigning the entire workbook.
  5. Review and revisit. Discuss an error, try a fresh example and return to the skill in a later session.

If the child is stuck, pause and use objects or another explanation. If the work is easy, ask for a reason, a related division fact or a story problem of their own. Finishing a page is useful only when you also learn something about their understanding.
