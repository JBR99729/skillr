First grade math worksheets and free practice: a guide for US families

Choosing a math worksheet for 1st grade is easier when you know the exact skill your child needs. A child might confidently count objects but still reverse two-digit numbers, lose the count after 99, or restart from one for every addition. Those are different learning needs. This guide shows how to choose a useful starting point, teach with simple materials, and connect paper work with SkillrHub's available free digital practice.

SkillrHub is built primarily for Australian Curriculum V9.0. US families can use its resources for supplementary learning by matching the topic and difficulty. Australian year labels stay visible, and selected US Common Core references help with discovery. They describe related support, not a completed alignment review or a replacement for your school's program.

Start with the skill, then choose the worksheet

Ask your child to count a small collection, read a few written numbers, show a number with objects, and explain a simple sum. Keep this informal. You are finding a sensible starting point rather than giving a surprise test. Notice whether the difficulty comes from understanding the mathematics, reading the directions, keeping track of objects, or writing the answer.

For example, a child who says “forty-seven” for 47 but builds seven groups of ten needs place-value support. More numeral-reading questions alone will miss that problem. A child who builds 47 correctly but writes 74 may benefit from saying “four tens, seven ones” while checking each digit. Choose a worksheet that makes the relevant thinking visible.

Counting to 120: look beyond reciting the sequence

Start a counting sequence at an unfamiliar point, such as 86, rather than always beginning at one. Ask for the next six numbers: 87, 88, 89, 90, 91 and 92. If the transition through 89 is difficult, use a number chart or a row of written numbers and say each number while pointing. Repeat with a different starting number after the pattern makes sense.

Connect the spoken sequence to an amount. Put 18 counters on the table, move each counted counter into a second group, and write 18 beside the collection. Rearrange the same counters without adding or removing any. Ask whether the amount changed. This distinguishes counting words, keeping track, and understanding that the final number names the whole collection.

A useful paper task might ask a learner to complete 97, 98, __, 100, __, 102. The missing numbers are 99 and 101. Invite the child to explain what happens after 99. If the sequence is copied without understanding, return to a chart and compare the written numbers with spoken counting.

Tens and ones: make the digits mean something

Use bundles of ten sticks, linking cubes, or drawn groups. Show 34 as three tens and four ones. Say the full sentence: “Three groups of ten make thirty, and four more make thirty-four.” Then place a written 34 beside the model. The first digit counts tens; the second counts the remaining ones.

Contrast 34 and 43 using two models. They contain the same digits but represent different amounts. Avoid reducing the explanation to “swap the numbers.” Ask how many tens each model contains. A learner who can explain the difference is more likely to use place value when comparing numbers or calculating later.

For a quick worksheet, ask your child to draw 26, write the number shown by five tens and two ones, and explain why 40 has a zero in the ones place. The answers are two tens and six ones, 52, and no additional ones beyond the four tens. Use complete bundles so the picture reinforces the meaning.

Addition within 20: choose a strategy the child can explain

For 8 + 5, place eight counters in a ten-frame and keep five beside it. Move two counters to complete the ten. Three remain, so the total is 10 + 3 = 13. Say what moved and why. The written calculation should follow the model rather than appearing as an unexplained shortcut.

Another child may count on from eight: nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen. That can be a useful starting method. When the child is secure, compare counting on with making ten and discuss which feels easier for this example. You do not need to demand several strategies in every question. First make one method understandable and reliable.

Include the related subtraction 13 − 5 = 8. A story about thirteen buttons, five placed in a box, and eight left on the table connects the calculation to an amount. If a child adds instead, ask them to model the action before choosing a sign. A correct answer copied from a nearby addition fact gives less information than an explained model.

A short practice sheet you can use today

Write these six prompts on paper, leaving room for counters or drawings: continue 58, 59, __, __; draw 42 using tens and ones; write the number made by six tens and three ones; solve 9 + 4; solve 15 − 6; and explain how you checked one answer. The numerical answers are 60 and 61, 42, 63, 13 and 9.

Use the final explanation to decide what comes next. If the child can solve 9 + 4 by making ten but cannot represent 42, keep the next session focused on place value. These prompts are an informal teaching check. They do not assess every part of first grade math or establish that a standard has been mastered.

Use a simple weekly sequence and review what changed

One manageable plan is to use the first session to model a skill, the second for a small amount of paper practice, the third for matching digital questions, and a later session for a fresh example. Keep the focus stable long enough to understand it. Revisit an earlier idea after a break instead of using every session to introduce new content.

Record a specific observation such as “built 52 using five tens and two ones without help.” Later, try 67 and see whether the same understanding transfers. Saved digital progress can support this discussion, but a score alone does not explain which representation or strategy the child understands. Use their words, work and feedback together.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Australian year label place my child in a US grade?

No. Choose by the learning goal, examples and difficulty. Keep your school's current expectations as the reference for grade placement and coverage.

Are the Common Core labels complete alignment claims?

No. They identify selected related support. Scope differs, and the current references do not certify an entire standard, grade or state curriculum.

Do I need to purchase a pack to use practice?

No. Available digital practice and public teaching guides are free. Optional paid printable packs are separate.
