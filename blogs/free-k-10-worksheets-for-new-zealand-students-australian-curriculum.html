Maths practice and worksheets for New Zealand learners

Good maths practice starts with an idea a learner can understand, represent and use again. A page of answers may show that work was completed, but a drawing, explanation or fresh example can reveal much more. This guide helps New Zealand families and whānau choose a useful SkillrHub topic, connect it with a short home activity, and decide what to practise next.

SkillrHub is primarily built for Australian Curriculum V9.0. New Zealand learners are welcome to use suitable teaching guides and available practice as supplementary resources. Australian year numbers remain visible, and matching year labels do not establish matching curriculum coverage. Start with the topic, examples and your learner's current school goal.

Use the current New Zealand learning goal as your starting point

The official New Zealand Mathematics and Statistics Years 0–10 curriculum in effect from January 2026 includes year-by-year teaching sequences. Use the relevant school guidance and official curriculum to identify the goal you want to support. An older overseas label or a familiar year number is less useful than knowing what the learner should be able to do.

Write the goal as an action: represent a two-digit number, explain equal parts, read a clock, or work out an interval. Ask the learner to show a small example before selecting a resource. That starting example can reveal a missing prerequisite and save time searching through worksheets that are either too easy or too demanding.

Choose by readiness when Australian and New Zealand years differ

Suppose your learner understands whole-number place value but finds a particular fraction model unfamiliar. You may need a clear explanation of that model rather than an entire earlier-year workbook. Open a topic guide, inspect the representation, and try one example together. Then decide whether the linked practice is suitable.

A search for “Year 5 maths” can lead to resources from several school systems. On SkillrHub, that label refers to an Australian year. Treat it as a browsing aid. Read the actual learning intention and question demands before using the resource for a New Zealand learner. Some relevant ideas may be spread across more than one Australian year.

If a task includes a context or word your child does not know, clarify it before assessing the mathematics. Read the direction aloud when helpful, but let the child make the mathematical decision. You want to distinguish a vocabulary difficulty from uncertainty about the number, model or method being practised.

Build number understanding with materials, pictures and numerals

Use 48 as a starting example. Make four groups of ten objects and eight single objects, or draw four tens and eight ones. Write 48 beside the model and ask the learner to connect each digit to the amount it represents. Saying “four tens and eight ones” makes the place-value structure explicit.

Then add one group of ten. There are now five tens and eight ones, so the number is 58. The ones have not changed. Compare that with adding one single object, which gives 49. Let the learner explain why the changes are different. A clear model helps prevent the idea that every added object simply changes the final digit.

The tens and ones guide is an Australian topic that may provide useful supplementary explanations. For counting and number representation, inspect numbers to 120. Check the full range and examples against the learner's current goal before opening a bank.

Use a number line to explain an addition

To work out 8 + 6, start at eight on a number line. Move two steps to reach ten, then four more to reach fourteen. The six has been split into two and four, so the total is 14. Connect the jumps to the written calculation rather than asking the learner to accept an unexplained answer.

A useful follow-up is 9 + 6. One step reaches ten and five more reach fifteen. Ask what stayed the same and what changed. Both examples use the same idea of completing a ten, while the starting number changes. This gives the learner a chance to use a familiar method with fresh numbers.

For subtraction, use 14 − 6 and explain how it relates to the first addition. Moving back six from fourteen reaches eight. The addition and subtraction within 20 guide can help you choose a model and a suitable practice route. A successful model is a starting point for understanding, not a reason to rush into harder calculations.

Fractions: make the whole and equal parts visible

Draw a rectangle and divide it into four equal parts. Shade three parts and name the shaded amount as three quarters of that rectangle. Ask the learner to explain what the four tells us and what the three tells us. The denominator describes the equal parts making the whole; the numerator counts the selected parts.

Draw another equal-sized rectangle and shade one half. Divide that rectangle into four equal parts without changing the shaded area. Two of the four parts are shaded, so one half is equivalent to two quarters of the same whole. Use the picture to explain the relationship before moving to written equivalent fractions.

A shape cut into four unequal pieces does not provide four quarters merely because there are four pieces. Invite the learner to compare the areas and explain the problem. The halves, quarters and eighths guide provides a topic starting point; check whether its examples fit the current New Zealand lesson.

Practise time through an everyday interval

Suppose an activity starts at 10:45 and finishes at 11:15. Draw a timeline with 10:45, 11:00 and 11:15 marked. The first interval is 15 minutes and the second is 15 minutes, so the whole activity lasts 30 minutes. Ask the learner to explain why crossing an hour boundary does not mean the activity lasts an hour.

Keep clock reading and duration distinct. A learner may read 10:45 correctly but still need support calculating an interval. Another may calculate with a written time while finding the hand positions unfamiliar. The clock reading and duration guide links the relevant Australian topics so you can inspect the appropriate skill.

Create a short home-learning sequence

Choose one focus for the week rather than trying to cover several strands in every session. Begin with materials or a drawing. Work through one example together, then let the learner try a fresh example. Use suitable digital questions for another opportunity to apply the idea, and revisit it after a break.

For place value, the first session might build 48 and 58. A later session can ask the child to represent 63 and explain what happens when one ten is added. For fractions, begin with equal parts and return later to compare two representations of the same amount. Keep the connection between sessions clear.

Use manageable tasks and adjust the amount of work to the learner. A short session that reveals a specific misconception is useful. If attention or reading becomes the main obstacle, pause and return to a clearer example. The goal is to understand and use the idea, rather than completing a fixed number of pages.

A paper activity with answers you can discuss

Write four prompts on a sheet: represent 37 using tens and ones; calculate 9 + 5; shade three quarters of a rectangle divided into four equal parts; and find the duration from 1:50 to 2:10. The answers are three tens and seven ones, 14, three of the four equal parts, and 20 minutes.

Choose the prompts that match the learner's current goal. For the time question, a timeline can show ten minutes to 2:00 and ten more to 2:10. For the addition, one step from nine completes ten and four more give fourteen. Encourage a model or a spoken explanation, and record how much support was needed.

Keep a useful observation and choose the next step

A note such as “explained 37 as three tens and seven ones without a model” tells you more than a page-completion tick. Add the example used, any help given, and what to revisit. Look at saved digital progress alongside paper work and the learner's explanation. None of those records alone establishes completion of a local curriculum.

If a learner can explain the idea, try a fresh example later. If they can only follow your model, keep the same concept and reduce the support gradually. If the task exposes a missing prerequisite, choose an earlier topic that addresses it. This makes resource selection a response to learning rather than a race through year labels.
