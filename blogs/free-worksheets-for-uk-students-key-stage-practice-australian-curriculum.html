Maths worksheets and free practice for UK primary learners

A search for maths worksheets can return hundreds of pages, yet the useful next step is usually small: understand one idea, see a worked example, and try it independently. For UK families and tutors, the challenge is choosing a resource that fits the current school lesson. This guide explains how to use SkillrHub's Australian Curriculum topic pages for supplementary learning, with practical number, calculation and time examples.

SkillrHub remains primarily designed for Australian Curriculum V9.0. Its year labels are Australian, and they do not automatically match the school year or curriculum used by a UK learner. Read the learning goal, number range and examples before choosing a worksheet or practice bank. Available digital practice and public teaching guides are free; optional printable packs are sold separately.

Check which curriculum and lesson you are supporting

Use your child's school plan as the starting point. The Department for Education's mathematics programmes linked below apply to England. Do not treat those documents, or an Australian topic label, as a single curriculum for every UK school. A useful conversation with the teacher might be: “Are we practising counting, representing tens and ones, or calculating with two-digit numbers?”

Even within a topic, the expected numbers and methods matter. A learner comparing numbers to 100 needs a different selection from one calculating with decimals. A child using counters to understand subtraction may not yet benefit from a page of written calculations. Match the intended thinking before considering whether the worksheet looks easy or difficult.

Choosing Year 1 maths worksheets: compare the actual range

The phrase “Year 1 maths worksheets” is useful for finding resources, but it does not make every result equivalent. England's Year 1 programme includes counting across 100 and early number representation. SkillrHub's Australian Year 1 numbers topic extends to 120. The shared ideas may be useful, while the complete range of questions can differ.

Open the numbers to 120 guide and check its examples against the lesson you need. If the learner is working within 100, use a suitable explanation or original paper task in that range. A digital bank may include additional numbers. Selecting a country reference does not automatically restrict every question to a local year-level limit.

Try a small starting check: ask for the number after 69, the number before 80, and the missing numbers in 46, 47, __, __, 50. The answers are 70, 79, 48 and 49. If an hour of worksheets would only repeat an uncertain counting pattern, begin with a chart and a short explanation instead.

Teach place value with a model the learner can check

Show 56 as five bundles of ten and six single objects. Write the number next to the model and connect each digit with its meaning. Ask the learner to make 65 alongside it. The two numbers use the same digits, but the amounts differ because the tens and ones have different values.

A helpful follow-up is to ask what changes when you add one more ten to 56. There are now six tens and six ones, so the number is 66. You have added ten, not one. If the learner says 57, return to the bundles and count the extra group. Let the model explain the difference.

The tens and ones topic offers a starting point for this idea. A suitable worksheet should connect pictures, spoken explanations and numerals. It should give enough space to show the thinking rather than requiring a long written explanation from a learner who can demonstrate the idea with objects.

Addition and subtraction: work through one complete example

For 7 + 6, begin with seven counters and six more. Move three from the second group to complete a ten. There are three left, so 10 + 3 = 13. Write 7 + 6 = 13 after the child has followed the model. Ask which part of the six was moved and why the total stayed the same.

Then use 13 − 6 with the same counters. Remove six and count the seven that remain. Connecting the two operations helps the learner see their relationship. Keep the story clear: adding more objects increases the collection; removing some decreases it. Avoid asking the child to memorise a rule about signs without understanding the action.

Read a worked example in the addition and subtraction within 20 guide, then offer one fresh calculation with the same method. For instance, 8 + 6 can become 10 + 4 = 14. This modest change lets you check whether the strategy transfers without introducing a different task at the same time.

Clock reading and duration need different practice

A worksheet showing a clock asks the learner to interpret a time. A question about how long an activity lasts asks for an interval. Those are connected skills, but success with one does not guarantee success with the other. Choose the appropriate section in the clock and duration resource guide rather than treating all time questions as interchangeable.

At 4:25, the minute hand points to the five because five groups of five minutes make 25 minutes. The hour hand should be part of the way from four to five. If it points exactly at four, the drawing suggests that the hour hand does not move continuously. Use a movable clock or a careful sketch to discuss this.

For duration, suppose a game begins at 4:25 and finishes at 4:50. The interval is 25 minutes. A timeline can show five-minute steps, or the learner can count from 25 minutes past to 50 minutes past. Only add an hour boundary when that simpler interval is understood. Keep the new demand visible.

A short session can include explanation, practice and review

Choose one goal, such as representing two-digit numbers. Begin with a model, work through an example together, and ask the learner to try a similar task. Allow them to point, draw or speak when that is enough to show the maths. Read the direction aloud if reading is not the skill you are assessing.

Finish by asking the child to check one answer. For 63, they might count six tens and three ones or compare the picture with the written digits. Later, use a different number to see whether they still understand. A short, focused session provides clearer information than a long page that mixes unfamiliar methods and many different topics.

Use a simple worksheet with purposeful variation

Write four tasks on a sheet: show 28 with tens and ones; write the number represented by four tens and seven ones; calculate 8 + 5; and find the time 15 minutes after 2:30. The answers are two tens and eight ones, 47, 13 and 2:45. Leave space for a model or a small timeline.

Use only the tasks that fit the learner's current lesson. If the goal is place value, keep the first two and add another similar example. If the goal is time, use the final task with two fresh starting times. A worksheet becomes more useful when its questions serve a clear teaching purpose, rather than covering unrelated skills to fill the page.

Inspect printable packs separately from free resources

Public teaching guides and available digital practice are free. Clearly labelled free worksheet links may also be provided. Longer printable packs are optional paid products with previews and checkout on Teachers Pay Teachers. Check what the listing includes before purchasing: the skills, question types, answer support and resource format matter more than the number of pages.

Keep a printed example and a brief note about the learner's method. “Used tens and ones to explain 56” is more informative than “finished page two.” Combine that observation with feedback and saved digital work when deciding what to revisit. This supports a learning conversation; it is not a claim that a child has completed a UK key stage or national curriculum.

Frequently asked questions

Does Australian Year 1 mean the same as Year 1 in England?

No automatic equivalence is claimed. Compare the specific learning goal, numbers and methods with the relevant school programme.

Can I use the resources for KS1 or KS2 revision?

You can use a suitable topic for supplementary practice. Inspect its scope first. SkillrHub does not currently claim complete alignment to either key stage.

Where can I check the official England requirements?

Use the Department for Education's mathematics programmes of study, alongside your school's plan. For a practical next step, open the Year 1 maths resource guide and choose one example that fits the learner's current goal.
