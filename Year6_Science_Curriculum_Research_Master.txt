AC9S6U01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Investigate habitat physical conditions and analyse how changing conditions affect growth and survival.
- Required boundary: the causal link is physical condition -> organism response/survival. Do not replace this with a generic “name an ecosystem” unit.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed in the current curriculum and existing curriculum index.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - L.3 “Describe ecosystems” presents a short habitat passage and multiple-select claims. The sampled Tallgrass Prairie item asked students to infer accurate ecosystem conditions from the text; only the nutrient-rich soil claim was supported.
  - Closest IXL supports: L.1 populations/communities/ecosystems; L.2 identify ecosystems; L.3 describe ecosystems; B.5 plant-growth protocol; B.2 independent/dependent variables.
  - IXL is partial rather than exact: use its evidence-reading and environment-feature formats, but add the ACARA-required changed-condition investigation.
- Khan Academy: ecology/community-structure resource coverage supports biodiversity, ecosystem interactions and disturbance effects; no direct AC9 code mapping.
- Additional trusted-source scan: ABC Education did not return a directly indexable, code-specific Year 6 lesson in its current public search results. NASA/CSIRO-style environmental evidence must be used only as contextual reading, not as a replacement for an age-appropriate fair investigation.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach “physical conditions” explicitly: light, temperature, water/moisture, salinity, soil/substrate, oxygen and shelter. Separate these from living factors such as predators or food.
- Use a controlled comparison: same seeds/soil/pot/period, deliberately vary one condition (for example light or salinity), record a measurable outcome, then make a qualified evidence claim.
- Practice coverage: identify physical versus living condition; read an observation table; choose a fair-test variable; predict a likely effect; select evidence; explain survival/growth using a condition.
- Test coverage: novel aquatic and terrestrial contexts, a graph/table/image, and at least one item where more than one factor changed so students must reject an unsupported causal conclusion.
- Misconceptions to target: “any environmental change is automatically harmful”; confusing weather event with a measured condition; treating correlation as proof; claiming an organism can survive anywhere if it has food.
- Feedback must name the evidence gap: “This condition changed too, so the test cannot show that light alone caused the difference.”

AC9S6U02 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Describe movement of Earth and other planets relative to the Sun and model the connections among Earth’s tilt, axial rotation, revolution and cyclic observable phenomena.
- Required boundary: distinguish three different movements/causes rather than delivering a generic solar-system recall lesson.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - P.1 “Analyse models of the Earth-Sun-Moon system” explicitly introduces models as physical objects, graphs, diagrams or simulations and asks students to interpret relative size. The sampled item required estimating that the Sun’s diameter is about 100 times Earth’s from a model.
  - Closest IXL supports: P.1 model analysis; P.2 day and night; P.3 changing night sky; P.4 planets; P.5 planet-property data.
  - IXL gives usable diagram/model and data-reading patterns but does not itself establish the full tilt/revolution causal chain.
- Khan Academy: available search/course coverage is supplementary; the current search interface returned no direct Australian Year 6 mapped exercise. Use its space-science explanations only for background sequencing, not as curriculum authority.
- Additional trusted-source scan: NASA education resources are an appropriate authority for diagrams/simulations of rotation, revolution, axial tilt, day/night and seasons. ABC Education search had no reliably indexed code-specific item; record as SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED, not a research failure.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Non-negotiable relationship map: rotation ≈ one day -> day/night; revolution ≈ one year; tilted axis + revolution -> changing angle/duration of sunlight and seasons. Earth’s distance from the Sun is not the cause of seasons.
- Include a physical or diagram model with a fixed tilted axis; require students to explain what the model represents and what it cannot show to scale.
- Practice coverage: label direction of rotation/revolution; match phenomenon to mechanism; sequence day/night; use two seasonal Earth positions; interpret a simplified model; compare planet paths/properties.
- Test coverage: new diagrams with different orientation, southern-hemisphere season context, identify a misleading model claim, and explain why a single still image cannot show an orbit.
- Misconceptions to target: rotation causes seasons; Earth is “closer to the Sun in summer”; tilt changes month to month; the Sun goes around Earth each day.
- Feedback needs causal language (“because the hemisphere is tilted toward/away from the Sun while Earth revolves”), not merely labels.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6U03 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Investigate energy transfer and transformation in electrical circuits, including circuit components, conductors and insulators.
- Required boundary: students must reason about a complete working circuit and energy transformation, not only recall component names.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - H.1 “Electric circuits” uses everyday devices and asks for the best energy transformation. Sampled prompt: choose the device converting electrical energy to light; a lamp was the intended response rather than an alarm (sound) or toaster (thermal).
  - Closest IXL supports: H.1 electric circuits; H.2 conductors and insulators; G.1 energy transformation; G.4/G.5 energy-transfer exploration.
  - IXL’s accessible contexts support item stems using appliances, labelled components and purpose-based classifications.
- Khan Academy: current authenticated search interface did not surface a direct Year 6 Australian-curriculum item. Treat any general electricity lesson as supplementary concept support only.
- Additional trusted-source scan: use a recognised science authority/ABC Education circuit visual where available during authoring QA; the public ABC search did not yield a code-specific indexed resource. Do not claim an ABC code mapping.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach a circuit as an unbroken conducting path. Battery/cell supplies energy; wires connect; switch opens/closes the path; load (lamp/buzzer/motor) transfers electrical energy to light/sound/motion/heat.
- Teach materials by circuit role: conductor permits current in the model; insulator is used for safe covering/separation. Avoid the inaccurate shortcut that an insulator “stops all electricity everywhere.”
- Practice coverage: complete/open circuit diagrams; component function; choose suitable material; trace energy transformations; diagnose one changed variable (gap, flat cell, broken conductor); compare two simple circuits.
- Test coverage: unseen diagrams and household contexts; a safe-design choice involving insulated wire; multi-step energy chain; evidence observation (lamp off) with competing explanations.
- Misconceptions to target: current is “used up” by the lamp; battery contains current rather than providing energy; a switch creates electricity; plastic is always an insulator without context.
- Feedback needs distinguish energy transfer/transformation from current flow and name why path continuity matters.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6U04 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Compare reversible changes (including dissolving and changes of state) with irreversible changes (including cooking and rusting) that produce new substances.
- Required boundary: decide from observable/chemical evidence whether a new substance has formed; do not classify solely by whether the original material is easy to recover.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - D.4 “Heating, cooling and changes of state” gives visual change-of-state diagrams and asks which change is caused by cooling; sampled answer pattern distinguishes freezing from melting.
  - Closest IXL supports: D.1 solids/liquids/gases; D.2–D.4 changes of state/heating/cooling; E.1 mixtures. IXL is strong for physical state-change vocabulary but does not fully cover new-substance reasoning or rusting/cooking.
- Khan Academy: current authenticated search interface was checked for “states of matter”; it exposed the search interface but no direct Australian Year 6 map. Record supplementary/non-mapped rather than inventing a linkage.
- Additional trusted-source scan: authoritative chemistry education should supply particulate/observable diagrams for dissolving, melting/freezing and rusting. ABC Education search currently lacks a reliable code-specific index result.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Reversible examples: melting/freezing, evaporating/condensing, dissolving where recovery method is possible. Irreversible examples: cooking and rusting, where a new substance forms.
- Use observation language: state change, colour/texture/gas/odour changes, residue, and whether the process can be reversed by simple physical means. Do not imply every visible change proves a new substance.
- Practice coverage: classify examples with reasons; complete a heating/cooling state-change arrow; choose recovery method for dissolved salt; compare rusting with melting; read a short before/after investigation.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar scenarios (e.g. wax, ice, sugar solution, baked batter, tarnish/rust), diagrams requiring process labels, and one “insufficient evidence” item.
- Misconceptions to target: dissolving means disappearing; all heating is irreversible; an irreversible process is merely hard to undo; melted metal and rusted metal are the same type of change.
- Feedback must use the distinction “no new substance / new substance formed”, then connect that to the example evidence.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6H01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Examine why advances in science are often the result of collaboration or build on the work of others.
- Required boundary: students must identify how shared evidence, specialised roles, peer checking and prior findings enable an advance; a generic biography of one “great scientist” is insufficient.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - No direct AC9S6H01 mapping was found: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.
  - Closest task form is C.3 “Use data from tests to compare engineering-design solutions”. The sampled item supplied three boat prototypes and two numerical requirements, then required selecting every design meeting each requirement. This supports evidence sharing and comparison, but collaboration must be authored explicitly.
- Khan Academy: no Australian Curriculum code map and no direct Year 6 collaboration exercise surfaced in the authenticated science search. Relevant scientist/history explanations are supplementary only.
- ABC Education: “Up, up and away!” models question-predict-test-observe and is explicitly produced with a science and technology centre; it is useful as a simple example of public science communication and institutional collaboration, though its Years 7–8 fire experiment is not suitable as a Year 6 practical.
- Trusted-source perspective: CSIRO and other research institutions organise projects around teams, disciplines, shared instruments, datasets and accumulated findings. Use age-appropriate case studies without implying that collaboration guarantees correctness.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach four mechanisms: divide specialist roles; pool observations/data; check and repeat one another’s work; build on published evidence and tools.
- Distinguish collaboration from merely “working in a group”: the work must contribute to a shared scientific question or improvement.
- Practice coverage: identify prior contribution versus new contribution; match team role to task; sequence claim-review-revision; select evidence showing an advance built on earlier work; compare individual and collaborative limitations.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar case studies, a timeline of incremental improvements, a shared-data table, and an item asking why independent replication strengthens confidence.
- Misconceptions to target: one scientist works alone from start to finish; later work makes earlier work worthless; consensus is a vote rather than an evidence-based judgment; every team member performs the same task.
- Feedback should name the specific contribution and explain how it enabled checking, extension or application.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6H02 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Investigate how scientific knowledge is used by individuals and communities to identify problems, consider responses and make decisions.
- Required boundary: the decision process includes evidence, options, benefits, limitations and stakeholder needs; it is not simply “science tells people the correct answer”.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - M.3 “Coral reef biodiversity and human uses: evaluate solutions” presents a damaged reef, scientific projections, ecosystem services and distinct interest-group requirements.
  - The sampled item asked what a council should do first; the intended reasoning was to determine each major interest group’s requirements before choosing a solution.
  - Closest supports: M.1 community protection of sea turtles; M.2 explore a coral-reef problem; M.3 evaluate solutions. This is a strong conceptual match even though it is not labelled with the AC9 code.
- Khan Academy: ecology/conservation resources can support ecosystem evidence, but no Australian Year 6 code-mapped decision task was found.
- ABC Education: curriculum-mapped science investigations provide Australian contexts and prompts for prediction, evidence and procedural reporting. Use ABC/BTN public-issue contexts only when the science evidence and decision trade-offs are explicit.
- Trusted-source perspective: community decisions may weigh scientific evidence together with cultural, environmental, social and economic considerations. Science informs a choice but does not determine values or priorities on its own.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Decision chain: identify the problem -> gather relevant scientific evidence -> identify affected groups and criteria -> compare possible responses -> justify a decision -> monitor outcomes and revise.
- Practice coverage: separate evidence from opinion; identify a relevant measurement; match response to criterion; compare options in a matrix; choose the best-supported claim; recognise uncertainty.
- Test coverage: novel Australian community contexts (water, habitat, energy or waste), quantitative evidence, conflicting stakeholder requirements and a justified recommendation.
- Misconceptions to target: the cheapest option is automatically best; any scientific fact is relevant; one stakeholder represents the whole community; uncertainty means no decision can be made.
- Feedback must state which evidence supports which criterion and acknowledge a genuine trade-off.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Pose investigable questions to identify patterns and test relationships, and make reasoned predictions.
- Required boundary: an investigable question must specify measurable/observable variables and be answerable with available methods; a prediction must include evidence-based reasoning.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - B.3 “Identify the experimental question” presents a complete method and asks which question it can actually answer.
  - Sampled context: compare the number of equal-sized drops of pure water and soapy water a penny can hold, using repeated trials. The valid question matched the two tested liquids; an option involving washing-up liquid was invalid because it was never tested.
  - Closest supports: A.1 science inquiry process; B.3 experimental question; B.4 questions investigable with given materials.
- Khan Academy: scientific-method material is supplementary and not AC9-mapped; no direct current Year 6 mapped exercise was identified.
- ABC Education, Years 5–6 “Pepper scatter experiment”: students predict the effect of detergent, observe evidence, add a no-detergent control and repeat while changing one variable. This is a strong Australian, age-matched inquiry model.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Question frame: “How does [variable changed] affect [variable measured] when [key controls] stay the same?”
- Prediction frame: “If … then … because …”; the “because” must use prior knowledge or observations, not repeat the prediction.
- Practice coverage: investigable/non-investigable; identify variables from a method; rewrite a vague question; match question to equipment/data; make and justify a directional prediction; distinguish pattern questions from relationship questions.
- Test coverage: novel materials lists and procedures, questions with unsafe/unmeasurable wording, prediction evidence and an item where the available data cannot answer the stated question.
- Misconceptions to target: every question is testable; predictions are random guesses; adding more changed variables improves a test; a yes/no wording automatically makes a question invalid.
- Feedback should identify the missing measurement, unavailable comparison or uncontrolled factor precisely.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I02 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Plan and conduct repeatable investigations to answer questions, including deciding variables to change, measure and control in fair tests.
- Required boundary: repeatability requires enough procedural detail, consistent measurement and repeated trials; “do it again” alone is not a repeatable method.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - B.2 “Identify independent and dependent variables” gives an extended experimental protocol and a direct variable-choice item.
  - Sampled context: a wooden race car used light versus heavy wheels of equal diameter, ran three trials per wheel type and measured travel time. The dependent variable was time to reach the bottom; wheel weight was the independent variable.
  - Closest supports: B.1 control/experimental groups; B.2 variables; B.5–B.7 interpret protocols about plant growth, diffusion and evaporation.
- Khan Academy: general experimental-design explanations are supplementary; no direct Australian Year 6 mapped sequence surfaced.
- ABC Education “Pepper scatter experiment”: explicitly asks students to add a control, record evidence and repeat the procedure by changing one variable only. It reinforces fair-test logic and the role of controls.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Planning structure: question; risk/safety; equipment with quantities; independent/dependent/control variables; numbered method; measurement units/precision; repetitions; results table.
- Explain why three or more trials and a mean/pattern check improve reliability, while repetition does not repair a systematically unfair method.
- Practice coverage: label variables; choose controls; order a method; select measuring equipment and units; add missing repeatability detail; identify control group; improve an unsafe or unfair plan.
- Test coverage: critique two plans, calculate/interpret repeated results, identify a confounding variable and write a short repeatable method for a new context.
- Misconceptions to target: the controlled variable is the outcome; all variables must be identical; one accurate-looking result is enough; changing two factors saves time without affecting validity; repeatable means getting exactly the same number.
- Feedback should separate validity (fair comparison), reliability (consistent pattern across repetitions) and precision (measurement resolution).
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I03 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Use equipment to observe, measure and record data with reasonable precision, using digital tools as appropriate.
- Required boundary: equipment choice, correct units, scale reading, precision and consistent recording must be applied in an investigation—not reduced to naming laboratory equipment.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - A.2 “Identify laboratory tools” uses image-based selection; the sampled item asked students to identify an Erlenmeyer flask. This supports equipment recognition but is below the full descriptor depth.
  - Q.1 “Choose units of distance, mass and volume” tests scale-sensitive unit choice. The sampled item asked for the most reasonable unit for the Nile River; kilometres was the viable estimate rather than metres, centimetres or millimetres.
  - Closest supports: A.2 laboratory tools; Q.1 unit selection; Q.2–Q.4 unit abbreviations. No exact IXL skill combines apparatus choice with measurement precision and data recording.
- Khan Academy: no Australian Curriculum code mapping. General measurement/data lessons are supplementary; advanced statistics error resources are not age-appropriate substitutes.
- ABC Education, “Who loves soil? Tim does!” (Years 5–6/7–8): shows field scientists measuring soil pH, texture and compaction, establishing sampling sites and recording information. This provides an authentic Australian context for linked observing, measuring and recording.
- ABC Education, “Knowing nutrition” (Years 5–6): requires quantities to be recorded in grams, millilitres and counts, reinforcing units matched to measured variables.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach the measurement chain: define variable -> select instrument -> select unit -> read scale at eye level -> record all values consistently -> note precision.
- “Reasonable precision” means appropriate to the instrument and question; it does not mean adding unsupported decimal places.
- Include ruler, measuring cylinder, balance, thermometer, stopwatch, pH indicator/meter and simple digital sensor contexts.
- Practice coverage: match instrument and variable; choose units; read analogue/digital displays; identify parallax/scale errors; record data in a prepared table; judge whether precision is reasonable.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar instrument diagrams, mixed-unit errors, repeated readings and a fieldwork/digital-tool context.
- Misconceptions: larger instrument is always more precise; digital means perfectly accurate; every measurement should have two decimals; estimates and measurements are interchangeable.
- Feedback must name the specific instrument/scale/unit mismatch and model the corrected record including its unit.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I04 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Construct and use appropriate representations, including tables, graphs and visual or physical models, to organise and process data and information, and describe patterns, trends and relationships.
- Required boundary: students must both construct/select a representation and interpret what it shows; decorative diagrams or graph-reading alone are incomplete.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - G.3 “Use tables and graphs to identify patterns about kinetic energy” provides aligned mass, speed and kinetic-energy data and asks students to compare quantities.
  - Sampled context: nickel (50 dg, 10 m/s, 250 mJ) versus penny (25 dg, 10 m/s, 125 mJ). Students must recognise equal speed and a paired increase in mass and kinetic energy.
  - Closest supports: G.3 table/graph pattern analysis; C.3 use test data to compare solutions; P.1 analyse models. IXL provides strong interpretation forms but little explicit table/graph construction.
- Khan Academy: graph and data lessons are supplementary and not AC9-mapped. Advanced statistical graphing is beyond this Year 6 descriptor.
- ABC Education, “Science Years 5–6: The Solar System”: explicitly develops a scale representation, discusses the chosen scale, and demonstrates multiple construction methods. This is strong evidence for modelling, scale and model limitations.
- ABC Education, “Knowing nutrition”: students collect quantities over time, compare them with recommended values and discuss patterns, supplying a familiar data-organisation context.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Representation selection: table for exact organised values; column graph for categories; line graph for change over continuous time; labelled diagram/model for structure or relationships.
- Construction checklist: descriptive title, labelled variables/axes, units, sensible even scale, accurate plotting and key where needed.
- Practice coverage: select representation; complete a table; choose axes; detect misleading/uneven scale; plot or identify a point; describe trend; distinguish pattern from unsupported cause; evaluate model strengths/limits.
- Test coverage: transform a short dataset into a representation choice, interpret novel graphs, compare two representations and explain one model limitation.
- Misconceptions: independent variable belongs on either axis arbitrarily; every dataset needs a line graph; a graph proves causation; a larger picture is a more accurate model.
- Feedback must connect the representation feature to the scientific purpose, for example why time belongs on the horizontal axis.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I05 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Compare methods and findings with those of others, recognise possible sources of error, pose questions for further investigation and select evidence to draw reasoned conclusions.
- Required boundary: conclusions must answer the original question using evidence and acknowledge method limits; “the hypothesis was correct” is not a sufficient conclusion.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - B.5 “Understand an experimental protocol about plant growth” provides materials, detailed method and two data tables.
  - Sampled protocol: 10 tomato plants, five without earthworms and five with two earthworms, same pot size/location/water, then count tomatoes after three months. The item checks whether claimed materials are actually required.
  - Closest supports: B.5–B.7 protocol interpretation; B.1/B.2 groups and variables; C.2/C.3 evaluate and compare design-test evidence. No single IXL skill covers method comparison, sources of error, further questions and conclusions together.
- Khan Academy: scientific-method resources are supplementary and not AC9-mapped. Type I/II error and standard-error lessons surfaced in search but are explicitly excluded as developmentally inappropriate for Year 6.
- ABC Education “Pepper scatter experiment” asks learners to compare predictions with observations, add a control, identify evidence and modify one variable. It offers an age-appropriate evaluation cycle.
- ABC Education “Who loves soil? Tim does!” models standardised sampling, measurement and recording, supporting discussion of why methods must be comparable.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Evaluation sequence: compare question/variables/method/sample/repeats -> compare findings -> identify plausible error -> judge effect on results -> select evidence -> conclude -> ask a focused next question.
- Distinguish random variation, measurement/reading error, inconsistent procedure and confounding variables. Avoid teaching “human error” as an adequate explanation.
- Practice coverage: match conclusion to data; choose relevant evidence; identify a method difference; predict error direction where possible; improve a method; pose a genuine extension question.
- Test coverage: two student methods/results, anomalous data, a conclusion stronger than the evidence, and an extension that changes one purposeful factor.
- Misconceptions: different results mean dishonesty; an anomaly should always be deleted; more data automatically fixes bias; conclusion repeats the method; every imperfection invalidates the investigation.
- Feedback must explain how the specific source of error could affect the measurement or comparison.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S6I06 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Write and create texts to communicate ideas and findings for specific purposes and audiences, including selection of language features, using digital tools as appropriate.
- Required boundary: the same investigation should be communicated differently for purpose and audience while retaining accurate methods, evidence, units and conclusions.

Evidence collected
- ACARA official Year 6 Science curriculum: exact content description confirmed.
- IXL Australia:
  - No direct Year 6 Science skill maps to creating audience-specific scientific texts: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.
  - IXL protocol passages, data tables and evidence-comparison tasks provide useful source material, but they assess interpretation rather than communication design.
- Khan Academy: no Australian Curriculum mapping or direct age-matched science-communication authoring exercise identified; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for this descriptor.
- ABC Education “Pepper scatter experiment” explicitly asks students to write a procedural report and identify evidence.
- ABC Education “Who loves soil? Tim does!” demonstrates a scientist communicating field methods to a community workshop and explaining technical ideas through accessible comparison.
- ABC Education “Knowing nutrition” requires recording quantities, justifying choices and discussing findings with peers, supporting audience-aware explanation.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach purpose/audience choices: procedural report for repeatability; results summary for peers; labelled infographic for families; recommendation for a community; oral/digital presentation for a class.
- Scientific integrity remains constant: correct terminology, evidence, units, uncertainty and source attribution. Language, structure and visual density may change with audience.
- Practice coverage: identify purpose/audience; select suitable format; order report sections; improve an ambiguous method; choose precise rather than emotive language; match graph/caption to message; write evidence-based claim.
- Test coverage: adapt one finding for two audiences, select necessary/irrelevant detail, correct a misleading caption, and draft a concise conclusion with evidence and limitation.
- Misconceptions: simpler language permits inaccuracy; more technical words always improve science writing; a colourful infographic needs little evidence; procedure and conclusion are interchangeable; digital tools guarantee clarity.
- Feedback should state which audience need was missed while preserving scientific accuracy.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.
