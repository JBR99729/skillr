Year 9 Maths • AC9M9ST01 • Topic guide

Analysing survey reports in media and digital contexts

This topic helps students become careful readers of survey reports. Students learn to identify who was surveyed, how the survey was conducted, what the data actually shows, and whether the conclusion is justified.

Core understanding

What students need to know

Key idea

A survey report should clearly state the population, sample size, sampling method and question wording.

Key idea

A strong conclusion matches the data and avoids exaggerating beyond what was asked.

Key idea

Graphs, percentages and headlines can make a claim look stronger than the evidence supports.

Key idea

Reliability improves when the sample is large, relevant and not obviously biased.

Formulas and representations

Useful rules and ways to represent the idea

Always ask: who was surveyed?
Check whether percentages include the sample size.
Look for loaded wording, missing options or unclear categories.
Separate the data from the author’s interpretation.

Worked example

Worked example: reading a survey claim

  1. A headline says, “90% of students want longer lunch breaks.”
  2. The report shows only 10 students from one class were surveyed.
  3. 90% means 9 out of 10 students, so the percentage is based on a very small sample.
  4. The claim may describe that class, but it is not strong evidence about all students.
  5. A cautious conclusion would say: in this small sample, most students preferred longer lunch breaks.

Common misconceptions

What to watch for

  • Trusting a percentage without checking the sample size.
  • Assuming a survey represents everyone when only one group was asked.
  • Confusing a headline with the actual statistical evidence.
  • Ignoring question wording that pushes people toward a certain answer.

Quick check

Try these before moving on

  1. A report says 80% of 20 people agree. How many people is that?
  2. Why is “Do you agree with our excellent new policy?” a biased question?
  3. List two details that make a survey report more trustworthy.

Support tip

For parents and teachers

Use real news headlines or school survey examples. Ask students to rewrite exaggerated claims as careful statistical conclusions.
