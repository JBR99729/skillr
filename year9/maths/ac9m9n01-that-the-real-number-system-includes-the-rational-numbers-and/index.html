Real Numbers and Exact Values

Learn how rational and irrational numbers fit together, how to recognise exact values, and why rounding too early can change an answer.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest

What you need to know

Real numbers are every number that can be placed on the ordinary number line. They split into two groups: rational and irrational numbers.

  • Rational numbers can be written as a fraction a/b, where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. Their decimals terminate or repeat.
  • Irrational numbers cannot be written as such a fraction. Their decimals continue forever without repeating a fixed pattern.

Learning goal: classify real numbers, move between exact and approximate forms, locate irrational numbers on a number line, and explain why an answer is exact or approximate.

How the number sets fit together

REAL NUMBERS

Irrational examples: √2, √7, π

RATIONAL NUMBERS

Examples: 3/4, −2.5, 0.333…

INTEGERS

Examples: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …

NATURAL NUMBERS

Counting numbers such as 1, 2, 3, …

The nesting matters. Every natural number is an integer; every integer is rational because, for example, −4 = −4/1; every rational number is real. Irrational numbers are also real, but they are not rational.

Fast test: terminating decimal → rational; repeating decimal → rational; non-terminating, non-repeating decimal → irrational.

Worked example 1 — classify a tricky square root

Question: Is √36 rational or irrational?

√36 = 6

Because 36 is a perfect square, √36 simplifies to the integer 6. Since 6 = 6/1, it is rational. Do not assume every square root is irrational.

Worked example 2 — exact versus approximate

A circle has radius 3 cm. Find its area exactly and then to 2 decimal places.

A = πr² = π(3)² = 9π cm²

9π cm² is exact because π has not been rounded.

9π ≈ 28.274333… ≈ 28.27 cm²

28.27 cm² is approximate because the true value has been rounded. Keep the exact form during calculations and round only when the question asks for an approximation.

Why rounding too early matters

Suppose you need 5π and you replace π with 3.14 immediately.

Exact form: 5πUsing π ≈ 3.14: 5 × 3.14 = 15.70Calculator value: 5π ≈ 15.707963…

The rounded calculation has already lost information. In multi-step calculations, that error can grow. Keep π, roots and fractions exact until the final rounding step whenever possible.

Worked example 3 — locating √10 without a calculator

Find two consecutive integers between which √10 lies.

3² = 9 and 4² = 169 < 10 < 16Therefore 3 < √10 < 4

So √10 is on the number line between 3 and 4. A calculator gives √10 ≈ 3.162, which confirms the estimate.

Constructing √2 on a number line

  1. Draw a square with side length 1.
  2. Its diagonal has length √(1² + 1²) = √2 by Pythagoras.
  3. Use that diagonal as a radius centred at 0 on the number line.
  4. Where the arc meets the number line is the exact position of √2.

Worked example 4 — solve and show an interval

Solve 1.2x − 5.4 > 10.8.

1.2x − 5.4 > 10.81.2x > 16.2x > 13.5

On a number line, use an open circle at 13.5 because 13.5 itself is not included, and shade/arrow to the right for values greater than 13.5.

Common misconceptions

  • “Every square root is irrational.” False. √25 = 5 is rational; √5 is irrational.
  • “π equals 3.14.” False. 3.14 is an approximation to π.
  • “A decimal that never ends must be irrational.” False. 0.333… never ends but repeats, so it is rational.
  • “Integers and rational numbers are separate sets.” Integers are contained inside the rational numbers because any integer n can be written n/1.
  • “Approximate values can replace exact values at any stage.” Early rounding can create avoidable error; keep exact forms until the final step.

Mastery check — important questions

1. Classify −7 as fully as possible.
Answer: Integer, rational and real.
2. Is 0.272727… rational?
Answer: Yes. The decimal repeats, so it can be written as a fraction.
3. Is √49 irrational?
Answer: No. √49 = 7, so it is rational.
4. Give an irrational number between 2 and 3.
Answer: √5 works because 4 < 5 < 9, so 2 < √5 < 3.
5. Which is exact: 6π or 18.85?
Answer: 6π. The decimal is rounded.
6. Why is 0.125 rational?
Answer: It terminates and equals 1/8.
7. Between which integers does √20 lie?
Answer: 4 and 5 because 16 < 20 < 25.
8. Can irrational × irrational be rational?
Answer: Yes. √2 × √2 = 2.
9. Why should you usually round only at the end?
Answer: To avoid accumulating rounding error through later steps.
10. What does an open circle at 4 with an arrow right mean?
Answer: x > 4.

Revision notes

  • Real numbers = rational numbers + irrational numbers.
  • Natural ⊂ integer ⊂ rational ⊂ real.
  • Terminating or repeating decimals are rational.
  • Non-terminating, non-repeating decimals are irrational.
  • π and unsimplifiable roots such as √2 are exact irrational values.
  • A rounded decimal is an approximation, not the exact value.
  • Use nearby perfect squares to estimate the position of square roots.
  • Keep exact values through working and round at the end unless told otherwise.
