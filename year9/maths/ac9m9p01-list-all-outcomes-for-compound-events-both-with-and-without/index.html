Concept boundary and prerequisites

Teaching progression

This progression makes the Year 9 probability relationship visible before independent application.

  1. 1. Compound Events & Probability
    Introduce the lesson goal and display the three approved representations.
    Students: Names the sample space and checks whether the experiment uses replacement.
  2. 2. What is a compound event?
    Pair each coin result with all six die results and count the completed sample space.
    Students: Lists or counts all ordered coin-die pairs.
  3. 3. Representing outcomes: lists and arrays
    Build the ordered list and matching 2 × 2 array one row at a time.
    Students: Matches each ordered pair to one array cell and identifies HH as favourable.
  4. 4. Tree diagrams for multi-stage events
    Draw and label the first branches, replace the marble, then repeat the same branches at stage two.
    Students: Multiplies along the red-red path and explains the unchanged branch probabilities.
  5. 5. Independent events: with replacement
    Mark the replacement step between two identical 52-card pools.
    Students: Simplifies each branch probability and multiplies along the path.
  6. 6. Dependent events: without replacement
    Cross out the drawn ace, recount the deck and recount the kings before labelling stage two.
    Students: Updates only the quantities changed by the first outcome and multiplies the path.
  7. 7. Population selection without replacement
    Model the class with 10 name cards and remove one Year 9 card after the first selection.
    Students: Updates numerator and denominator and calculates 2/15.
  8. 8. Quick summary and check
    Hide the solution, ask students to label both stages, then reveal and compare.
    Students: Identifies no replacement, labels both branches and simplifies the product.
Worked examples

Compound Events & Probability

Map multi-step events and calculate their probabilities using systematic lists, two-way tables and tree diagrams.

Teacher prompt: What must be recorded before calculating the probability of a compound event?

What is a compound event?

Coin outcomes H or T combined with die outcomes 1 to 6 give 2 × 6 = 12 equally likely outcomes: H1 to H6 and T1 to T6.

Teacher prompt: Why are there 12 outcomes rather than 8?

Representing outcomes: lists and arrays

Two coin flips give (H,H), (H,T), (T,H) and (T,T). Only (H,H) has two heads, so P(2 heads) = 1/4 = 25%.

Teacher prompt: Why are HT and TH different outcomes?

Tree diagrams for multi-stage events

Bag: 3 red, 2 blue, with replacement. P(R then R) = 3/5 × 3/5 = 9/25.

Teacher prompt: Why does the second red branch remain 3/5?

Independent events: with replacement

From 52 cards, replace and shuffle after drawing an ace: P(ace then king) = 4/52 × 4/52 = 1/13 × 1/13 = 1/169.

Teacher prompt: Which numbers show that replacement has restored the experiment?

Dependent events: without replacement

Keep the ace out: P(ace then king) = 4/52 × 4/51 = 4/663.

Teacher prompt: Why does the king numerator stay 4 while the denominator becomes 51?

Population selection without replacement

Class of 10: 4 Year 9 and 6 Year 10. P(two Year 9 students) = 4/10 × 3/9 = 2/15.

Teacher prompt: What changes after the first Year 9 student is selected?

Quick summary and check

Bag: 3 green, 2 yellow. Eat one green, then pick again: P(G then G) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 3/10.

Teacher prompt: A sweet is eaten after the first pick. Why is the second green probability 2/4?

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Compound Events & Probability

Curriculum: discussing two-step chance experiments, such as the game of Heads and Tails, describing the different outcomes and their related probabilities

Understanding outcomes with and without replacement.

Teacher: Introduce the lesson goal and display the three approved representations.

Ask: What must be recorded before calculating the probability of a compound event?

Look for: States that all outcomes and the replacement condition must be known.

E2: Representing outcomes: lists and arrays

Curriculum: using systematic methods such as lists or arrays to record outcomes and assign probabilities, such as drawing the names of students from a bag to appoint 2 team leaders

A systematic list or two-way array organises two-stage outcomes so none are missed or counted twice.

Teacher: Build the ordered list and matching 2 × 2 array one row at a time.

Ask: Why are HT and TH different outcomes?

Look for: Lists four unique outcomes and calculates 1/4.

E3: Tree diagrams for multi-stage events

Curriculum: using a tree diagram to represent a three-stage event and assigning probabilities to these events; for example, selecting 3 cards from a deck, assigning the probability of drawing an ace, then a king, then a queen of the same suit, with and without replacing the cards after every draw

A tree branches at each stage: multiply probabilities along a path and add probabilities across disjoint successful paths.

Teacher: Draw and label the first branches, replace the marble, then repeat the same branches at stage two.

Ask: Why does the second red branch remain 3/5?

Look for: Obtains 9/25 and links equal stage probabilities to replacement.

E4: Population selection without replacement

Curriculum: assigning probabilities to compound events involving the random selection of people from a given population; for example, selecting 2 names at random from all of the students at a high school and assigning the probability that they are both in Year 9

Randomly selecting distinct people uses without-replacement probabilities because one person cannot be selected twice.

Teacher: Model the class with 10 name cards and remove one Year 9 card after the first selection.

Ask: What changes after the first Year 9 student is selected?

Look for: Uses 4/10 × 3/9 and explains both decreases.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • The sample space or dependency changes without being noticed — Rebuild the outcomes and label replacement or conditioning explicitly.
  • The compound outcomes model is copied without interpretation — Connect every label in ‘Map multi-step events and calculate their probabilities using systematic lists, two-way tables and tree diagrams.’ to its mathematical role.
  • The result is not verified — Use a second representation, boundary case, inverse operation or simulation check.
Support, core and extend

Support: Reduce numerical or representational load while retaining the same decision and explanation.

Core: Connect at least two representations, justify the method and verify independently.

Extend: Test a boundary case, counterexample or model limitation without introducing later-year procedures.

Quick check and mastery evidence

Prompt: What must be recorded before calculating the probability of a compound event?

Expected idea: Record every possible outcome systematically and identify whether replacement changes later probabilities.

Students should explain the relationship in their own words, apply it in a new case and verify the result using another representation, estimate or check.
