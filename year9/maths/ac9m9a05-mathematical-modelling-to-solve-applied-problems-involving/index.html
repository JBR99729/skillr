Year 9 Maths • AC9M9A05

Mathematical modelling with linear, quadratic and financial contexts

Mathematical modelling is about turning a real situation into a useful mathematical representation. In Year 9, students use linear and quadratic relationships, rates, constraints and financial contexts to make predictions, compare options and explain limitations.

Core understanding

What students need to understand

A model is a simplified version of reality

Models focus on important quantities and ignore some detail. Good models are useful, not perfect.

Variables represent changing quantities

Choosing clear variables makes the equation easier to build and interpret.

Linear models show constant change

A linear model is suitable when the same amount is added or removed each step.

Quadratic models can represent curved patterns

Area, projectile-style paths and some optimisation contexts can involve quadratic behaviour.

Key tools

Formulas and representations

linear model: y = mx + c

simple interest: I = Prt

total amount: A = P + I

quadratic model example: y = ax² + bx + c

Choose the formula or representation that matches the question. In Year 9, marks often come from defining variables, showing substitutions and interpreting the result.

Worked example

Worked example: choosing between two hire plans

  1. Plan A costs $20 plus $8 per hour, so A = 20 + 8h.
  2. Plan B costs $50 plus $5 per hour, so B = 50 + 5h.
  3. Set equal to find the break-even point: 20 + 8h = 50 + 5h.
  4. 3h = 30, so h = 10.
  5. Interpretation: for 10 hours the plans cost the same; below 10 hours Plan A is cheaper, above 10 hours Plan B is cheaper.

Quick check

Try these before practice

  1. Write a linear cost model for a $12 booking fee plus $4 per item.
  2. Explain why a model may be useful even if it is not perfectly realistic.
  3. Find the simple interest on $800 at 6% p.a. for 3 years.

Common misconceptions

Watch for these mistakes

  • Writing an equation without defining variables.
  • Forgetting units, such as dollars, hours or years.
  • Using a linear model when the pattern is not changing by a constant amount.
  • Giving a numerical answer without interpreting what it means in context.

Support tip

For parents and teachers

Ask students to say what the variables mean, what assumptions are being made, and whether the final answer is sensible. This builds stronger modelling habits than calculation alone.
