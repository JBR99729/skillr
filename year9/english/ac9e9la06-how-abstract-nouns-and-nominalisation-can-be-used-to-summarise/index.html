Abstract nouns and nominalisation

This topic develops control of formal academic language. Students learn how abstract nouns and nominalisation can summarise ideas, build cohesion across a paragraph, and make writing sound more analytical.

Start practiceTake testOpen worksheet

Core understanding

What students need to know

Abstract noun

An abstract noun names an idea, quality or concept, such as justice, fear, loyalty or conflict.

Nominalisation

Nominalisation turns a verb, adjective or clause into a noun group, such as resist → resistance.

Summary

A noun group can summarise a whole previous idea so the next sentence can build on it.

Formality

Nominalisation often creates a more formal, analytical tone, but overuse can make writing dense.

English language tools

Useful terms and ways to analyse the idea

decide → decision
argue → argument
people resisted the rule → this resistance
the character feels isolated → the character's isolation

Worked example

Worked example: making analysis more concise

  1. Basic: “The villagers reject the outsider because they are afraid of change.”
  2. Nominalised: “The villagers' rejection of the outsider reflects their fear of change.”
  3. The noun “rejection” condenses the action.
  4. The abstract noun “fear” names the underlying idea.
  5. The revised sentence sounds more analytical and links action to meaning.

Common misconceptions

What to watch for

  • Turning every verb into a noun and making writing too heavy.
  • Using abstract nouns without evidence from the text.
  • Confusing a concrete noun with an abstract concept.
  • Nominalising a sentence but losing who performed the action.

Quick check

Try these before moving on

  1. Turn “the character betrays her friend” into a noun group.
  2. Name an abstract noun that could describe a dystopian society.
  3. Rewrite “the crowd protested loudly” using the noun “protest”.

Support tip

For parents and teachers

Ask students to circle verbs in a paragraph, then choose one that could become a useful noun group. The goal is controlled condensation, not making every sentence more complicated.
