Direct and indirect evaluation in texts

This topic helps students explain how texts make judgements. Evaluation can be obvious, such as “excellent” or “dangerous”, or indirect, through imagery, comparison, selection of detail, sentence structure and tone.

Core understanding

What students need to know

Direct evaluation

Direct judgement uses clear evaluative words such as “unfair”, “valuable”, “reckless” or “effective”.

Indirect evaluation

Writers can imply judgement through what they include, omit, emphasise or compare.

Intensity

Adverbs, adjectives and modality can strengthen or soften the judgement.

Reader effect

Evaluation guides readers towards an attitude before they may consciously notice it.

English language tools

Useful terms and ways to analyse the idea

evaluative adjective: irresponsible, generous, harmful
adverb of intensity: barely, clearly, extremely
nominalisation: failure, success, threat
imagery can imply approval or criticism

Worked example

Worked example: spotting indirect evaluation

  1. Sentence: “The proposal crept through council with barely a whisper of public debate.”
  2. The verb “crept” indirectly suggests secrecy or suspicion.
  3. “Barely a whisper” minimises public discussion and implies concern.
  4. The sentence does not say “the proposal was bad”, but it positions readers to doubt the process.
  5. A strong answer explains both the language choice and the implied judgement.

Common misconceptions

What to watch for

  • Assuming evaluation only appears as adjectives.
  • Calling every descriptive word evaluative without explaining judgement.
  • Ignoring how structure and selection of detail create bias.
  • Forgetting to distinguish direct statement from implication.

Quick check

Try these before moving on

  1. Which word evaluates in “a reckless decision”?
  2. How could a writer indirectly criticise a character without saying “bad”?
  3. Rewrite “the plan failed” so the evaluation is stronger.

Support tip

For parents and teachers

Ask students to underline the judgement first, then decide whether it is directly stated or indirectly implied. This builds stronger analytical paragraphs.
