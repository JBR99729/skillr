Connect datasets without forcing every year to agree AC9S9I05

Learning intention: Align variables and dates across sources, quantify patterns and exceptions, and use scientific explanations without claiming that association alone establishes causation.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two quantities can rise overall while individual years differ

The saved NOAA station record and NASA global temperature-anomaly record cover the same calendar years, 1990–2005. CO₂ rises from 354.45 to 379.98 ppm, an increase of 25.53 ppm. The temperature anomaly rises from 0.45 to 0.68°C, an endpoint increase of 0.23°C. There are 15 elapsed years between those endpoints, not 16. Temperature does not increase every year: from 1998 to 1999, CO₂ rises by 1.70 ppm while the anomaly falls by 0.23°C. This exception challenges an “every year” claim. It does not alone refute a greenhouse influence or identify the contribution of each cause.

Learn the skill

1. Match the quantities before connecting them

CO₂ is a dry-air mole fraction measured at a station, expressed in parts per million. Temperature anomaly is a global land–ocean index relative to 1951–1980, not an absolute temperature or a faulty reading. Use the same dates but keep the units, spatial coverage and methods distinct.

1990: 354.45 ppm and +0.45°C anomaly. 2005: 379.98 ppm and +0.68°C anomaly.
Try it · Why not subtract a CO₂ value from a temperature value?

They are different measured quantities with different units and spatial coverage. Compare each series with itself across the matched years; do not invent a shared numerical unit.

2. Quantify a trend and distinguish description from explanation

An endpoint change describes the selected interval; an endpoint rate is not a fitted trend. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation, affecting energy loss to space under comparable conditions. That mechanism is separate evidence; these paired values alone cannot isolate causation or estimate climate sensitivity.

CO₂ endpoint change: 25.53 ppm across 1990–2005. Elapsed time: 15 years.
Try it · What is the average endpoint rate, and what does it not establish?

25.53 ÷ 15 = 1.702 ppm/year, about 1.70 ppm/year. It is an endpoint average over this window, not the increase in every year and not a causal temperature response per ppm.

3. Keep exceptions and investigate context

A departure from a simple pattern may reflect genuine variation, different influences, error or a limited model. Check metadata and independent context before excluding a point. Volcanic aerosols can reduce surface heating, and ocean–atmosphere variability such as El Niño can alter annual temperature; the table does not assign an exact share to either.

1998: 366.84 ppm, +0.61°C. 1999: 368.54 ppm, +0.38°C.
Try it · How should the apparent countertrend be reported?

CO₂ increased by 1.70 ppm while the anomaly decreased by 0.23°C. Retain both years, reject a strictly year-by-year monotonic claim, and examine additional influences and uncertainty without assigning the whole difference to one cause.

Common mistakes

“Anomaly means erroneous data.” Here a temperature anomaly is a difference from a defined reference climate.

“Similar graph shapes prove causation.” Mechanisms, methods and competing explanations require further evidence.

“A cooling year must be deleted.” Short-term variation is relevant evidence, not automatically a recording error.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a connected evidence analysis

Use the table to calculate both endpoint changes and the 1998–1999 contrast. Write a paragraph containing the overall pattern, a quantified exception, a relevant mechanism and one limit. Keep station/global coverage and units visible.

Adjust the challenge Support: match years and label quantities before calculating. Core: connect the two series and exception. Extend: compare selected windows and explain why a different window can change a numerical summary.

This is secondary-data analysis. No climate measurement, forecast or field activity is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Why is 0.68°C not the world’s absolute temperature?

It is an index difference from the 1951–1980 reference climate.

2. Does a 0.12 ppm uncertainty entry mean a 95% interval?

No. The saved NOAA definition is a standard deviation of differences between independently determined annual means; do not relabel it as a confidence interval.

3. What is the 1991–1992 contrast?

CO₂ increases from 355.70 to 356.54 ppm, +0.84 ppm; the anomaly decreases from 0.41 to 0.22°C, −0.19°C. This is another counterexample to an every-year claim.

FAQ

Where does the greenhouse explanation come from?

The saved research includes NASA’s greenhouse mechanism explanation: gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation. Its physical reasoning adds evidence beyond the paired table; the table alone is not an attribution study.

Should missing numerical uncertainty be treated as zero?

No. State that the selected table does not supply it and avoid inventing error bars or assuming perfect certainty.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Describe patterns and trends using variables, direction and data values.
  • Identify relationships without automatically claiming causation.
  • Recognise and investigate anomalies rather than deleting them automatically.
  • Connect multiple datasets/information sources to build a stronger explanation.
Prerequisite knowledge

Read tables and graphs, calculate simple summaries, identify variables and understand that measurements include uncertainty and variation.

Key concept

A strong trend statement names both variables, direction and evidence: “As temperature increased from 20°C to 50°C, mean reaction time decreased from …”. Relationships can be positive, negative, non-linear or absent. A relationship alone does not prove that changing one variable caused the other.

An anomaly is a result that does not fit the broader pattern. It may be a measurement problem, natural variation or important new evidence. Check records and repeat where appropriate before deciding how to treat it. Connecting independent data sources can strengthen or complicate a conclusion.

Worked examples

Example 1 — quantified trend

Weak: “it went down.” Better: “In the saved Mauna Loa annual record, CO₂ increased from 354.45 ppm in 1990 to 379.98 ppm in 2005: an endpoint rise of 25.53 ppm.”

Example 2 — anomaly

Four repeats cluster near 30 s and one is 71 s. Check timing notes/equipment and repeat if possible. Do not erase 71 s solely because it is inconvenient.

Example 3 — association

Areas with more tree cover also have lower surface temperature. This supports an association; other factors such as shade orientation or surface type may also contribute. A causal claim needs stronger design/evidence.

Common misconceptions
  • Correlation proves causation. Competing explanations may produce the pattern.
  • Anomalies should always be removed. Investigate them first.
  • A trend description needs no numbers. Quantification makes analysis testable and precise.
  • No obvious trend means useless data. Absence of a relationship can be scientifically informative.
Guided practice
  1. For the saved 1990 and 2005 Mauna Loa annual CO₂ values 354.45 and 379.98 ppm, replace “the graph rises” with a quantified endpoint comparison.
  2. In repeat times 29, 30, 31, 30 and 71 s, identify the value that departs from the cluster and warrants investigation.
  3. List two possible explanations for the anomaly.
  4. Rewrite a causal claim as an association claim where the design is observational.
Independent practice
  1. Describe a positive relationship using named variables.
  2. Describe a non-linear trend.
  3. Identify and investigate one anomalous point.
  4. Compare results from two datasets.
  5. State whether a dataset supports causation and justify.
  6. Write a conclusion that includes pattern, evidence and one limitation.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Dataset A shows a strong relationship between two variables; Dataset B from another location shows a weaker relationship and two anomalies. Construct the most defensible combined conclusion and propose one follow-up test that could distinguish a genuine contextual difference from measurement problems.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes a trend statement strong? Variables, direction, range and quantitative evidence.
  2. What is an anomaly? A result inconsistent with the broader pattern that warrants investigation.
  3. Does a relationship prove causation? No; design and competing variables matter.
  4. Why connect datasets? Independent evidence can reveal consistency, context dependence or conflict.
Practice and review
  1. Use the saved endpoints: Mauna Loa CO₂ 354.45 ppm in 1990 and 379.98 ppm in 2005; global temperature anomaly 0.45 and 0.68°C relative to 1951–1980. Write a quantified endpoint comparison for each quantity.
    Name variables and use at least one numerical comparison.
  2. Evaluate an anomalous result and propose the next action.
    Do not delete automatically; check evidence and repeat where justified.
  3. Decide whether a reported relationship justifies a causal conclusion.
    Use the investigation design and possible confounders.
Check understanding
  • I quantify trends.
  • I investigate anomalies.
  • I distinguish association from causation.
  • I connect evidence sources.

Exit ticket: Write one sentence that reports a relationship without overclaiming causation.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Ban unsupported phrases such as “proved” and “the graph went up”. Require at least one quantitative reference in data-analysis responses.

Parent/carer

Ask the student what pattern they see, which numbers support it, and what else could explain the pattern.
