Construct evidence-based scientific arguments — AC9S9I07

Build claims that are proportional to varied evidence, explain how evidence supports the reasoning, address limitations and counter-evidence, and use secondary data ethically with proper attribution and relevant cultural protocols.

LearnClassroom ViewHomeworkPracticeTest
1. Learning goals
  • Construct claim–evidence–reasoning arguments using more than one relevant source where appropriate.
  • Weight evidence by quality, relevance and consistency.
  • Address limitations or conflicting evidence rather than hiding it.
  • Access, use and cite secondary data ethically, including relevant cultural permissions/protocols.
2. Prerequisite knowledge

Recall data analysis, source evaluation, validity, uncertainty and the difference between a claim and evidence.

3. Concept teaching

A scientific argument is not a debate opinion. It includes a specific claim, relevant evidence and reasoning that explains why that evidence supports the claim. Strong arguments also acknowledge uncertainty and evidence that does not fit perfectly.

Secondary data must be traceable. Cite the original source where possible and check who collected the data, how, when and for what purpose. Ethical use includes permissions, privacy, attribution and—where culturally governed knowledge or data are involved—appropriate community protocols. Publicly visible does not always mean ethically unrestricted.

4. Worked/modelled examples

Example 1 — claim/evidence/reasoning

Claim: insulation reduced heat loss in this setup. Evidence: insulated containers cooled more slowly across repeated trials. Reasoning: lower energy transfer to surroundings is consistent with the observed smaller temperature decrease.

Example 2 — conflicting evidence

Four high-quality datasets support a trend while one smaller dataset does not. A strong argument reports the conflict, compares methods and gives a cautious overall judgement rather than deleting the inconvenient source.

Example 3 — secondary data

A student finds an unattributed graph reposted online. Better practice is to trace it to the original dataset/report, verify context and licence/permissions, then cite the source used. If data relate to culturally governed knowledge, check the appropriate protocol before reuse.

5. Common misconceptions and corrections
  • An argument is just a confident claim. It requires evidence and reasoning.
  • More sources always mean stronger evidence. Relevance and quality matter.
  • Conflicting evidence should be omitted. It should be examined and weighed.
  • Anything online can be reused freely. Attribution, permissions, privacy and cultural protocols may apply.
6. Guided practice
  1. Label claim, evidence and reasoning in a short paragraph.
  2. Replace an irrelevant piece of evidence with a relevant one.
  3. Add one limitation to an overconfident argument.
  4. Trace a secondary graph to an original source and list citation details needed.
7. Independent practice
  1. Write a claim supported by a supplied dataset.
  2. Select the two strongest evidence items from four and justify.
  3. Add reasoning that links evidence to the science concept.
  4. Address one piece of conflicting evidence.
  5. Explain why source provenance matters.
  6. Describe an ethical/cultural protocol that could affect whether secondary data may be reused.
8. Reasoning/problem-solving

Three sources address the same claim: a large government dataset, a small school investigation and a social-media infographic with no source. Build an evidence hierarchy, explain how each can or cannot contribute, and state the strongest claim justified by the combined evidence.

9. Important questions and answers
  1. What makes a scientific argument? A claim supported by relevant evidence and scientific reasoning.
  2. How should evidence be weighted? By relevance, quality, method, consistency and uncertainty—not source count alone.
  3. Why cite secondary data? To preserve provenance, allow checking and acknowledge the work/data source.
  4. Why consider cultural protocols? Some knowledge/data are governed by permissions and responsibilities beyond ordinary citation.
10. Assessment-style questions + review hints
  1. Construct a claim–evidence–reasoning response from a supplied dataset.
    The reasoning must explain the scientific connection, not repeat the numbers.
  2. Evaluate three sources and decide which should carry most weight.
    Use method/provenance/relevance rather than authority labels alone.
  3. Revise an argument to incorporate conflicting evidence and ethical secondary-data use.
    Acknowledge uncertainty and cite/permission requirements explicitly.
11. Exit ticket/mastery check
  • I can build CER arguments.
  • I weight evidence quality.
  • I address conflicts/limits.
  • I use secondary data responsibly.

Exit ticket: What is missing from “The graph rises, therefore my hypothesis is correct”?

12. Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Assess evidence selection and reasoning separately. Include provenance/cultural-protocol scenarios so citation is taught as responsible data practice, not formatting trivia.

Parent/carer

Ask “what is your claim, what is your best evidence, and why does that evidence actually support it?”
