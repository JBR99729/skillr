YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Build the warrant and keep the limits visible AC9S9I07

Learning intention: Synthesise varied evidence into a qualified scientific argument, evaluate contrary evidence, and respect provenance, privacy and cultural authority when using secondary information.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Numbers support a claim only when reasoning connects them

A defensible argument distinguishes two claims. The saved 1990–2005 NOAA and NASA records support an overall increase in their respective quantities, but do not support “global temperature rises every year whenever CO₂ rises”. The 1998–1999 counterexample is relevant: CO₂ increases by 1.70 ppm while the anomaly decreases by 0.23°C. Greenhouse absorption and re-emission of infrared radiation provide a physical warrant for an influence on energy balance; they do not turn this short paired record into a complete causal attribution. The conclusion should integrate the trend, mechanism, counterevidence and scope, rather than hide an awkward year.

Learn the skill

1. Construct claim, evidence and scientific warrant

A claim answers the question. Evidence supplies relevant observations or measurements. The warrant explains why they matter using science. Varied evidence adds strength when it addresses different weaknesses; ten reposts of one table are not ten independent datasets.

Claim: both recorded quantities increase overall from 1990 to 2005. Evidence: CO₂ +25.53 ppm; anomaly +0.23°C. Mechanism: greenhouse gases affect outgoing infrared energy transfer.
Try it · What is missing from “The graph rises, so CO₂ caused all of the change”?

It jumps from a pattern to complete causal attribution. It needs the physical reasoning, treatment of other influences and uncertainty, and a narrower conclusion. The paired window alone cannot assign all change to CO₂.

2. Address alternatives and contrary evidence

An argument should test the strongest relevant alternative, not merely repeat a preferred position. A counterexample can defeat a universal claim while leaving a qualified relationship worth investigating. Compare methods and coverage, not source labels alone.

Counterclaim: “A rising CO₂ value guarantees a higher temperature anomaly in every following year.” The 1998 and 1999 pairs are 366.84/0.61 and 368.54/0.38, in ppm/°C.
Try it · Construct a qualified response.

The pairs contradict the guarantee: CO₂ rose while the anomaly fell. They do not by themselves reject greenhouse influence. Annual temperature has other influences; the overall 1990–2005 pattern and physical mechanism must be considered with those limits.

3. Make secondary evidence traceable and responsibly usable

Record creator, dataset/title, dates, variables, units, baseline, method, version/snapshot and a locator. Preserve context when paraphrasing or adapting. Citation does not substitute for privacy, licence or culturally specific authority and permissions.

Fictional separate case: a knowledge holder allowed a class to hear an explanation but expressly refused public posting. A learner proposes posting the notes with a citation.
Try it · Does attribution make publication acceptable?

No. Respect the refusal and the agreed purpose. Seek appropriate guidance only through the relevant school/community process; otherwise omit the restricted material and use an authorised alternative. Do not assume a universal protocol or disclose the withheld knowledge.

Common mistakes

“Evidence speaks for itself.” Explain the scientific warrant and the conditions of the claim.

“More sources must be stronger.” Check relevance, methods and independence.

“Citation grants permission.” Attribution, consent, authority and licence answer different questions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Construct and challenge an argument

Write a claim using the climate record, two quantified evidence statements, a scientific warrant and a limitation. Challenge the every-year counterclaim, then list the source metadata needed for checking. Explain how the refusal scenario changes what may be published.

Adjust the challenge Support: colour-code claim, evidence, warrant and limit in your own paragraph, also labelling them in words. Core: answer the counterclaim. Extend: compare a source with complete methods against an unattributed repost without assuming authority labels settle quality.

Use the supplied public-data snapshot and fictional permissions case. Do not collect personal information, reproduce restricted knowledge or contact anyone as part of this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Are the sixteen yearly pairs sixteen independent studies?

No. They are paired observations within two series with their own methods and dependence. Do not inflate evidence counts.

2. Can a quotation be accurate but ethically inappropriate to publish?

Yes. Accurate wording and a citation do not override a restriction on publication or sensitive personal/cultural information.

3. What makes an argument different from a list of facts?

A clear claim, relevant evidence, a reasoning link, consideration of limits and an appropriately qualified judgement.

FAQ

Why cite the saved version as well as link the live table?

The live dataset can be revised. A date/window and snapshot identity explain exactly which values were analysed.

Does an argument need a preferred policy recommendation?

No. It may explain what the evidence supports, what it cannot decide and which additional checks would help. Value choices should remain transparent.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Construct claim–evidence–reasoning arguments using more than one relevant source where appropriate.
  • Weight evidence by quality, relevance and consistency.
  • Address limitations or conflicting evidence rather than hiding it.
  • Access, use and cite secondary data ethically, including relevant cultural permissions/protocols.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall data analysis, source evaluation, validity, uncertainty and the difference between a claim and evidence.

Key concept

A scientific argument is not a debate opinion. It includes a specific claim, relevant evidence and reasoning that explains why that evidence supports the claim. Strong arguments also acknowledge uncertainty and evidence that does not fit perfectly.

Secondary data must be traceable. Cite the original source where possible and check who collected the data, how, when and for what purpose. Ethical use includes permissions, privacy, attribution and—where culturally governed knowledge or data are involved—appropriate community protocols. Publicly visible does not always mean ethically unrestricted.

Worked examples

Example 1 — claim/evidence/reasoning

Claim: insulation reduced heat loss in this setup. Evidence: insulated containers cooled more slowly across repeated trials. Reasoning: lower energy transfer to surroundings is consistent with the observed smaller temperature decrease.

Example 2 — conflicting evidence

Four high-quality datasets support a trend while one smaller dataset does not. A strong argument reports the conflict, compares methods and gives a cautious overall judgement rather than deleting the inconvenient source.

Example 3 — secondary data

A student finds an unattributed graph reposted online. Better practice is to trace it to the original dataset/report, verify context and licence/permissions, then cite the source used. If data relate to culturally governed knowledge, check the appropriate protocol before reuse.

Common misconceptions
  • An argument is just a confident claim. It requires evidence and reasoning.
  • More sources always mean stronger evidence. Relevance and quality matter.
  • Conflicting evidence should be omitted. It should be examined and weighed.
  • Anything online can be reused freely. Attribution, permissions, privacy and cultural protocols may apply.
Guided practice
  1. Label claim, evidence and reasoning in this fictional example: “Insulation reduced cooling in the matched setup. Insulated repeats lost 4 and 5°C while matched uninsulated repeats lost 9 and 10°C. Insulating material can reduce thermal energy transfer to the cooler surroundings.”
  2. Replace an irrelevant piece of evidence with a relevant one.
  3. Add one limitation to an overconfident argument.
  4. Trace a secondary graph to an original source and list citation details needed.
Independent practice
  1. Using saved Mauna Loa CO₂ endpoints 354.45 ppm in 1990 and 379.98 ppm in 2005, write an endpoint claim without claiming every year changed by the same amount.
  2. Claim: both recorded quantities rose overall from 1990 to 2005. Compare four items: A, NOAA CO₂ endpoints 354.45 and 379.98 ppm; B, NASA global anomaly endpoints 0.45 and 0.68°C; C, an unattributed “always warmer” slogan; D, a poll of favourite seasons. Select the two strongest relevant items and justify.
  3. Add reasoning that links evidence to the science concept.
  4. Address one piece of conflicting evidence.
  5. Explain why source provenance matters.
  6. Describe an ethical/cultural protocol that could affect whether secondary data may be reused.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Three sources address the same claim: a large government dataset, a small school investigation and a social-media infographic with no source. Build an evidence hierarchy, explain how each can or cannot contribute, and state the strongest claim justified by the combined evidence.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes a scientific argument? A claim supported by relevant evidence and scientific reasoning.
  2. How should evidence be weighted? By relevance, quality, method, consistency and uncertainty—not source count alone.
  3. Why cite secondary data? To preserve provenance, allow checking and acknowledge the work/data source.
  4. Why consider cultural protocols? Some knowledge/data are governed by permissions and responsibilities beyond ordinary citation.
Practice and review
  1. Construct a qualified response from saved 1998/1999 pairs: CO₂ 366.84/368.54 ppm and global temperature anomaly 0.61/0.38°C. Evaluate the claim that a CO₂ rise guarantees an anomaly rise every year.
    The reasoning must explain the scientific connection, not repeat the numbers.
  2. Evaluate three sources and decide which should carry most weight.
    Use method/provenance/relevance rather than authority labels alone.
  3. Revise an argument to incorporate conflicting evidence and ethical secondary-data use.
    Acknowledge uncertainty and cite/permission requirements explicitly.
Check understanding
  • I can build CER arguments.
  • I weight evidence quality.
  • I address conflicts/limits.
  • I use secondary data responsibly.

Exit ticket: What is missing from “The graph rises, therefore my hypothesis is correct”?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Assess evidence selection and reasoning separately. Include provenance/cultural-protocol scenarios so citation is taught as responsible data practice, not formatting trivia.

Parent/carer

Ask “what is your claim, what is your best evidence, and why does that evidence actually support it?”
