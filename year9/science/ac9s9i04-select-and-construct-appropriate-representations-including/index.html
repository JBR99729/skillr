YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Make the table, graph and relationship tell the same story AC9S9I04

Learning intention: Select and construct representations for a purpose, calculate useful summaries, and explain the assumptions and limits of mathematical models.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Unequal times need unequal horizontal spacing

An illustrative record gives total measured mass at 0, 2, 5 and 9 minutes as 100, 98, 95 and 91 g. A table preserves the exact pairs; a time graph makes the steady change visible. Place times proportionally, not at four equally spaced category positions. These values fit the simple relationship m = 100 − t when m is in grams and t in minutes: mass decreases by 1 g per minute over this supplied interval. This idealised model describes the record; it does not establish a universal process or justify extrapolation indefinitely.

Learn the skill

1. Choose and construct for the question

Use a table for exact lookup, a time graph for change, a scatter plot for paired numerical association and separated bars for categories. Label variables, units, scales and source. A continuous time axis must preserve actual intervals.

The four recorded times are 0, 2, 5 and 9 min.
Try it · Why would equally spaced positions labelled 0, 2, 5 and 9 mislead?

They would make 2-, 3- and 4-minute intervals appear equal and distort the apparent rate. Use a numerical axis with positions proportional to time.

2. Choose summaries while retaining the data

Mean uses every value; median locates the middle; range describes the distance between extremes. Each summary hides information, so keep the raw values and investigate unusual observations before excluding them.

Separate fictional repeat times: 4.0, 4.1, 4.2 and 8.1 s.
Try it · Calculate the mean, median and range, and explain the effect of 8.1 s.

Mean = 20.4 ÷ 4 = 5.1 s; median = (4.1 + 4.2) ÷ 2 = 4.15 s before reporting to a suitable precision (about 4.2 s here); range = 4.1 s. The high value raises the mean. It may be real or erroneous; investigate rather than delete it automatically.

3. Connect a model to units, domain and assumptions

Define equation symbols and check that the model reproduces the data. A rate is change divided by elapsed time, with units. Do not treat a fitted relationship as a cause, or use a valid short interval as permission for unrealistic distant predictions.

At t = 9 min, m = 91 g. The toy model is m = 100 − t over the recorded 0–9 min interval.
Try it · What is the endpoint rate, and why not predict a negative mass far later?

The rate of mass change is (91 − 100) ÷ 9 = −1 g/min, a loss rate of 1 g/min. Extrapolating beyond the supported interval can fail as conditions or available material change; negative mass signals misuse of the model.

Common mistakes

“The same numbers make any graph valid.” Spacing, variable type and labels also encode meaning.

“An unusual value must be wrong.” Check records and context before deciding on exclusion.

“An equation applies forever.” State its variables, assumptions and supported range.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Construct and reconcile representations

Draw a correctly scaled time–mass graph, label the four points and check each against the equation. Then calculate the three summaries for the four repeat times and explain what a summary cannot reveal on its own.

Adjust the challenge Support: mark every minute before plotting. Core: connect table, graph and equation. Extend: propose another representation for comparing categories and explain why this time graph would not suit it.

Use the supplied illustrative records on paper. No real mass-loss process or school experiment is being reported.

Three tricky questions

1. What mass does this model give at 5 min?

95 g, matching the supplied record.

2. Must every graph start its vertical axis at zero?

No. A clearly labelled restricted axis may reveal small changes. Explain the scale and avoid interpreting an exaggerated visual slope as a larger numerical change.

3. Does a line through all four values explain why mass fell?

No. It describes the relationship. A mechanism needs evidence about the system and what crossed its boundary.

FAQ

Why keep a table as well as a graph?

The table supports exact lookup and checking; the graph reveals pattern and rate more quickly.

Why not report every calculator digit?

Derived calculations can carry guard digits, but final reported precision must reflect the measurements and purpose.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Select a representation that fits the variable types and question.
  • Construct tables and graphs with correct labels, units and scales.
  • Use suitable descriptive statistics such as mean, median, range or percentage.
  • Explain what a representation reveals and what it can hide.
Prerequisite knowledge

Know independent/dependent variables, basic graph conventions, units and simple calculations of mean and range. Distinguish continuous numerical data from categories.

Key concept

Representation is a reasoning choice. A line graph is useful for continuous change in an ordered variable such as time; a scatter plot shows association between paired numerical variables; a bar chart compares categories; a table preserves exact values. Statistics compress a dataset but can hide distribution or anomalies.

Axes need variable names and units. Scales should reveal the data honestly. A mathematical relationship or model is useful only when supported by the observed pattern and stated domain; do not force a trend line through data merely because software offers one.

Worked examples

Example 1 — cooling over time

Temperature measured every minute is two continuous numerical variables. A line graph with time on the x-axis and temperature on the y-axis makes the change over time visible.

Example 2 — fertiliser categories

If three fertiliser types are categories and final plant heights are compared, a bar chart of an appropriate summary can show group differences. Keep raw/repeat data available so the summary does not hide variability.

Example 3 — mean versus median

For readings 10, 11, 11, 12, 40, the mean is strongly affected by 40 while the median is 11. Choosing a statistic requires attention to the data distribution and investigation purpose.

Common misconceptions
  • Any graph is acceptable if the numbers are correct. Graph type must fit variables and question.
  • The mean is always the best average. Outliers/skew can make median more informative.
  • A graph starting above zero is automatically dishonest. It can be valid if clearly scaled and interpreted, but may exaggerate visual differences.
  • Software chooses the science. Tools construct; students must justify the representation.
Guided practice
  1. Choose a graph type for time–temperature data.
  2. Choose a graph type for three habitat categories and species counts.
  3. For time–mass pairs (0 min, 100 g), (2 min, 98 g), (5 min, 95 g) and (9 min, 91 g), specify both axis labels and units for a new graph.
  4. Compare mean and median for repeat times 4.0, 4.1, 4.2 and 8.1 s; explain why the largest value needs investigation rather than automatic removal.
Independent practice
  1. Create a correctly headed data table.
  2. Choose and justify a representation for each context: temperature measured over time; paired leaf length and width measurements; species counts in three named habitat categories.
  3. Calculate mean, median and range for repeat times 4.0, 4.1, 4.2 and 8.1 s; distinguish calculation precision from reporting precision.
  4. Explain which statistic is most informative and why.
  5. Critique a misleading graph scale.
  6. Evaluate whether a fitted relationship is justified by the observed data range.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Two students represent the same data: one uses a table and the other a scatter plot with a trend line. Explain which representation is better for checking individual values, which is better for seeing association, and what additional evidence is needed before treating the trend as a causal relationship.

Questions and answers
  1. How do I choose a graph? Start with variable type and the scientific question.
  2. Why include units? A number without its measured quantity/unit can be scientifically ambiguous.
  3. Why use statistics? To summarise central tendency or spread, while recognising what summary loses.
  4. Does a trend line prove a relationship? It describes a pattern; causation requires stronger evidence.
Practice and review
  1. Select and justify the best representation for a supplied investigation.
    Name the variable types and what the graph/table must reveal.
  2. Construct a graph from time–mass pairs (0 min, 100 g), (2 min, 98 g), (5 min, 95 g) and (9 min, 91 g), then identify one pattern.
    Label axes/units and use a sensible scale before interpreting.
  3. Compare two summaries/representations and evaluate which better answers the question.
    Discuss information gained and information hidden.
Check understanding
  • I choose representations purposefully.
  • I construct correct scientific graphs.
  • I select suitable statistics.
  • I can critique misleading/weak representations.

Exit ticket: When would a scatter plot tell you more than a table, and what could it hide?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Ask “why this representation?” before marking formatting. Give the same dataset with different investigation questions so students see that representation choice can change.

Parent/carer

Show a small table of household measurements and ask which display would make the pattern easiest to see—and what details would be lost.
