YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Keep raw measurements distinct from calculated results AC9S9I03

Learning intention: Select equipment by range and resolution, record traceable measurements with justified precision, and distinguish repeated readings from useful independent samples.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Cumulative readings are not four separate delivered masses

An invented balance record uses a 200 g-range balance displaying 0.01 g increments. A receiving container is tared once, then left on the balance between four deliveries. Cumulative readings are 0.00, 9.98, 19.99, 29.97 and 39.98 g. Subtract successive readings: delivered masses are 9.98, 10.01, 9.98 and 10.01 g. Their total is 39.98 g. The calculated mean is 9.995 g, which can be reported as approximately 10.00 g at the original display precision; the calculation does not create a calibration certificate. Summing the cumulative readings would count earlier deliveries repeatedly.

Learn the skill

1. Match range, resolution and technique to the task

Range covers the expected total load, including the container; resolution describes the display increment, not guaranteed accuracy. Check zero/reference behaviour and use the relevant instrument procedure. A flask contains a specified fixed volume; a burette measures variable delivered volumes from reading differences.

Option A: balance range 200 g, increment 0.01 g. Option B: range 5 kg, increment 1 g. Container plus contents stays below 100 g.
Try it · Which better distinguishes deliveries differing by a few hundredths of a gram?

A has adequate range and finer resolution for that purpose. A reference check and technique still matter; finer digits alone do not demonstrate accuracy.

2. Preserve raw endpoints and show derivation

Keep instrument settings, units, trial IDs and observations with raw readings. Mark subtractions and averages as derived values. Do not add digits to raw readings because a spreadsheet can display them; keep guard digits during calculations and round the final report sensibly.

Delivery 3: before 19.99 g; after 29.97 g. A learner records “29.970000 g delivered”.
Try it · What two problems need repair?

The reading is cumulative rather than the delivery difference, and its extra zeros imply unsupported raw precision. The delivered mass is 29.97 − 19.99 = 9.98 g.

3. Choose useful sampling and digital settings

Record the device, mode, rate/interval, start/stop rule and sampling unit. Rapid serial points from one object are not independent objects. Useful sample size depends on the question, variability, selection, ethics and resources; no single number fits all investigations.

Hypothetical logger: one reading every 2 s from time 0 through 20 s inclusive, same sensor in the same cup.
Try it · How many readings are saved, and are they eleven independent cups?

There are 11 readings at 0, 2, …, 20 s, a rate of 0.5 readings/s. They describe one time series, not eleven independent cups. Sample different independent cups if cup-to-cup variation is the target.

Common mistakes

“Display resolution equals accuracy.” Accuracy also depends on calibration, conditions and method.

“Sum every displayed mass.” First determine whether readings are cumulative or separate.

“Many sensor points fix biased sampling.” A biased selection remains biased even with many serial measurements.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Reconstruct a traceable record

Recalculate each delivery and the total, label raw and derived columns and list the metadata another team would need. Design a separate sample of independent objects for a population question and explain how it differs from repeated measurements of one object.

Adjust the challenge Support: subtract neighbouring readings with a number line. Core: check total and mean. Extend: explain why treating final gross mass as contents without subtracting the container creates an offset, while subtracting successive endpoints cancels a constant container or zero offset.

Use the fictional data on paper. No laboratory delivery, calibration or sensor operation is claimed or required.

Three tricky questions

1. What is the range of the four delivered masses?

10.01 − 9.98 = 0.03 g; this describes spread in these values, not the instrument’s full uncertainty.

2. Can the first four cumulative readings be averaged to estimate a delivery?

No. They measure different accumulated totals. Use successive differences for the individual deliveries.

3. If the balance scale factor reads 1% high, will taking more repeats remove that bias?

No. A persistent scale-factor bias affects the differences too; more repeats do not remove it. By contrast, a constant additive container or zero offset cancels when successive endpoints are subtracted. Investigate reference checks and method rather than assuming all calibration errors behave alike.

FAQ

Why keep endpoints after calculating a difference?

They allow the calculation, offsets and transcription to be checked later.

Is one reading every 2 s always suitable?

No. Match the question’s timescale and the sensor’s response. Faster recording does not make a slow sensor respond instantly.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Select equipment with suitable range, resolution and measurement type.
  • Record units and significant measurement precision consistent with the instrument.
  • Explain why sample size and repeated measurements affect usefulness.
  • Choose digital tools when they improve precision, sampling or recording.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall SI units, reading scales, variables and repeat trials. Understand that precision describes measurement detail/consistency and does not automatically mean accuracy.

Key concept

Instrument choice must fit the expected values. A metre ruler marked only in 1 mm divisions cannot justify hundredths of a millimetre from its scale alone; a 10 mL measuring cylinder may be more suitable than a beaker for small volumes. Record only digits justified by the scale or sensor resolution.

A larger useful sample can reduce the influence of random variation and better represent a population. Repeats help estimate consistency. Digital sensors can collect many measurements rapidly, but they still require calibration, sensible sampling rates and correct units.

Worked examples

Example 1 — ruler resolution

If the smallest marked division is 1 mm, recording a length as 12.3476 cm invents unsupported detail. Record precision that reflects the instrument and method.

Example 2 — sample size

Measuring leaf length on one leaf cannot describe variation in a tree. A planned sample from multiple leaves/locations gives more representative data, provided sampling is not biased.

Example 3 — digital sensor

A temperature probe logging every second is useful for a rapid cooling curve. Logging 1000 times per second adds data volume without meaningful information if the process and sensor response are much slower.

Common misconceptions
  • More decimal places mean better data. Extra digits can be false precision.
  • A digital instrument is automatically accurate. Calibration and sensor limitations still matter.
  • One precise reading is enough. Repeats/sample size address variation.
  • More data are always better. Data must be relevant, well sampled and interpretable.
Guided practice
  1. Choose between a beaker, measuring cylinder and pipette for a 5.0 mL measurement.
  2. A digital balance displays in 0.01 g increments. Repair the raw-record entry 9.980000 g so it matches the display precision.
  3. Explain why five repeated timings are more informative than one.
  4. Select a useful digital sampling rate for a changing temperature graph and justify.
Independent practice
  1. Define range, resolution and sample size.
  2. Choose equipment for measuring 0.25 g, 25 mL and 1.5 m.
  3. A table records 9.980000 g from a balance displaying 0.01 g increments. Identify and repair the false raw precision.
  4. Design a sampling plan for plant height in a school garden.
  5. Explain how repeated measurements improve evidence.
  6. Evaluate when a digital sensor is preferable to manual readings.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Group A uses a high-resolution sensor on two samples; Group B uses a lower-resolution but adequate sensor on 30 representative samples. Decide which dataset better answers a population question and explain the trade-off between measurement precision and sampling quality.

Questions and answers
  1. What is resolution? The smallest change an instrument can meaningfully distinguish.
  2. Why repeat measurements? To reveal random variation and estimate a more stable result.
  3. Why use a useful sample size? To reduce over-reliance on unusual individual observations and better represent the target population.
  4. When are digital tools useful? When they improve appropriate collection, precision, frequency or recording—not merely because they are digital.
Practice and review
  1. Select equipment for three measurements and justify each choice.
    Refer to range/resolution and expected value.
  2. Critique a dataset containing excessive decimal places and only one trial.
    Separate false precision from insufficient repetition.
  3. Compare two sampling/measurement strategies for a field study.
    Balance representativeness, precision, feasibility and bias.
Check understanding
  • I choose equipment purposefully.
  • I record defensible precision.
  • I can justify repeats/sample size.
  • I evaluate digital-tool usefulness.

Exit ticket: Why can a very precise instrument still produce a weak investigation?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Make students state instrument resolution in practical work and justify sample size before collection. Penalise invented decimal places.

Parent/carer

Use a kitchen scale or ruler and ask what the smallest meaningful reading is and why writing extra digits would be misleading.
