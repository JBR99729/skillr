YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Explain how a response reduces a disturbance AC9S9U01

Learning intention: Compare nervous and endocrine coordination, trace stimulus–response pathways, and explain how negative feedback regulates a changing internal variable.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A cooling response can become smaller as the body recovers

In this simplified warm-body model, temperature rises above its usual regulated range. Temperature receptors provide information to the brain’s temperature-control centre. Sweat glands and skin blood vessels act as effectors: sweat evaporation and increased skin blood flow can increase heat loss when conditions allow. As the deviation becomes smaller, the drive for these corrective responses decreases. This is negative feedback because the response opposes the original rise, not because the response is harmful. It is an explanatory model, not a person’s medical record.

Learn the skill

1. Compare signalling systems

Nervous signals travel electrically along neurons and chemically across many synapses; they commonly produce rapid, targeted responses. Endocrine glands release hormones into blood, reaching cells with suitable receptors; many effects develop more slowly and last longer. These are general patterns with exceptions, not absolute timing rules. Circulation, nerves, glands and muscles cooperate.

Case A: a rapid withdrawal from a harmful stimulus. Case B: insulin carried in blood changes uptake and storage of glucose in target tissues.
Try it · Which signalling route is central in each case?

A mainly uses neural pathways to coordinate muscle action. B is endocrine signalling via blood to responsive tissues. Nervous and endocrine systems can interact, so classification does not mean either acts without other systems.

2. Trace the full response pathway

Name the detected variable, sensor, coordinator, effector and response. Effectors do the work; receptors detect change. In the cold, reduced skin blood flow can conserve heat and muscle shivering can increase heat production. A complete account explains how those actions affect the original temperature deviation.

Cold model: temperature falls, receptors detect the change, the brain coordinates shivering and reduced skin blood flow.
Try it · Which are the effectors, and how do their actions help?

Skeletal muscles produce shivering and skin blood vessels change blood flow. These actions increase heat production or reduce heat loss, opposing the fall. They do not make internal conditions match the colder air.

3. Explain the direction of feedback

Negative feedback opposes deviation and reduces its own stimulus as regulation improves. In a simplified glucose loop, high blood glucose stimulates pancreatic insulin release; uptake by responsive tissues and storage help lower glucose, reducing the stimulus for insulin release. Low glucose can stimulate glucagon, promoting liver glucose release. No clinical threshold or treatment decision follows from this model.

Faulty proposed loop: glucose is already high → more glucagon → liver releases more glucose → glucose rises further.
Try it · Why is the proposed response not corrective negative feedback?

It amplifies the original high-glucose deviation. A suitable high-glucose model instead uses insulin-mediated uptake/storage and shows the corrective stimulus decreasing as glucose returns toward range.

Common mistakes

“Negative means harmful.” Here it means opposing the original change.

“Homeostasis fixes one exact number forever.” Regulated variables fluctuate within suitable ranges.

“Hormones affect every cell equally.” Responses depend on suitable receptors and other conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build and repair a feedback loop

Draw the warm-body pathway with five named roles. Add a return arrow explaining how the original deviation and corrective drive change. Then rewrite the faulty glucose loop using the supplied explanation.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort role cards before connecting them. Core: explain every arrow. Extend: compare the consequences of a detection failure and an effector failure without diagnosing a real person.

Use paper models only. No temperature exposure, exercise test, blood measurement or medical decision is part of this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. If sweating increases but little sweat evaporates, is cooling guaranteed?

No. Evaporation and environmental conditions affect heat loss. Sweat production alone does not guarantee the model’s expected cooling.

2. Does every hormone act slowly?

No. Timing varies; some hormone responses are rapid. Use the general comparison without treating it as a universal rule.

3. Can a downward graph alone establish negative feedback?

No. Explain the stimulus, response and causal direction. A falling variable might have other causes, and a response must oppose the original deviation.

FAQ

Why include the circulatory system?

It carries hormones and redistributes thermal energy, helping effectors and control pathways work together.

Can one organ belong to more than one functional system?

Yes. For example, some reproductive organs also produce hormones. System labels organise functions rather than place organs into exclusive boxes.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Trace a response as stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response.
  • Compare nervous signalling with endocrine signalling by signal type, speed and duration.
  • Explain negative feedback as a response that opposes a change and moves a variable back toward its normal range.
  • Apply the feedback model to unfamiliar examples without claiming homeostasis means an exact unchanging value.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall cells, tissues, organs and organ systems; that organisms respond to stimuli; and that blood transports substances. You should be able to distinguish a cause from a response and read a simple flow diagram.

Key concept

Coordination is a system, not one organ acting alone

A stimulus is a detectable change. A receptor detects it. A coordinating centre processes information, and an effector such as a muscle or gland produces the response. The nervous system sends fast electrical impulses along neurons; the endocrine system releases hormones that travel in blood and usually act more slowly but for longer.

Negative feedback

Many internal variables fluctuate around a normal range. If a variable moves too far from that range, a corrective response opposes the change. As the variable returns toward range, the corrective signal reduces. This is negative feedback.

Worked examples

Example 1 — temperature rises during exercise

  1. Thermoreceptors detect the rise.
  2. The brain coordinates responses.
  3. Sweat glands increase secretion and skin blood vessels widen.
  4. Evaporation and greater heat transfer lower temperature.
  5. As temperature approaches its normal range, the corrective response decreases.

Example 2 — nervous versus endocrine response

Touching a hot object requires a rapid, targeted response, so nervous signalling is well suited. Regulation that depends on hormones carried in blood is slower and can last longer. The key comparison is not “better versus worse”; each system suits different coordination needs.

Example 3 — unfamiliar feedback graph

If a graph shows a variable rising above its usual range and a corrective response increasing shortly afterwards, identify the disturbance first, then explain how the response would oppose it. Do not call it negative feedback merely because the graph slopes down.

Common misconceptions
  • “Negative” means harmful. Correction: negative feedback means the response opposes the original change.
  • Homeostasis keeps values perfectly constant. Correction: values vary within suitable ranges.
  • Nerves and hormones do the same job in the same way. Correction: compare electrical neural signalling with chemical hormonal signalling.
  • One organ system works independently. Correction: regulation depends on interacting receptors, coordinators, effectors and transport systems.
Guided practice
  1. A student steps into cold air. Identify a likely stimulus and one receptor.
  2. Choose whether a rapid withdrawal reflex is mainly nervous or endocrine and justify the choice.
  3. Complete: stimulus → receptor → ______ → effector → response.
  4. Explain why reduced sweating as body temperature falls back toward range is consistent with negative feedback.
Independent practice
  1. Define stimulus, receptor and effector using one connected example.
  2. Compare nervous and endocrine coordination in a two-column table.
  3. Describe the temperature-regulation pathway from detection to response.
  4. Explain why homeostasis is dynamic rather than static.
  5. A response increases the original disturbance. Explain why this is not negative feedback.
  6. Apply the feedback model to regulation of water balance or another teacher-provided body variable.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A patient can detect a rise in body temperature but their sweat glands do not respond. Use the control-system model to locate the possible failure and predict the consequence. Give more than one plausible failure point and state what evidence would distinguish them.

Questions and answers
  1. Why is negative feedback important? It limits deviations from suitable internal ranges by opposing change.
  2. How do nervous and endocrine systems differ? Nervous signals travel rapidly along neurons; endocrine signals are hormones carried in blood and are usually slower and longer lasting.
  3. What makes an explanation complete? It links stimulus, detection, coordination, effector action and how the response changes the original variable.
  4. Does homeostasis mean “no change”? No. It means regulation within limits despite ongoing disturbances.
Practice and review
  1. Application Explain how the body responds when core temperature rises.
    Review hint: Name the receptor/coordinator/effector sequence and show how the response opposes the rise.
  2. Assessment standard Compare nervous and endocrine coordination and give one situation in which each is useful.
    Review hint: Compare signal, pathway, speed and duration rather than listing unrelated facts.
  3. Transfer Use the supplied warm-body pathway model to decide whether it describes negative feedback and justify your conclusion. Identify how the response changes the original deviation; a delay alone would not establish the feedback direction.
    Review hint: State what the response does to the original disturbance; timing alone is insufficient.
Check understanding
  • I can trace a complete stimulus-response pathway.
  • I can compare neural and hormonal signalling.
  • I can explain negative feedback without saying conditions are perfectly constant.
  • I can apply the model to a new regulation example.

Exit ticket: In four sentences, explain why sweating after a temperature rise is an example of negative feedback.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher guidance

Require every explanation to identify the changed variable and the direction of the corrective response. Use flow diagrams before introducing more detailed examples. Keep hormone detail to what is needed to explain coordination and feedback at Year 9.

Parent/carer guidance

Ask the student to explain one everyday response such as sweating or shivering using the five-part pathway. If they skip the receptor or effector, prompt them to identify who detects the change and who carries out the response.
