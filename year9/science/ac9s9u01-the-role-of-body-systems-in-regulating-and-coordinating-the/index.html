Body systems, coordination and regulation

This topic helps students understand how the human body detects change, communicates information and coordinates responses. It connects body systems to homeostasis, feedback and everyday examples such as temperature control and blood glucose regulation.

Core understanding

What students need to know

Key idea

The nervous system sends fast electrical messages through neurons.

Key idea

The endocrine system sends slower chemical messages called hormones through the blood.

Key idea

Receptors detect changes and effectors carry out responses.

Key idea

Homeostasis means keeping internal conditions within a suitable range.

Scientific language and representations

Useful rules and ways to represent the idea

stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response
negative feedback reduces change and helps restore balance
nervous responses are usually faster than hormonal responses
body systems work together rather than acting separately

Worked example

Worked example: temperature regulation

  1. Body temperature rises during exercise.
  2. Receptors detect the change and send information to the brain.
  3. The brain coordinates responses such as sweating and increased blood flow to the skin.
  4. Sweating and heat loss help lower body temperature.
  5. This is negative feedback because the response reduces the original change.

Common misconceptions

What to watch for

  • Thinking one organ system works alone without interacting with others.
  • Confusing hormones with nerves.
  • Thinking homeostasis means the body never changes.
  • Describing a response without explaining the stimulus or feedback pathway.

Quick check

Try these before moving on

  1. Name one body condition that is regulated.
  2. Compare nervous and hormonal communication.
  3. Explain why sweating is part of a feedback response.

Support tip

For parents and teachers

Use familiar examples such as feeling hot, being hungry or reacting to danger. Ask students to identify the stimulus, receptor, coordinator, effector and response.
