YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Change the explanation for the audience and preserve the result AC9S9I08

Learning intention: Create and revise scientific texts for a defined purpose and audience, selecting useful language, figures and digital features while retaining evidence and uncertainty.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two audiences need different wording for the same measured difference

An invented school record uses three independent matched paving blocks, each with one shaded and one unshaded region of the same surface material. Temperatures are recorded at the same stated midday observation using a consistent infrared measurement method; instrument settings and viewing conditions are held consistent within the comparison. Shaded readings are 28, 29 and 30°C; paired unshaded readings are 35, 36 and 37°C. The means are 29 and 36°C, a 7°C difference. A committee summary can foreground the paired method and limitations. A Year 6 explanation can define surface temperature plainly. Both must retain the numbers and explain that this record does not directly measure human comfort or all future weather. The pairs are linked within blocks, not six independent observations.

Committee version

Across three matched paving blocks at the stated midday observation, mean surface temperature was 29°C in shaded regions and 36°C in paired unshaded regions. This 7°C contrast describes these conditions; comfort, installation cost and other weather were not measured.

Year 6 version

The shaded paving surfaces were cooler in our invented comparison: 29°C on average, compared with 36°C without shade. That is 7°C cooler at this observation. We measured the surface, so this does not tell us exactly how every person would feel.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Choose content for the purpose and audience

Ask what the audience needs to understand or decide. A scientific report needs sufficient method and evidence; a public explanation may lead with the finding and define unfamiliar terms. Adapt detail and structure without changing the result.

Purpose A: help a committee assess the strength of the comparison. Purpose B: explain the result to younger learners.
Try it · Which details change emphasis, and which must remain?

A emphasises matched units, procedure and missing evidence. B defines the measured surface quantity simply. Both retain the 29/36°C means, 7°C difference, stated conditions and limits; neither promises a universal result.

2. Give every text feature a scientific job

Tables preserve exact values; graphs reveal patterns; captions identify quantities, comparison and conditions. Use headings to organise reasoning and an equivalent text description for meaningful visual evidence. Do not rely only on colour to distinguish groups.

Weak caption: “Amazing shade!” The table supplies three paired surface-temperature comparisons at one midday observation.
Try it · Write a more informative caption.

Example: “Surface temperatures in three matched paving blocks at the stated midday observation. Shaded mean: 29°C; paired unshaded mean: 36°C; difference: 7°C. The record does not measure human comfort.”

3. Create, check and revise with suitable tools

Choose a spreadsheet for transparent calculations, a graphing tool for scaled axes or a document for a clear brief. Keep raw data and check the export: numbers, units, pairing, sources, legibility, reading order and alternative descriptions. A polished digital output can still be scientifically wrong.

Draft headline: “Shade makes every playground exactly 7°C safer.”
Try it · What revision preserves the evidence?

Example: “Shaded paving averaged 7°C cooler in this three-block comparison.” Add the measurement conditions and limit. Safety and universal playground effects were not measured; “exactly” overstates certainty.

Common mistakes

“Simpler wording permits simpler facts.” Simplify expression while preserving quantities, conditions and certainty.

“A figure needs no explanation.” A useful caption or description tells the reader what the evidence means.

“Software guarantees quality.” Check the science, calculation and exported reading experience.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create two useful messages from one record

Write a committee results paragraph and a younger-audience explanation using the paving table. Add a caption and a text description of your chosen visual, then annotate the audience choices. Check that both versions preserve the same finding and limitation.

Adjust the challenge Support: underline quantities and limits before rewriting. Core: produce two accurate versions. Extend: justify a digital tool and describe how you would check the exported result without claiming you have already tested it.

Use the supplied fictional packet. No hot-surface measurement, student survey, real publication or audience testing is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Does the record contain six independent paving blocks?

No. There are three independent matched blocks, each contributing a linked shaded/unshaded pair.

2. Can these temperatures tell us exactly how comfortable people feel?

No. Surface temperature is one quantity. Comfort also depends on other conditions and was not directly measured here.

3. Must a useful communication include many visuals?

No. Include features that serve the purpose. One clear table or graph with a meaningful caption may be sufficient.

FAQ

How much method detail belongs in a public explanation?

Keep the conditions needed to understand the finding and its limits. A brief version can link to a fuller method; it must not hide a limitation that changes the meaning.

What should change after checking a draft?

Repair any factual, numerical, audience or accessibility mismatch. Preserve the underlying result while improving wording, structure and features.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify audience and purpose before selecting a communication form.
  • Use scientific terminology accurately while explaining unfamiliar terms when needed.
  • Select tables, graphs, diagrams and digital features that contribute meaning.
  • Maintain evidence accuracy when simplifying or adapting a message.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall claim–evidence–reasoning, data representations, source citation and core features of practical reports, explanations and evidence-based arguments.

Key concept

Purpose could be to explain a mechanism, report an investigation, argue from evidence, advise a decision maker or inform a public audience. Audience affects assumed background knowledge, vocabulary and amount of detail. It does not justify changing data or exaggerating certainty.

Scientific texts use features purposefully: headings create structure, tables preserve exact values, graphs show patterns, diagrams explain relationships, captions state significance, citations establish provenance. Digital tools can support accessibility and interactivity, but decorative media that do not add scientific meaning can distract.

Worked examples

Example 1 — class report

For a teacher assessing an investigation, include question, method, processed data, analysis, conclusion and limitations with enough detail to evaluate the evidence.

Example 2 — public explainer

For a community audience, lead with the key finding, define specialised terms, use one clear graph and explain uncertainty in plain language. Keep the result accurate rather than removing caveats to sound certain.

Example 3 — decision brief

For a school leader choosing an energy option, compare relevant evidence, costs/benefits and uncertainty concisely. A recommendation should be traceable to evidence, not hidden behind visual polish.

Common misconceptions
  • Good science writing must sound complicated. Precision matters more than unnecessary jargon.
  • Simplifying means removing uncertainty. Uncertainty can be explained clearly without distortion.
  • More graphs/images always improve communication. Each feature should serve a scientific purpose.
  • Audience changes the result. Audience changes presentation, not evidence.
Guided practice
  1. Rewrite for younger learners: “At the stated midday observation, mean surface temperatures in three matched paving blocks were 29°C in shaded regions and 36°C in paired unshaded regions; human comfort was not measured.”
  2. Select a graph or table for a stated communication purpose.
  3. Replace the caption “Amazing shade!” using this fictional result: three matched paving blocks at one midday observation had shaded mean 29°C and paired unshaded mean 36°C; comfort was not measured.
  4. Identify the overclaim in “Shade makes every playground exactly 7°C safer”, given only a 7°C mean surface-temperature contrast across three matched blocks at one midday observation.
Independent practice
  1. Define purpose and audience for three science-text scenarios.
  2. Rewrite a jargon-heavy paragraph accurately.
  3. Choose two useful visual features for an investigation report.
  4. Write a clear public summary of the fictional three-block result: shaded mean surface temperature 29°C, paired unshaded mean 36°C at one midday observation, with no direct comfort measurement. Preserve the conditions and uncertainty.
  5. Transform the same evidence into a decision brief.
  6. Evaluate whether a digital interactive feature adds scientific meaning or decoration.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A dataset shows a modest effect with substantial uncertainty. Produce two communication plans: one for a Year 9 science class and one for a local decision maker. Explain what you would change and what evidence/caveats must remain identical.

Questions and answers
  1. What changes with audience? Vocabulary, assumed background, structure, detail and format.
  2. What must not change? The underlying evidence, data integrity and justified level of certainty.
  3. When should I use a visual? When it communicates a relationship, pattern, structure or comparison more effectively than prose alone.
  4. Why cite sources? To show provenance and let readers check evidence.
Practice and review
  1. Adapt a technical explanation for a younger/public audience without losing accuracy.
    Define terms and simplify syntax; preserve evidence/certainty.
  2. Design the structure and visual features for a scientific investigation report.
    Justify each feature by its communication job.
  3. Critique a persuasive science infographic that overstates a result.
    Identify the mismatch between evidence and wording/visual emphasis, then rewrite it.
Check understanding
  • I identify audience/purpose first.
  • I use scientific language accurately.
  • I choose visuals for meaning.
  • I preserve evidence and uncertainty.

Exit ticket: What can you simplify for a public audience, and what must you never simplify away?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Have students communicate one dataset twice for different audiences, then annotate every deliberate change. Mark scientific fidelity separately from presentation quality.

Parent/carer

Ask the student to explain one science finding first to you, then as if speaking to a scientist. Notice what changes while the evidence stays the same.
