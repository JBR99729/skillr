Rearrange the atoms without changing their identities AC9S9U07

Learning intention: Link word equations, molecular models and balanced equations, and explain conservation of mass within a clearly defined material boundary.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Three reactant molecules can form two product molecules

This original paper model uses two carbon monoxide molecules and one oxygen molecule to form two carbon dioxide molecules: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂. Before and after, there are two carbon atoms and four oxygen atoms. The total number of molecules changes from three to two, but the count of each atom type is conserved. Letters identify atom types; colours and joining lines are model conventions. This is a symbolic representation, not instructions to produce or handle these gases.

RepresentationWhat it communicates
Word equationCarbon monoxide + oxygen → carbon dioxide
Balanced formula equation2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂
Reactant inventory2 C and 4 O atoms in three molecules
Product inventory2 C and 4 O atoms in two molecules
Model limitSimplified grouping and colour; not natural colour, scale, reaction speed or a practical method

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Translate between representations

Words name substances, formulas state composition, and a molecular model shows a selected inventory and grouping. Identify reactants before the arrow and products after it. The arrow means forms or yields. In an ordinary chemical reaction, bonds and grouping can change while element identities remain conserved.

In 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂, a learner counts only two oxygen atoms on the left because they ignore oxygen in CO.
Try it · What complete oxygen count repairs the model?

The two CO molecules contain two O atoms, and O₂ contains two more: four on the left. Two CO₂ molecules contain four on the right. Count every formula, not only the obvious O₂ group.

2. Balance by count, adjust and recount

Keep correct chemical formulas fixed. A coefficient multiplies the whole formula; a subscript is part of the substance identity. Start with CO + O₂ → CO₂: left has three O atoms, right has two. Coefficients of 2 for CO and CO₂ give two C and four O on each side. Recount all elements and use the smallest whole-number ratio.

A student changes CO to CO₂ on the reactant side merely to make atom counts look closer.
Try it · Why is that invalid as a balancing step?

It changes carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide, a different substance. Balance the stated reaction by changing coefficients, giving 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂, without changing the formulas.

3. Check mass against the whole boundary

Consider a separate hypothetical gas-forming reaction record, not the CO model: a complete closed assembly measures 152.6 g before and after reaction. After some gas leaves the measured boundary, the assembly measures 151.8 g. The 0.8 g decrease is consistent with matter leaving, not atoms being destroyed. Quantifying gas mass exactly would also require ruling out spills, vapour and handling effects.

Closed assembly: 152.6 g → 152.6 g. After opening and gas release: 151.8 g.
Try it · What comparison tests conservation, and what does opening change?

Compare the same complete closed assembly before and after reaction. Opening lets matter cross its boundary, so the later assembly alone is no longer the same closed material system. The missing 0.8 g is not evidence that atoms vanished.

Common mistakes

“Balanced means equal molecule counts.” Count each atom type; molecules can regroup into a different total number.

“Subscripts are adjustable balancing numbers.” Changing them changes the substance.

“A falling balance reading proves mass destruction.” Check material crossing the boundary and measurement conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Rebuild and audit the reaction

Draw the three reactant molecules with labelled C and O tokens, then rearrange exactly those tokens into two CO₂ molecules. Add the word equation and an atom-count table. Explain separately why the open assembly’s mass change does not overturn conservation.

Adjust the challenge Support: inventory atoms before drawing groups. Core: justify each coefficient. Extend: explain how a gaseous reactant entering an open system can produce an apparent mass gain.

This is a paper-model task. Do not handle carbon monoxide, burn substances, mix chemicals or seal a gas-producing reaction for this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Are 4CO + 2O₂ → 4CO₂ atom counts balanced?

Yes, but the coefficients have a common factor of two. The smallest whole-number ratio is 2:1:2.

2. Does balancing force a real reaction to happen completely?

No. It represents the atom ratios of the stated reaction, not reaction speed, feasibility or complete conversion.

3. Can atoms change element identity in an ordinary chemical reaction?

No. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms and bonds. Nuclear changes, considered in a different topic, can change proton number and element identity.

FAQ

Why count oxygen from every formula?

The same element can occur in more than one reactant or product; conservation concerns the total count of that element.

Does an unchanged mass reading prove every part of the method is flawless?

No. Check calibration, resolution, leaks and other changes. Mass agreement supports the account but does not independently validate every assumption.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify reactants and products in word and symbolic equations.
  • Model reactions as atoms rearranging rather than appearing, disappearing or changing element identity.
  • Balance simple equations by changing coefficients, not subscripts.
  • Use a closed-system model to explain conservation of mass.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall elements, compounds, atoms, molecules, chemical formulas and evidence that a chemical change forms new substances. Be able to count atoms in a simple formula.

Key concept

During a chemical reaction, bonds and groupings change, but the atoms themselves are conserved. A word equation names substances; a particle model shows how particles rearrange; a balanced symbolic equation shows equal numbers of each atom type before and after.

To balance an equation, change whole-number coefficients in front of formulas. Changing a subscript changes the substance and is therefore not a valid balancing method. Conservation of mass is easiest to observe in a closed system where gaseous products cannot escape.

Worked examples

Example 1 — word to symbol

hydrogen + oxygen → water becomes H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Count atoms: oxygen is not balanced, so the equation needs coefficients: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Example 2 — never alter a subscript to balance

Changing H₂O to H₂O₂ would create hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Instead, keep formulas fixed and alter coefficients.

Example 3 — apparent mass loss

If an open flask reaction produces gas, the measured flask mass may fall because product matter leaves the measured system. In a closed container, total mass before and after remains the same within measurement uncertainty.

Common misconceptions
  • Atoms disappear when substances react. Existing atoms are rearranged.
  • Changing subscripts is a balancing method. It changes chemical identity; use coefficients.
  • If measured mass falls, conservation failed. Check whether matter escaped the chosen system.
  • A balanced equation means equal numbers of molecules. It requires equal counts of each atom type, not necessarily equal particle counts.
Guided practice
  1. Identify reactants/products in magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.
  2. Count each element on both sides of H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.
  3. Explain why changing H₂O to H₂O₂ is invalid.
  4. Balance H₂ + Cl₂ → HCl using coefficients.
Independent practice
  1. Write a word equation for the supplied reaction in which carbon monoxide and oxygen form carbon dioxide.
  2. Count atoms in 2CO₂ and 3H₂O.
  3. Balance Mg + O₂ → MgO.
  4. Balance N₂ + H₂ → NH₃.
  5. Explain conservation of mass using a particle model.
  6. Explain why an open-system experiment may appear to lose mass when a gas forms.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A reaction in an open beaker has a final measured mass 1.8 g lower than the starting mass. A student concludes that atoms were destroyed. Evaluate the conclusion, propose a plausible explanation and design a simple change to the experiment that would better test conservation of mass.

Questions and answers
  1. What changes in a chemical reaction? How atoms are bonded/grouped into substances.
  2. What stays conserved? The number of each atom type and therefore total matter/mass in a closed system.
  3. Why balance equations? To represent equal numbers of each atom type before and after.
  4. Why use coefficients? They change how many formula units participate without changing substance identity.
Practice and review
  1. Balance a simple equation and justify each coefficient using atom counts.
    Make a before/after atom-count table.
  2. Use particle reasoning to explain conservation of mass in a closed-system reaction.
    Say atoms rearrange; do not say mass is “made” equal by balancing.
  3. An experiment appears to lose mass. Evaluate whether this disproves conservation of mass.
    Define the system boundary and consider escaping gas/measurement uncertainty.
Check understanding
  • I can identify reactants and products.
  • I can count atoms accurately.
  • I can balance using coefficients.
  • I can explain conservation in a closed system.

Exit ticket: Why is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O a better representation than H₂ + O₂ → H₂O?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Move students through word → particle → symbolic representations. Require atom counts before algorithmic balancing and use sealed/open reaction comparisons to make system boundaries explicit.

Parent/carer

Use coloured counters as atoms. Ask the student to rearrange them into new groups without adding or removing counters, then connect that model to a balanced equation.
