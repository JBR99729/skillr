YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Make the method answerable, repeatable and responsible AC9S9I02

Learning intention: Align methods to questions, control and document error sources, and integrate risk, ethics and Country/Place responsibilities before conducting an investigation.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The unit being repeated matters as much as the number of trials

This paper plan compares two specified paper-spinner configurations. Each template is 20 mm wide with a 60 mm body and two 10 mm-wide blades; blade length is 30 mm in A and 50 mm in B. Split the blade region down its centre to the body line and fold one blade forward and the other backward at 90° to the body. Use the same paper grade, no clips and no additional folds. Release each constructed spinner without a push at 1.0 m above the same clear landing surface in still air. Time release to first floor contact, make five drops of each spinner in alternating A/B order, and record every trial. These are repeated drops of two objects, not ten independent specimens. Longer blades also change paper area and mass; this design compares the configurations, rather than isolating an aerodynamic mechanism. No trial has actually been performed.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Align the comparison and document the method

Record exact dimensions, construction, release-height reference point, timing device/resolution, start/finish rules and collection order. Another group needs the method, not instructions to obtain the same answer. For a later approved run, follow the method, record raw results immediately and document deviations.

Flawed alternative: use longer blades, change to heavier paper, drop from a different height and report only the fastest trial.
Try it · Which changes prevent a useful comparison?

Paper grade and height change alongside blade configuration, and selecting the fastest trial hides variation. Match those conditions, retain all trials and state the configuration comparison precisely.

2. Separate repeats, errors and risk controls

Repeated drops reveal variation from release and timing. They do not test variation among separately built spinners or remove a consistent timing offset. A safe plan identifies a harm mechanism, prevention and stop condition, then follows school approval and supervision.

Proposed release: stand on a chair while classmates move through the landing area.
Try it · How should the method change?

Use a teacher-approved release position reachable without climbing; clear and supervise the landing area and stop if it becomes occupied. Record timing uncertainty and use a consistent timing method. A warning alone does not fix the procedure.

3. Build ethics and local responsibilities into field plans

An approved school activity does not automatically authorise entry, sampling, moving artefacts, recording knowledge or public sharing. Identify the relevant local authority and requirements through school/community guidance before planning those actions; respect conditions and refusal.

Separate fictional field proposal: school approval exists, but a route may include a heritage place and no cultural/access or recording permissions have been checked.
Try it · What must happen before visiting or collecting?

The teacher must establish the appropriate local custodians/authority, land-manager requirements and proposed activities, seek necessary guidance/permissions and redesign if not authorised. Leave heritage sites and artefacts undisturbed and follow applicable local reporting guidance; do not invent one rule for every Country/Place.

Common mistakes

“Five drops are five independent spinners.” They are repeated measurements of the same constructed object.

“Repeats remove systematic bias.” A shared offset can persist through every repeat.

“Scientific interest grants access.” Authority and permissions depend on the place, activity and knowledge involved.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Audit a plan before conducting it

On paper, write a trial-record header for the spinner plan, identify two controls and two uncertainty sources, and repair the chair-release proposal. Then write the next responsible action for the fictional field proposal and an alternative if access is refused.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort validity, documentation, risk and permission decisions. Core: justify each repair. Extend: add independently constructed spinners and explain what new variation that would test.

This activity is a paper audit. Any later practical or visit needs teacher-approved equipment, risk assessment, supervision and relevant local permissions; no approval is supplied here.

Three tricky questions

1. What would five separately built spinners add?

They could reveal construction-to-construction variation, which repeated drops of one object cannot estimate by themselves.

2. Would identical times prove the method is valid?

No. Timing could be consistently biased or the question could exceed the design. Judge controls, outcome and claim separately.

3. May a found artefact be moved if replaced later?

Do not assume so. Leave it undisturbed, follow school and applicable local guidance and seek the appropriate authority’s direction.

FAQ

Does reproducible mean identical numbers?

No. Sufficiently clear methods allow others to check the result within relevant variation. State whether the check uses saved data or new measurements.

What if an unsafe condition appears during an approved run?

Stop and follow the agreed safety procedure. Document the change and review the plan; prior approval does not make changing conditions safe.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Design a method that tests the stated question and controls important variables.
  • Explain validity, reliability/reproducibility and sources of random/systematic error.
  • Write a method another group could follow consistently.
  • Build risk, ethics and cultural/heritage protocols into planning rather than adding them afterwards.
Prerequisite knowledge

Know independent, dependent and controlled variables; basic laboratory safety; measurement units; and why repeated measurements can reveal variation.

Key concept

Validity asks whether the investigation actually tests the intended relationship. If two important variables change together, causation becomes difficult to interpret. Reproducibility asks whether others can obtain compatible results using the described method. More repeats do not repair a fundamentally invalid design.

Errors should be anticipated. Random variation causes readings to scatter; systematic error shifts readings in a consistent direction. Risk controls reduce likelihood/severity of harm. Ethical requirements protect people, animals and environments. Work involving Country/Place, cultural heritage or community knowledge may require permission and respectful protocols; scientific curiosity does not override those responsibilities.

Worked examples

Example 1 — confounded design

To test temperature on dissolving time, one group also crushes the tablet at high temperature. Two variables now differ. Fix: keep tablet size/surface area constant while changing temperature.

Example 2 — reproducible method

“Heat it and time it” is too vague. Specify volume, temperatures, tablet mass/form, start/end timing rule, equipment, number of repeats and how data are recorded.

Example 3 — protocol before sampling

A field investigation proposes collecting material from a culturally significant site. Before sampling, determine access permissions, whether collection is appropriate, environmental impact and any relevant cultural/heritage protocols. Redesign if collection is not permitted.

Common misconceptions
  • More repeats make any experiment valid. Repeats improve confidence in consistency, not a confounded design.
  • Error means someone made a mistake. Measurement uncertainty and variation are normal features of data.
  • Safety is a form completed after writing the method. Controls must shape the method.
  • Permission is unnecessary if the purpose is scientific. Ethics and cultural protocols constrain acceptable methods.
Guided practice
  1. A proposed test changes blade length, paper grade and release height together, then attributes descent-time differences only to blade length. Identify the confounding changes.
  2. Rewrite one vague step so another group could reproduce it.
  3. Classify a zero-offset balance as systematic error and reaction-time variation as random error.
  4. Add one risk control and one ethical/environmental consideration to a field investigation.
Independent practice
  1. List variables for a reaction-rate investigation.
  2. Write a six-step reproducible method.
  3. Identify two likely error sources and their likely effect.
  4. Explain why three repeats cannot fix a poor control variable.
  5. Create a risk-control table for one investigation.
  6. Explain when permissions or cultural/heritage protocols may change a sampling plan.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Three groups test the same question. Group A controls variables but performs one trial; B performs ten trials but changes two variables; C controls variables, repeats five times and uses a poorly calibrated sensor. Rank the designs for different purposes and identify the highest-priority improvement for each.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes an investigation valid? Its design isolates/tests the intended relationship closely enough to answer the question.
  2. What makes it reproducible? Methods are clear and conditions/data collection are sufficiently specified for others to repeat.
  3. What is a systematic error? A consistent bias that shifts measurements in one direction.
  4. Why include ethics/protocols? Valid science must also be conducted responsibly and with appropriate permissions.
Practice and review
  1. Critique an investigation in which two variables change together.
    Name the validity problem and propose a precise control.
  2. Explain how repeats, calibration and a clear method affect evidence quality differently.
    Do not claim they solve the same problem.
  3. Redesign a field investigation to address risk, environmental ethics and relevant cultural protocols.
    Show how the method itself changes, not just a warning added at the end.
Check understanding
  • I can diagnose validity problems.
  • I can write reproducible steps.
  • I distinguish random and systematic error.
  • I integrate risk, ethics and protocols into design.

Exit ticket: Why can a highly repeatable experiment still be invalid?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Give flawed methods for diagnosis before asking students to write their own. Require students to justify each control variable and discuss ethical/protocol decisions in the same planning document.

Parent/carer

Ask whether someone else could repeat the student’s method without asking questions, and what could accidentally change the result.
