Trace carbon through a connected network AC9S9U03

Learning intention: Represent carbon stores and transfers across the atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and geosphere, and explain how changed process rates affect the network.

WORKED EXAMPLE

An atmospheric gain does not create new carbon

In this original toy model, the atmospheric carbon store receives 11 arbitrary units from fossil-fuel combustion and 6 from respiration during one time step. Photosynthesis removes 9 units and net ocean uptake removes 5. The atmospheric change is 11 + 6 − 9 − 5 = +3 units. Other stores supply or receive that carbon, so the whole stated system can conserve carbon while the atmospheric store grows. These are invented model transfers, not measured Earth fluxes or current climate figures.

Learn the skill

1. Identify stores and their spheres

A store holds carbon; a process transfers it. Atmospheric carbon dioxide, living biomass, dissolved ocean carbon and fossil fuels illustrate the four spheres. Living algae are part of the biosphere even though they live in water. A store amount and a transfer per time step are different quantities.

Items: dissolved carbon dioxide; an algal cell; a limestone rock; carbon dioxide in air.
Try it · Assign each to the sphere relevant to its carbon store.

Dissolved carbon dioxide: hydrosphere. Algal cell: biosphere. Limestone rock: geosphere. Carbon dioxide in air: atmosphere. Location in water alone does not make every item a hydrospheric carbon store.

2. Label processes and conserve atoms

Photosynthesis incorporates carbon from carbon dioxide into organic molecules. Respiration returns carbon dioxide to air or water; plants also respire. Feeding moves organic carbon between organisms. Combustion rapidly transfers fuel carbon to products including carbon dioxide. Burial and geological processes connect to longer-term stores; carbon does not follow one compulsory circle.

Trace: dissolved carbon dioxide → algal sugars → snail tissue → dissolved carbon dioxide → air.
Try it · Name the four transfers in order.

Photosynthesis, feeding, respiration and outgassing. The first crosses hydrosphere to biosphere, feeding stays within the biosphere, respiration returns carbon to water, and outgassing moves it to the atmosphere.

3. Explain changed rates and model limits

A reservoir changes when its total inflow differs from its outflow. Increased fossil-carbon combustion can move carbon out of geological stores faster, while other processes also vary. Ocean exchange can run in both directions; “net uptake” already subtracts outgassing. Toy tokens help trace conservation but cannot establish actual global quantities, timescales or policy effects.

Keep the toy inflows at 17 units. Increase photosynthesis from 9 to 12 units while net ocean uptake remains 5.
Try it · What happens to the toy atmospheric store?

Outflow becomes 12 + 5 = 17 units, matching inflow, so its net change is zero for that step. This is conditional bookkeeping, not evidence that a particular real intervention will exactly balance Earth’s carbon cycle.

Common mistakes

“Combustion creates carbon.” It rearranges carbon atoms already present in fuel.

“Plants only take carbon dioxide in.” Plants also respire and release carbon dioxide.

“Net ocean uptake means no outgassing.” Net is the balance of opposing transfers.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Audit and redraw a carbon account

Draw four sphere-labelled stores and at least five process-labelled arrows. Calculate the toy atmospheric change and identify which other stores lose or gain carbon. Explain one limitation of the chosen boundary and why energy does not cycle in the same way as these carbon tokens.

Adjust the challenge Support: distinguish boxes from arrows first. Core: trace two different routes for one carbon atom. Extend: explain why a balanced atmospheric step does not mean every reservoir has an unchanged size.

Use paper tokens and supplied model quantities. No burning, field sampling or current emissions calculation is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Can carbon stay in a store for many steps?

Yes. Different stores and processes have different residence times; a useful network includes storage as well as movement.

2. Should respiration from marine organisms always point straight to air?

No. Carbon dioxide can first enter surrounding water. Label the relevant medium and any later exchange.

3. Does a +3 atmospheric change violate conservation?

No. Carbon can enter that selected store from other stores. Check the complete boundary before judging total conservation.

FAQ

Why use arbitrary units?

They make the original model’s accounting explicit without pretending to supply measured Earth quantities.

Is the cycle only photosynthesis and respiration?

No. Combustion, feeding, decomposition, ocean exchange and geological pathways also connect stores.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify major carbon stores in all four Earth spheres.
  • Name processes that transfer carbon, including photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion and ocean exchange.
  • Trace a carbon atom through several stores without treating the cycle as one fixed loop.
  • Predict consequences when human activity changes a carbon transfer rate.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall photosynthesis and respiration at a word-equation level, food chains, combustion, and the atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and geosphere. Distinguish matter from energy.

Key concept

A store is where carbon is held; a transfer is the process moving it. Carbon dioxide in air is an atmospheric store; living biomass is biospheric; dissolved carbon is hydrospheric; carbonate rock and fossil carbon are geospheric stores.

Photosynthesis transfers carbon from atmospheric CO₂ into biomass. Respiration and decomposition return carbon to the atmosphere or water. Combustion rapidly transfers carbon in fuels/biomass to atmospheric CO₂. Oceans exchange carbon with the atmosphere, and geological processes act over much longer timescales.

The carbon cycle is a network, not a simple circle. Different pathways operate at different rates. Carbon atoms are rearranged into different substances; they are not created by combustion.

Worked examples

Example 1 — carbon from air to animal

Atmospheric CO₂ → photosynthesis → plant biomass → feeding → animal biomass. Each arrow is a process and each box is a carbon store.

Example 2 — burning fuel

Carbon already stored in fuel reacts during combustion and becomes part of products including CO₂. The carbon is transferred and rearranged, not newly created.

Example 3 — changing a transfer rate

If fossil-fuel combustion increases while other transfer rates remain similar, carbon moves from long-term geospheric stores to the atmosphere faster. A good answer states the affected stores and direction of change rather than saying “the cycle stops”.

Common misconceptions
  • Combustion creates carbon. Carbon atoms were already in the fuel.
  • The carbon cycle is one circular route. It is a network with several stores and pathways.
  • All pathways operate at the same rate. Biological, oceanic and geological processes span very different timescales.
  • Energy cycles like carbon. Matter cycles; energy flows and is transferred.
Guided practice
  1. Name one carbon store in each Earth sphere.
  2. Add a process label to an arrow from atmosphere to plant.
  3. Trace carbon from a plant to atmospheric CO₂ through two possible pathways.
  4. Explain how combustion changes the geosphere and atmosphere stores.
Independent practice
  1. Distinguish a carbon store from a carbon transfer.
  2. Explain roles of photosynthesis and respiration in opposite carbon transfers.
  3. Trace carbon through atmosphere → plant → animal → atmosphere.
  4. Explain one route by which carbon can enter the hydrosphere.
  5. Predict how reduced forest biomass may affect at least two carbon stores.
  6. Evaluate a diagram that shows carbon arrows but no process labels.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A region experiences extensive forest clearing and increased fossil-fuel use at the same time. Build a cause-and-effect explanation for likely changes to carbon transfers and stores. Identify which parts are direct consequences and which would require measured evidence to quantify.

Questions and answers
  1. Where is carbon stored? In atmospheric gases, living/dead biomass, water and dissolved carbon, soils, sediments, rocks and fossil carbon.
  2. What does photosynthesis do to carbon? It transfers carbon from CO₂ into organic molecules in producers.
  3. Why is combustion important? It can rapidly transfer stored carbon to atmospheric CO₂.
  4. Why is the cycle a network? Carbon can move among several stores by multiple pathways operating at different rates.
Practice and review
  1. Draw and explain a carbon pathway containing at least three Earth spheres.
    Label every arrow with a process, not only a direction.
  2. Explain how photosynthesis, respiration and combustion interact in the carbon cycle.
    State which store loses carbon and which gains it for each process.
  3. Predict how a sustained increase in combustion could alter carbon stores and explain why the change does not violate conservation of matter.
    Track existing carbon atoms; do not say carbon is created.
Check understanding
  • I can name four Earth-sphere carbon stores.
  • I can label transfer processes correctly.
  • I can trace carbon through a network.
  • I can reason about a changed transfer rate.

Exit ticket: Trace one carbon atom from atmospheric CO₂ into a plant and back to the atmosphere by two different pathways.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Make students name processes on every arrow and distinguish matter stores from energy transfers. Use one carbon token to force conservation reasoning before discussing large-scale human impacts.

Parent/carer

Ask where the carbon in food came from and where it can go next. The goal is a traceable chain, not memorising a poster.
