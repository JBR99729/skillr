YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Judge what a method can show before accepting its claim AC9S9I06

Learning intention: Assess validity and reproducibility separately, expose assumptions, and reconcile conflicting evidence with conclusions proportional to the methods and uncertainty.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The end temperatures do not isolate cup colour

A fictional report puts a black cup in a shaded room and a white cup in direct sunlight. After 10 minutes, their recorded temperatures are 28 and 32°C. It concludes that black cups always cool better. Location and illumination changed with colour, and initial temperatures, water volumes, cup materials, measurement procedure and repeat structure are not reported. The end readings describe these two arrangements; they do not isolate colour, establish temperature decreases or justify “always”. The 10-minute duration is supplied and must not be criticised as missing. A stronger plan would define the question and outcome, match important conditions, randomise positions, document the method and retain repeated raw results.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Separate validity from reproducibility

Validity asks whether the method addresses the question appropriately. Reproducibility needs enough detail for a meaningful check; a well-described confounded method can reproduce a misleading comparison. Name the specific check because terms vary across scientific fields.

A second team receives only: “Use the two colours, wait 10 minutes and read temperature.”
Try it · What separate criticisms apply?

Validity: exposure/location changes with colour. Documentation: initial conditions, quantities, cup properties and measurement details are missing. Repeating vague directions does not repair either problem.

2. Identify an assumption and its effect

End temperatures alone do not show how much cooling occurred. Assuming equal starts when they are unreported may change or reverse the inferred temperature decreases. A small spread in repeats would not eliminate a shared calibration or design bias.

The conclusion assumes that both cups started at the same temperature, but the report does not state any start readings.
Try it · What measurement would test that assumption?

Record each initial temperature and define the temperature-change calculation with matched exposure and other controls. Without starts, 28°C versus 32°C is an endpoint comparison, not a comparison of cooling amounts.

3. Use conflicts to refine the claim and next test

Compare methods, samples, conditions and uncertainty before deciding why results differ. Two outcomes can disagree because the settings differ. Do not treat conflict as proof of fraud, and do not suppress the inconvenient result.

Follow-up proposal: compare the same cup materials, equal starting water volumes/temperatures, matched illumination and measured independent repeats, with colour as the intended difference.
Try it · What could this improve, and what cannot be promised?

It can better test a colour effect under the specified conditions and reveal variability. It does not guarantee any preferred result or justify an all-cups claim. Record and report the actual outcomes, including conflicts.

Common mistakes

“Repeatable means valid.” A shared confound or bias can persist through repeats.

“A plausible explanation proves the result.” The method still needs to test competing explanations.

“Uncertainty means nothing is known.” It helps set the appropriate strength and scope of a conclusion.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write an evidence-weighted evaluation

Write two separate judgements on the cup report: method validity and reproducibility. Name an assumption, explain its effect and rewrite the conclusion. Propose a specific improvement and state what evidence would still be needed.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort reported facts from assumptions. Core: connect each flaw to its effect. Extend: distinguish recomputing saved data, collecting new data under the method and testing another context.

Critique the fictional record on paper. Do not heat cups, use sunlight exposures or carry out a practical without appropriate school approval.

Three tricky questions

1. Is the 10-minute interval missing?

No. It is explicitly given. Critique the information actually absent, rather than adding a false defect.

2. Can a larger sample fix different illumination between colours?

Not by sample size alone. The confound must be addressed in the design.

3. Does this report establish that black cups are ineffective in every context?

No. Failure to support the universal claim is not proof of the universal opposite.

FAQ

What is a better conclusion here?

In the two different arrangements, the reported endpoints were 28°C and 32°C after 10 minutes. The design does not isolate colour or establish cooling amounts.

Does an author’s commercial interest automatically make the measurements false?

No. Disclose and examine possible conflicts, methods and evidence; an interest alone does not establish fraud or invalidate every result.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Assess whether a method is valid for its question and reproducible by others.
  • Evaluate whether a conclusion is proportional to the data.
  • Identify assumptions, uncertainty and plausible alternative explanations.
  • Compare conflicting evidence without selecting only the result you prefer.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall controls, measurement error, repeats, patterns/anomalies and the difference between association and causation.

Key concept

Method evaluation asks whether procedures and measurements can answer the question fairly. Conclusion evaluation asks whether the claim follows from the evidence. A precise dataset can still come from an invalid design; a valid design can still have high uncertainty.

Look for sample bias, uncontrolled variables, measurement limitations, selective reporting and assumptions. When studies conflict, compare method quality, samples, contexts and uncertainty. Scientific conclusions should use calibrated language such as “supports”, “is consistent with” or “does not provide sufficient evidence”, rather than automatic “proves”.

Worked examples

Example 1 — invalid but consistent

A miscalibrated sensor gives nearly identical readings every trial. The data are consistent but systematically biased; consistency alone does not make the measurement valid/accurate.

Example 2 — overclaiming

An observational study finds students who sleep longer report higher concentration. The evidence supports an association, not proof that increasing sleep alone caused the difference.

Example 3 — conflicting studies

Study A has 20 participants and a large effect; Study B has 500 and a smaller effect. Compare design, sample selection and uncertainty before deciding which should carry more weight; size alone also does not settle every methodological issue.

Common misconceptions
  • Repeatable means valid. A biased method can repeat consistently.
  • A conclusion should restate the hypothesis. It must respond to actual evidence.
  • Conflicting evidence means choose the study you trust most. Compare methods and uncertainty systematically.
  • Uncertainty makes evidence useless. Uncertainty tells you how strongly to state the conclusion.
Guided practice
  1. A black cup is placed in a shaded room and a white cup in sunlight; their end temperatures after 10 minutes are 28 and 32°C. Identify a validity flaw in attributing the difference only to colour.
  2. Identify what would make the method reproducible.
  3. Rewrite “this proves” as a claim proportional to the data.
  4. List two reasons two studies might reasonably differ.
Independent practice
  1. Distinguish validity from reproducibility.
  2. A report uses only final cup temperatures of 28 and 32°C to claim that the first cup cooled more. Identify an unverified assumption needed to compare temperature decreases.
  3. Explain the effect of a biased sample.
  4. Evaluate whether an anomaly weakens a conclusion.
  5. Compare two conflicting evidence summaries.
  6. Write a conclusion that explicitly acknowledges uncertainty.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A company claim is supported by its own small experiment, while an independent study reports a smaller benefit and wider uncertainty. Construct an evidence-weighted judgement, specifying which methodological details you would need before accepting or rejecting the claim.

Questions and answers
  1. Validity or reproducibility? Validity asks whether the method tests the intended question; reproducibility asks whether others can obtain compatible evidence.
  2. What is an assumption? Something treated as true for the reasoning to work but not directly established by the data.
  3. How should conflicting evidence be handled? Compare quality, context and uncertainty rather than ignoring inconvenient results.
  4. Why state uncertainty? It sets an appropriate strength and scope for the conclusion.
Practice and review
  1. Method report: put a black cup in a shaded room and a white cup in sunlight, wait 10 minutes and record final temperatures of 28 and 32°C. Starts, volumes, cup properties and measurement technique are unreported. Evaluate validity for a colour-effect claim and reproducibility separately.
    Use different evidence for the two judgements.
  2. Critique a conclusion that claims causation from observational data.
    Identify the overclaim and at least one alternative explanation.
  3. Reconcile two conflicting studies into a cautious overall conclusion.
    Weight method quality, sample/context and uncertainty.
Check understanding
  • I assess method validity.
  • I assess reproducibility separately.
  • I locate assumptions/uncertainty.
  • I calibrate conclusions to evidence.

Exit ticket: Give an example of a result that could be reproducible but not valid.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Require separate “method” and “conclusion” judgements. Give conflicting mini-studies so students practise weighting evidence rather than hunting for one correct graph.

Parent/carer

When the student sees a strong claim, ask: how was it tested, who/what was sampled, what else could explain it, and how certain should we be?
