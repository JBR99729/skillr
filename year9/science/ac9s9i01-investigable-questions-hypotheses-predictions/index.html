YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Make a question testable and use results to refine a model AC9S9I01

Learning intention: Develop operational questions, distinguish explanations from observable predictions, and revise an explanatory model when evidence does not fit.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

An overall pattern can support one idea and challenge a simpler model

An invented record compares identical lidded cups with 0, 4 or 8 mm of the same insulating material. Each starts with 100 mL of water at 50°C in a 22°C room; after 12 minutes the temperature losses are 9, 6 and 5°C. A suitable question is: how does insulation thickness affect the temperature decrease after 12 minutes under these conditions? Insulation can reduce thermal energy transfer to the cooler surroundings, so a reasoned prediction is less cooling with thicker insulation. A separate constant-change model predicts that each extra 4 mm reduces loss by 3°C: it predicts 3°C loss at 8 mm, whereas the record gives 5°C. Revise that simple model; do not discard the whole insulation explanation. One record per thickness does not establish a general law.

Learn the skill

1. Generate an operational question

Name what is compared, what is measured, when it is measured and the relevant conditions. An investigable question can also be observational; changing a variable is not compulsory in every scientific inquiry.

Available record: the three cup thicknesses and temperature losses above, with equal initial temperature, water volume, cup and lid.
Try it · Why would “Does insulation work?” be weaker?

It does not define “work”, the comparison or timing. Naming temperature decrease after 12 minutes and insulation thickness makes the question answerable by this record.

2. Connect hypothesis, mechanism and prediction

A hypothesis is a tentative explanatory claim. A prediction is an expected observable result if that explanation and the stated conditions apply. Reasoning links the two. An if–then sentence without a scientific reason is not a complete explanation.

Hypothesis: insulation reduces transfer to cooler surroundings. Prediction: an 8 mm layer will give a smaller 12-minute temperature decrease than no layer, with other conditions matched.
Try it · What makes the prediction testable, and what does it not guarantee?

The losses can be measured and compared. The explanation supports the expected direction but does not guarantee an exact numerical loss or prove that every unmeasured condition was controlled.

3. Use a mismatch to improve the model

Compare the actual result with the specific prediction, then check measurement quality, controls and assumptions. A useful revision remains testable. Do not fit a universal formula to three unreplicated conditions.

Constant-change model: loss falls by 3°C for every extra 4 mm. Starting loss at 0 mm is 9°C; observed loss at 8 mm is 5°C.
Try it · What is the model’s 8 mm prediction, and what is a useful next check?

The prediction is 3°C, so it differs from the 5°C record by 2°C. Repeat matched tests and add intermediate thicknesses to examine diminishing benefit and variation; the mismatch alone does not establish the exact cause.

Common mistakes

“A hypothesis is the question.” It proposes a testable explanation rather than asks for one.

“A supported prediction proves the model.” Other explanations and untested conditions may remain.

“Unexpected results make inquiry useless.” They can reveal limits and guide a better test.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a question–explanation–test chain

Write a precise question from the cup record, a mechanism-based hypothesis and a directional prediction. Calculate the constant-change model’s 8 mm prediction, compare it with the record and propose one discriminating follow-up.

Adjust the challenge Support: identify comparison, outcome and timing first. Core: explain every link. Extend: design an observational temperature question and state its causal limits.

Analyse the supplied fictional record on paper. Do not heat water or test body temperature.

Three tricky questions

1. What is the 4 mm cup’s final temperature?

50 − 6 = 44°C.

2. Is the 8 mm result evidence that insulation increases cooling?

No. Its loss is smaller than the uninsulated loss. It challenges the constant-change numerical model, not the observed overall direction.

3. If cup size also changes, what becomes harder to infer?

The effect of thickness alone. Cup geometry and other changed features could affect thermal transfer.

FAQ

Must a hypothesis predict a number?

No. A clear directional or qualitative prediction can be testable; an exact number needs a justified quantitative model.

Can a question be investigable without being safe or feasible here?

A question may be scientifically testable in principle but unsuitable for a school investigation. Check practical, ethical and safety limits before choosing a method.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Rewrite broad questions into measurable, investigable questions.
  • Write directional predictions and testable hypotheses with scientific reasons.
  • Recognise when an inquiry is observational/model-based rather than a controlled experiment.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall variables, observations, measurements and the difference between a description and an explanation. Know that not every scientific question can ethically or practically be tested by changing one variable.

Key concept

An investigable question identifies evidence that could answer it. For a relationship test, name the independent variable (changed/compared) and dependent variable (measured). A prediction states the expected pattern. A hypothesis makes a testable relationship claim and usually includes a scientific reason.

Strong form: “How does water temperature affect the time required for a fixed mass of soluble tablet to dissolve?” Prediction: “As temperature increases, dissolving time will decrease.” Hypothesis adds why. Keep claims within what the planned evidence can test.

Worked examples

Example 1 — vague to investigable

“Does heat affect reactions?” → “How does temperature affect the time for a fixed reaction endpoint under otherwise controlled conditions?” Now both variables are measurable.

Example 2 — hypothesis

“With more of the same insulation around an otherwise matched cup, the temperature decrease after 12 minutes will be smaller because insulation can reduce thermal energy transfer to cooler surroundings.” The reason is scientifically linked to the predicted direction; this is a testable prediction, not a guaranteed result.

Example 3 — non-manipulative inquiry

“Is tree-canopy cover related to midday surface temperature across school locations?” can be investigated by systematic observation rather than deliberately changing canopy cover.

Common misconceptions
  • Any question is investigable. Evidence must be measurable/observable and feasible.
  • A hypothesis is a guess. It is a testable, reasoned explanation/relationship.
  • A prediction must be correct. It must be testable; evidence can contradict it.
  • All science requires changing one variable. Observational studies and model tests are also scientific inquiries.
Guided practice
  1. Identify IV/DV in a temperature–reaction question.
  2. Rewrite “Which fertiliser is best?” to define measurable growth and conditions.
  3. Add a direction to a prediction.
  4. Add scientific reasoning to turn the prediction into a hypothesis.
Independent practice
  1. Write an investigable question about light and plant growth.
  2. Name IV, DV and three controls.
  3. Write a directional prediction.
  4. Write a reasoned hypothesis.
  5. Convert a broad environmental question into an observational inquiry.
  6. Evaluate whether a proposed question can actually be answered by the planned data.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A student wants to test “Does social media harm sleep?” Design a Year 9-appropriate, ethical investigable question that avoids claiming more causation than the feasible data can establish. Explain what type of evidence it could and could not provide.

Questions and answers
  1. What makes a question investigable? It can be answered with obtainable, relevant observations or measurements.
  2. What makes a hypothesis strong? A testable relationship plus a scientific reason.
  3. Can evidence reject a hypothesis? Yes; that is a useful scientific outcome.
  4. Must every inquiry manipulate a variable? No; some investigate naturally occurring patterns.
Practice and review
  1. Rewrite a vague question into a measurable investigation and identify variables.
    Specify what changes/compares, what is measured, and enough conditions to make the question clear.
  2. For identical cups starting at 50°C with 100 mL water in a 22°C room, compare 0 and 4 mm of the same insulation after 12 minutes. Write a prediction and reasoned hypothesis about temperature decrease.
    Prediction = expected direction; hypothesis = direction plus scientific reasoning.
  3. Evaluate whether an observational dataset can establish causation.
    State what relationship it can show and what uncontrolled explanations remain.
Check understanding
  • I can identify variables.
  • I can make a vague question measurable.
  • I can distinguish prediction from hypothesis.
  • I can match claims to evidence type.

Exit ticket: Turn “Does temperature matter?” into an investigable question and hypothesis.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Do not reduce inquiry to an IV/DV worksheet. Include observational questions and ask what evidence can legitimately answer each question.

Parent/carer

Take an everyday “I wonder…” and ask: what could we measure, what would we compare, and what conclusion would that evidence actually support?
