YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Explain how needs become research priorities AC9S9H04

Learning intention: Connect societal needs and values to research questions, compare funding choices under constraints, and protect the evidence from a preferred outcome.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A limited budget makes the trade-off visible

A fictional research panel has 100 budget units. Project W would investigate local water-quality variation and costs 60 units; S would compare shade materials and costs 40; I would study building insulation and costs 55. Each project must be funded in full. W + S costs 100 and S + I costs 95, so either pair is feasible. W + I costs 115 and exceeds the budget. Choosing between feasible options requires stated priorities and evidence about need, feasibility and benefit. Cost alone does not establish which research is most valuable. These are invented proposals, not actual grant prices or promised results.

SWhich shade design reduces surface heating under matched conditions?40 unitsHeat exposure and accessible shared spaces

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Turn needs into researchable questions

A need describes a problem; a value describes what matters when responding. Water security may motivate monitoring, while fairness may direct sampling toward an underserved area. Community experience can reveal neglected questions, and scientific expertise helps decide what evidence would answer them.

Residents report that one neighbourhood has never been included in water monitoring.
Try it · How can this change research focus without deciding the result?

Include a justified question and sampling plan for that neighbourhood. Measure and report the findings honestly, including results that do not match residents’ expectations.

2. Compare stakeholders and funding criteria

Government, communities, industry and researchers can influence topics through funding, advocacy, expertise and access. Declare criteria such as urgency, equity, feasible methods, uncertainty and long-term benefit before judging proposals. No group’s preference makes a scientific conclusion true.

Panel A gives urgent local need greatest weight; Panel B gives long-term energy capability greatest weight.
Try it · Can the panels reasonably select different feasible pairs?

Yes. W + S and S + I are both affordable. Different transparent weights can justify different choices; each panel must explain whose needs are served and what is postponed.

3. State the opportunity cost and preserve evidence

Funding one question can delay another. A defensible decision explains this opportunity cost and how priorities will be reviewed. Changing methods to meet ethical requirements is legitimate; changing observations to satisfy a funder is not.

A sponsor offers to fund W only if the final report says every site is safe.
Try it · What should be challenged?

The predetermined conclusion compromises evidence standards. Funding can support the question, but the method, analysis and reporting must allow any supported result, with appropriate safeguards and disclosure.

Common mistakes

“Funding proves the answer.” Funding determines what can be investigated; evidence determines support for a finding.

“The cheapest project must come first.” Cost is a constraint and one criterion, not a complete account of value.

“Community concerns are unscientific.” They can identify important questions; proposed explanations still need testing.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Defend a transparent allocation

Choose W + S or S + I. State three criteria, explain the needs and values behind them, check the total cost and identify the research postponed. Write one reason to revisit the decision if new evidence or needs emerge.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort need, value, cost and evidence statements. Core: justify a feasible pair. Extend: explain how different legitimate weights could change the choice.

Use the fictional proposals only. No real grant application, public consultation or funding commitment is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Why can the panel not fund all three projects?

Their total cost is 155 units, exceeding 100. The projects are indivisible in this scenario.

2. Does unused funding make S + I invalid?

No. It costs 95, leaving 5 units. A justified allocation need not spend every unit, and 5 cannot fund another complete listed project.

3. Can a small affected community justify priority?

Yes. Severity, equity, neglected need and feasibility can matter alongside population size. Explain the criteria rather than assuming a universal ranking.

FAQ

Are priorities fixed forever?

No. New needs, evidence, capabilities and consultation can change which questions deserve resources.

Do values belong only outside science?

Values can shape questions and ethical methods. They cannot justify inventing or suppressing inconvenient results.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Explain why research priorities involve choices under limited resources.
  • Identify legitimate societal factors that influence research focus.
  • Separate “what should be researched” from “what evidence shows”.
  • Build transparent criteria for comparing competing research priorities.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that research requires funding, time, expertise, equipment and participants. Understand that evidence supports scientific conclusions, while values can influence decisions about goals and priorities.

Key concept

Societies influence research through government priorities, industry investment, community needs, public-health urgency, environmental concerns, ethics and advocacy. This influence is not automatically improper: choosing which scientifically worthwhile questions receive scarce resources is partly a social decision.

A defensible priority process makes criteria visible. Possible criteria include severity of the problem, number of people affected, equity, likelihood of useful results, cost, time, risk and long-term benefit. Values should not be used to change measurements or fabricate results; they can legitimately shape which questions are asked and funded.

Worked examples

Example 1 — emerging disease

A sudden outbreak can increase urgency and redirect researchers/funding toward diagnostics, transmission and treatment. The social need changes priority, not the rules for evaluating evidence.

Example 2 — environmental monitoring

A community experiencing repeated water-quality problems may prioritise local monitoring. Equity and direct impact can justify attention even if the affected population is smaller than another national issue.

Example 3 — competing programs

Program A has high potential benefit but high uncertainty; B has modest benefit but is ready to deploy; C focuses on an underserved group. A transparent decision compares them against stated criteria rather than pretending there is one purely scientific ranking.

Common misconceptions
  • Social influence always corrupts science. Societies legitimately choose priorities; evidence standards still matter.
  • The most popular issue should always get the most funding. Popularity is only one possible consideration.
  • Values can decide whether data are true. Values guide priorities/decisions, not measured evidence.
  • Funding priority equals scientific certainty. High-priority research can involve substantial uncertainty.
Guided practice
  1. List four resources that constrain research.
  2. Classify urgency, equity, evidence strength and cost as decision criteria and explain each.
  3. Explain why a small affected population might still justify research priority.
  4. Distinguish choosing a research question from interpreting its results.
Independent practice
  1. Define research priority.
  2. Give five societal factors that can influence research focus.
  3. Explain one legitimate role for values in research planning.
  4. Explain one place where values should not replace evidence.
  5. Create a four-criterion framework for comparing research proposals.
  6. Evaluate the claim “the most scientifically interesting question should always be funded first”.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A funding body can support only one project: reducing heat risk in disadvantaged suburbs, developing a higher-efficiency battery, or investigating a rare disease mechanism. Build and apply a transparent decision framework, then explain how a different value weighting could produce a different defensible choice.

Questions and answers
  1. Why do priorities exist? Research resources are limited, so not every question can receive equal attention.
  2. What can shape priorities? Need, urgency, equity, benefit, cost, risk, ethics, feasibility and public priorities.
  3. Do priorities determine scientific truth? No. Evidence and method determine support for scientific claims.
  4. Can two priority choices both be defensible? Yes, if they transparently weight legitimate criteria differently.
Practice and review
  1. Explain two ways society can influence scientific research focus.
    Use mechanisms such as funding, regulation, urgency or community need.
  2. Distinguish societal influence on research priorities from influence on scientific evidence.
    Priorities choose questions; they do not make results true.
  3. Compare two hypothetical research proposals using at least four explicit criteria.
    State criteria before choosing; acknowledge trade-offs.
Check understanding
  • I can explain resource constraints.
  • I can identify societal priority factors.
  • I can separate values from evidence validity.
  • I can justify a transparent priority decision.

Exit ticket: Why can society legitimately influence what scientists study without legitimately deciding what the data must show?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Use allocation scenarios rather than partisan cases. Reward transparent criteria, evidence use and recognition of trade-offs rather than one “correct” funding choice.

Parent/carer

Ask which research problem the student would fund with a limited budget and what fair criteria they would use to defend that choice.
