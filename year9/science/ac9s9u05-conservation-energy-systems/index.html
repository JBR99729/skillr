YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Account for every output before judging efficiency AC9S9U05

Learning intention: Apply conservation of energy to a defined system and analyse efficiency using consistent inputs, useful outputs and other transfers.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Seventy-five per cent useful still means all energy is accounted for

Fictional device A receives 360 J during a stated operating interval and transfers 270 J to its intended useful output, 72 J through heating and 18 J through sound. Assume no net increase in stored energy within the device during that interval. The outputs total 360 J, so energy is conserved. Efficiency is 270/360 × 100% = 75%. The other 90 J is transferred elsewhere; it has not vanished. Useful output depends on purpose: sound can be useful from a speaker but unwanted from a lifting motor.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Define boundary, interval and purpose

Name what is inside the system, what crosses its boundary, the time interval and the useful task. If internal stores change, include that change in the account. Transfers and transformations describe where energy goes or changes form; “wasted” means less useful for the chosen purpose, not destroyed.

A calculation uses electrical input over 5 seconds and mechanical output over 15 seconds.
Try it · Why is the proposed efficiency ratio invalid?

The values refer to different intervals. Match the boundary and interval, and include any change in stored energy, before comparing useful output with input.

2. Calculate the useful fraction

Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%. A consistent power ratio can also work when the same operating conditions apply. Do not divide joules by watts and call the result a percentage. A proportional Sankey diagram uses band thickness to represent energy amounts; all output bands must account for the input.

Use A’s supplied 270 J useful output from 360 J input. Input band thickness is 120 drawing units.
Try it · What thicknesses represent useful output, heating and sound?

Useful: 90; heating: 24; sound: 6 drawing units. Their total is 120. The scale is 3 J per drawing unit, and the efficiency remains 75%.

3. Make a fair comparison

Fictional B delivers the same 270 J of useful output for the same defined task using 300 J input, with no net stored-energy change. B’s efficiency is 90%, and 30 J goes to other outputs. A fair comparison needs equal purpose and output as well as compatible boundaries and conditions. Efficiency alone does not provide cost, speed or suitability.

A: 360 J input for 270 J useful. B: 300 J input for 270 J useful. No operating times or prices are supplied.
Try it · Which is more efficient, and what cannot be ranked from these records?

B is more efficient: 270/300 = 90%, compared with A’s 75%. The records do not rank operating speed, power, price or overall purchase suitability because the necessary information is missing.

Common mistakes

“Low efficiency means energy was destroyed.” Count the other transfers and any change in stores.

“All heating is waste.” Usefulness depends on the intended task and boundary.

“More efficient means faster.” Efficiency is a ratio; speed and power require appropriate time information.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Draw a proportional energy account

Use a 120-unit input band and split it into A’s three calculated outputs. Label the boundary, interval assumption and useful task. Compare A and B for the same 270 J useful output and write one question their efficiencies alone cannot answer.

Adjust the challenge Support: add outputs before calculating percentages. Core: preserve the proportional band scale. Extend: revise an account if the device also stores some input energy during startup.

Use fictional records and paper diagrams. No appliance testing, electrical connection or heating experiment is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Can an ideal model have 100% efficiency?

Yes, if all input becomes the defined useful output with no other transfers or store change. Real systems must be measured and their boundaries specified; conservation alone does not make every device 100% efficient.

2. If a motor is hotter at one point, must it be less efficient?

No. Local temperature depends on cooling, material and operating history as well as energy transfers. Measure the complete matched input/useful-output account.

3. If a startup interval has 100 J input, 70 J output and 30 J stored internally, is conservation broken?

No. Input equals outward transfer plus the increase in internal stores: 100 = 70 + 30 J. The steady-operation assumption used for A would not apply to that interval.

FAQ

Why show sound separately?

It is a transfer in waves and can serve a different purpose from mechanical output or heating. Do not count the same energy twice after sound is absorbed elsewhere.

Can I compare energy and power efficiencies?

Use like quantities consistently: energy divided by energy, or compatible useful and input powers. State the operating conditions and interval where relevant.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Apply conservation of energy to a clearly defined system.
  • Distinguish useful output from dissipated outputs such as heating and sound.
  • Calculate efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%.
  • Evaluate realistic design changes without confusing efficiency with speed or power.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall energy transfer and transformation, joules, kinetic/potential energy and heating. You should be comfortable with percentages and ratios.

Key concept

Conservation means total energy is accounted for when the system boundary and time interval are consistent. A device can be inefficient even though energy is conserved because some output is not useful for the intended purpose.

Efficiency = useful output / total input × 100%. If a motor receives 200 J and gives 140 J useful movement, the other 60 J must appear in other transfers such as heating and sound. For the same system boundary, efficiency cannot exceed 100%.

Worked examples

Example 1 — calculate efficiency

Input = 200 J; useful output = 140 J. Efficiency = 140/200 × 100% = 70%. Dissipated output = 60 J.

Example 2 — identify a boundary error

A student compares electrical input measured for 10 s with useful output measured for 20 s. The calculation is invalid because the energy values refer to different intervals. Match system boundary and interval first.

Example 3 — improving a system

Insulating a hot-water system may reduce unwanted heat transfer to the surroundings. If the same useful heating requires less input, efficiency improves. Saying “it runs faster” is not enough.

Common misconceptions
  • Inefficient systems destroy energy. Energy is conserved; it becomes less useful outputs.
  • Efficiency can exceed 100%. Not for consistent energy input/output accounting.
  • Efficiency means speed. Efficiency is a ratio of useful output to total input.
  • Only useful output counts in conservation. All outputs must be included in the energy account.
Guided practice
  1. A lamp takes 100 J and produces 25 J useful light. Identify dissipated energy.
  2. Calculate the lamp efficiency.
  3. Explain why the remaining energy has not disappeared.
  4. Suggest one change that could increase useful output for the same input.
Independent practice
  1. Define system boundary and efficiency.
  2. A device receives 500 J and has 325 J useful output. Calculate efficiency.
  3. For the same device, calculate dissipated output.
  4. Explain why a 120% efficiency claim signals an accounting/measurement problem.
  5. Use the supplied device A and B records to compare their efficiencies for the same 270 J useful output.
  6. Evaluate whether reducing sound output necessarily improves efficiency for the device’s intended purpose.
Reasoning/problem-solving

Two motors produce the same useful mechanical output. Motor A draws less input energy but runs hotter at one measured point. Decide what can and cannot be concluded about efficiency and propose measurements needed for a fair comparison.

Questions and answers
  1. Is energy lost in an inefficient device? No; it is transferred into outputs that are less useful for the purpose.
  2. What is efficiency? The fraction/percentage of input energy transferred to the intended useful output.
  3. Why define the system? What counts as input and output depends on the chosen boundary and interval.
  4. Can efficiency be over 100%? Not with correct, like-for-like energy accounting.
Practice and review
  1. A pump receives 800 J and transfers 520 J usefully. Calculate efficiency and account for the remaining energy.
    Show the ratio, percentage and dissipated output.
  2. Explain why conservation of energy and low efficiency can both be true.
    Separate total energy accounting from usefulness.
  3. Evaluate a proposed design improvement that claims to double efficiency without changing measured input/output data.
    Use the definition of efficiency and identify what evidence must change.
Check understanding
  • I can balance an energy account.
  • I can calculate efficiency correctly.
  • I can explain dissipated energy.
  • I can evaluate a design claim.

Exit ticket: A 60%-efficient device receives 300 J. How much useful energy does it produce, and where might the rest go?

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Insist on a system boundary before calculations and make students account for the remainder after every efficiency problem. Do not mix energy and power values without converting/aligning intervals.

Parent/carer

Use a household appliance and ask what its intended useful output is and what unwanted outputs can be noticed as heat or sound.
