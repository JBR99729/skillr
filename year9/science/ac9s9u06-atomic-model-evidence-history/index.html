YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Connect new evidence to a revised atom AC9S9U06

Learning intention: Explain how subatomic discoveries changed atomic models, use particle notation accurately, and describe natural radioactive decay toward stable nuclei.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A new particle changes the explanation

Cathode-ray evidence supported a common negative constituent, the electron, contradicting a strictly indivisible atom. Rare large deflections in thin-foil scattering supported a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus. Later hydrogen-nucleus evidence identified the proton as a positive nuclear constituent, allowing the nucleus’s positive charge to be attributed to protons. Chadwick’s evidence for a neutral particle of about proton mass then refined the nuclear model further. These were different lines of evidence, not one experiment discovering every particle. A modern school model places protons and neutrons in the nucleus and electrons around it; it does not give literal planetary electron tracks.

Learn the skill

1. Explain revision through evidence

State the observation, an earlier model’s limitation and what a revised model explains better. Diffuse positive charge does not explain rare large alpha deflections well; concentrated nuclear charge does. Scattering is an interaction with charge, not proof every deflected particle physically hit a solid nucleus. Some successful parts of earlier models can remain useful.

A claim says: “The gold-foil experiment directly discovered electrons, protons and neutrons together.”
Try it · How should the claim be corrected?

Gold-foil scattering supported a concentrated positive nucleus. Electron evidence came from earlier cathode-ray work, while proton and neutron identifications involved later evidence. Connect each model revision to its actual evidence rather than merging the discoveries.

2. Use proton and neutron counts

Atomic number Z is the proton number and identifies the element. Mass number A is protons plus neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z. For a neutral atom, electrons equal protons. Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts. A mass number is an integer count, not the decimal weighted atomic mass often shown in a periodic table.

The supplied neutral silicon-28 and silicon-30 atoms both have Z = 14.
Try it · What differs, and why are they the same element?

They have 14 and 16 neutrons respectively, while both have 14 protons and 14 electrons. The shared proton number makes both silicon; adding neutrons does not create a different element.

3. Track natural nuclear change

An unstable nucleus can decay spontaneously. In beta-minus decay a neutron becomes a proton, with an electron and antineutrino emitted: A stays the same and Z rises by one. Natural carbon-14 becomes stable nitrogen-14 this way. Alpha decay removes two protons and two neutrons; uranium-238 becomes thorium-234, which is still unstable and needs further decay. Gamma emission lowers nuclear excitation without changing A or Z and need not end all possible decay.

Given parents: carbon-14 has Z = 6; uranium-238 has Z = 92. Carbon emits beta-minus; uranium emits alpha.
Try it · Give each first daughter’s A and Z and distinguish their stability.

Carbon-14 gives nitrogen-14 with A = 14 and Z = 7, a stable endpoint in this example. Uranium-238 gives thorium-234 with A = 234 and Z = 90; that first daughter is radioactive. Not every emission immediately produces a stable nucleus.

Common mistakes

“Atomic drawings are photographs.” They are models with stated purposes and limits.

“Isotopes have different proton numbers.” Isotopes share proton number and differ in neutrons.

“Every decay immediately produces a stable atom.” Some daughters continue through a decay series.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build an evidence and particle account

Make three evidence→revision cards for electrons, the nucleus and neutrons. Add the supplied silicon isotope counts and the two first-daughter calculations. Explain why chemical atom rearrangement and nuclear decay are different kinds of change.

Adjust the challenge Support: label A and Z before subtracting. Core: distinguish evidence from model pictures. Extend: explain why a gamma emission can change nuclear energy without changing the element.

Use paper records only. No radioactive source, detector experiment, chemical handling or medical decision is part of this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is the emitted beta-minus electron an orbital electron that simply fell out?

No. It is produced in the nuclear neutron-to-proton transformation, together with an antineutrino; it is not an electron removed from an atomic shell.

2. Can you predict the exact decay time of one unstable nucleus?

No. Individual decay events are random, although large populations show statistical patterns.

3. Does a gamma emission change the element?

No. Neither proton number nor mass number changes. It removes excitation energy; whether the resulting nucleus can later decay is a separate question.

FAQ

Must I memorise every date?

The central task is explaining how particular evidence exposed a model’s limits and supported a revision.

Can ordinary chemical reactions turn silicon-28 into silicon-30?

No. Those isotopes differ in neutron number. Ordinary chemical reactions rearrange atoms and bonds without adding neutrons to their nuclei.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals
  • Explain why scientific atomic models changed when new evidence appeared.
  • Connect discovery of electrons, the nucleus, protons and neutrons to revisions of the model.
  • Distinguish a model from a literal picture of an atom.
  • Explain that natural radioactive decay involves unstable nuclei changing toward more stable arrangements and is random for an individual nucleus.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall atoms, elements, protons, neutrons and electrons; atomic number; and that scientific models are simplified representations supported by evidence.

Key concept

Atomic models changed because observations did not fit older explanations. Dalton’s solid-particle model was useful for chemical combination but could not represent internal structure. Evidence for electrons led to a divisible atom. Rutherford-style scattering evidence supported a tiny dense positive nucleus. Later evidence for protons and neutrons refined the nuclear model.

Some nuclei are unstable. In natural radioactive decay, a nucleus changes and releases radiation, producing a different nuclear arrangement that may be more stable. The exact decay time of one nucleus is unpredictable; large samples show statistically predictable behaviour. This lesson does not require quantum orbitals or senior nuclear mathematics.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Rutherford evidence

Most alpha particles passed through thin foil, but a small number deflected strongly. A diffuse positive charge could not explain rare large deflections well; a concentrated positive nucleus could.

Example 2 — model versus reality

A diagram with electrons on neat circular paths can help show a nucleus and surrounding electrons, but it should not be treated as a photograph or literal track of electron motion.

Example 3 — random decay

For one unstable nucleus, we cannot predict the exact moment it will decay. For a very large sample, the overall fraction decaying over time can show a predictable statistical pattern. Random does not mean “without scientific pattern”.

Common misconceptions
  • Scientists replaced models because of guesses. Revisions followed evidence older models could not explain.
  • Atomic diagrams are literal photographs. They are purposeful models with limits.
  • Radioactive atoms decide when to decay. Individual decay is random, not intentional.
  • Random decay means nothing is predictable. Large samples show predictable statistical behaviour.
Guided practice
  1. Match electron discovery, scattering evidence and neutron discovery to a model revision.
  2. Explain why one surprising scattering result mattered.
  3. State one useful feature and one limitation of a simple atomic diagram.
  4. Explain the difference between an unpredictable individual decay and a predictable large-sample pattern.
Independent practice
  1. Order major atomic-model changes and attach evidence to each.
  2. Explain why finding electrons contradicted an indivisible-atom model.
  3. Use scattering observations to infer nuclear structure.
  4. Explain why discovering neutrons refined the nuclear model.
  5. Define radioactive decay at Year 9 level.
  6. Evaluate the claim “because decay is random, scientists cannot make useful predictions about radioactivity”.
Reasoning/problem-solving

A newly observed particle behaviour cannot be explained by a class model of the atom. Describe how scientists should respond: what evidence should be checked, what predictions the old and revised models should make, and why a model should not be abandoned after one unexplained observation without scrutiny.

Questions and answers
  1. Why did atomic models change? New evidence exposed limits of older models and supported improved explanations.
  2. What did scattering evidence support? A tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus with much of the atom being comparatively empty space.
  3. What is natural radioactive decay? A spontaneous change in an unstable nucleus accompanied by emitted radiation.
  4. Can we predict exactly when one nucleus decays? No; individual events are random although large samples have predictable statistics.
Practice and review
  1. Explain how one piece of evidence caused a change in an atomic model.
    State the observation, what the old model predicted and what the new model explained better.
  2. Compare the roles of electrons, protons and neutrons in the modern simplified atomic model.
    Location and charge are enough; do not drift into senior quantum detail.
  3. Explain why radioactive decay can be random for an individual nucleus yet predictable for a large sample.
    Separate individual events from statistical behaviour.
Check understanding
  • I can link evidence to model revision.
  • I can describe a simplified nuclear atom.
  • I can state a model limitation.
  • I can explain random decay versus population predictability.

Exit ticket: Give one example of evidence that forced an atomic model to change and explain the reasoning.

Teacher + parent guidance

Teacher

Teach the history as an evidence chain. Ask what each observation ruled out or made more plausible. Keep radioactive-decay treatment conceptual unless the local course deliberately extends it.

Parent/carer

Ask “what new evidence made the old picture inadequate?” after each model change. That question matters more than memorising dates.
