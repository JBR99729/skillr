IXL QUESTIONS/PATTERNS INVESTIGATED:
- X.9 presents two complete sentences and asks which is simple, compound or complex. Actual practice required discriminating a one-clause sentence from a sentence containing a dependent clause introduced by “while”. Two answers were submitted correctly; the counter progressed from 0 to 2 and SmartScore from 0 to 19. Prompts change the target class, while distractors include long simple sentences and sentences with compound predicates so length alone is unreliable.
- X.9 worked teaching defines clause, independent clause and dependent clause before contrasting simple, compound and complex structures. It explicitly warns that an introductory phrase does not create another clause. The solution names the subordinating conjunction, isolates the dependent clause and explains why the full sentence is complex.
- GG.1 uses a two-option choice to complete a sentence with the conjunction whose semantic relationship fits. One cause-result item was answered correctly. The worked sequence teaches and=addition/similarity, but=contrast, or=choice, so=cause-result, with examples connecting words, phrases and independent clauses.
- GG.2 requires selecting the conjunction directly inside a continuous sentence rather than choosing from a separate list. One actual item joining two noun phrases with “and” was submitted correctly. The worked solution names both the word and the equal units it connects.
- GG.3 requires selecting the subordinating conjunction inside a complete complex sentence. One actual contrast/concession item using “although” was submitted correctly. The worked explanation identifies the dependent clause and states that it cannot stand alone.
- GG.4 uses two alternative correlative pairs. The learner must infer the logical relationship: both true, both untrue, alternatives, or uncertain alternatives. One actual uncertainty item was answered correctly with whether/or. Worked teaching covers both/and, not only/but also, neither/nor, either/or and whether/or and stresses parallel/equal sentence parts.
- GG.5 changes the answer format to typed production. One member of a pair is supplied and the learner types its mate. One actual either/or item was completed correctly by typing “or”. The example uses but also as the cue for not only.

IXL DIFFICULTY PROGRESSION:
- Recognition begins with locating an overt conjunction or choosing between two semantically distinct options.
- Classification then requires clause-boundary reasoning and distinguishing phrases, compound predicates, two independent clauses and dependent-plus-independent structures.
- Correlative tasks add paired logic and parallel structure.
- Typed completion removes visible answer options.
- The mapped sequence supports conjunction knowledge and broad complex-sentence recognition, but the sampled IXL tasks do not fully require students to compose embedded relative, non-finite or content clauses that elaborate a noun or idea.

IXL MISCONCEPTIONS/TRAPS:
- Treating every long sentence as complex.
- Treating an introductory prepositional phrase as a dependent clause.
- Counting a compound subject or compound predicate as two clauses.
- Choosing a conjunction by familiarity rather than semantic relationship.
- Confusing coordinating and subordinating conjunctions.
- Selecting the wrong member of a fixed correlative pair.
- Ignoring parallel grammatical form after correlative conjunctions.
- Assuming any subordinate clause is embedded; AC9E6LA05 requires the additional Year 6 idea that a clause can be placed inside another clause or noun group to elaborate, extend or explain.

IXL LEARN-FROM-EXAMPLE INSIGHTS:
- Teach subject-plus-predicate and complete-thought tests before naming sentence types.
- Visually isolate clause boundaries.
- Link conjunction choice to meaning: addition, contrast, choice, result, time, cause, condition and concession.
- After an answer, identify the conjunction, name the clause types, and explain the relationship.
- Contrast phrases with clauses explicitly.
- Use paired examples where surface length is similar but clause structure differs.

- Prompt formats therefore include two-choice selection, multi-select-in-text, single-word completion and full verb-group production. Context sentences supply temporal, agreement and comparison cues rather than asking only for definitions.

IXL DIFFICULTY PROGRESSION:
- Recall regular and irregular simple forms, then coordinate tense with subject agreement.
- Move from selecting an auxiliary to producing an entire progressive or perfect verb group.
- Distinguish present/past/future meaning using discourse cues, including yesterday, all afternoon, by a later event and present relevance.
- Identify adverbs with different functions, then supply relative adverbs according to place/time/reason.
- Progress from regular one-syllable -er/-est and multisyllabic more/most patterns to spelling changes and irregular well/badly paradigms.
- Harder authoring should combine tense and adverb choices in paragraphs, remove explicit tense labels, and require explanation or revision for precision and effect.

IXL MISCONCEPTIONS/TRAPS:
- Copying the base verb unchanged; overgeneralising -ed to irregular verbs.
- Selecting a semantically plausible verb form that conflicts with time cues.
- Omitting the auxiliary, choosing the wrong be/have form, or confusing the -ing form with the past participle.
- Treating future perfect as ordinary future or past perfect.
- Assuming every -ly word is an adverb or missing non--ly adverbs and adverbs modifying adjectives/adverbs.
- Confusing where, when and why by focusing on the clause rather than its antecedent.
- Using more/most with short adverbs, adding -er/-est to long adverbs, or creating forms such as more better.
- Confusing adjectival good/bad with adverbial well/badly.
- Treating grammatical correctness as sufficient when the descriptor also requires deliberate choices that expand or sharpen meaning.

IXL LEARN-FROM-EXAMPLE INSIGHTS:
- Give meaning first (ongoing, completed before a reference point, place/time/reason, comparison), then a compact formation rule and completed sentence.
- Progressive tables align present is/am/are, past was/were and future will be with the -ing form.
- Perfect tables align present has/have, past had and future will have with the past participle.
- Identify what an adverb modifies and ask whether it answers how, when, where or to what extent.
- Teach comparative versus superlative by number of actions/groups compared; present one-syllable and multisyllabic formation in parallel tables.
- Isolate irregular paradigms well–better–best and badly–worse–worst.
- Feedback should name the cue, apply the rule, and rebuild the whole clause rather than merely reveal the word.
