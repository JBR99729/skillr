YEAR 6 SCIENCE · AC9S6U02

Connect Earth’s movements to observable cycles

Learning intention: Distinguish rotation from revolution and use a consistently tilted-axis model to explain changing daylight through the year.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The same tilt points a different way relative to the Sun

Earth and the other planets orbit the Sun. Earth also rotates on its axis. In the two-position model, the axes remain parallel: around June the Southern Hemisphere tilts away from the Sun, and around December it tilts towards it. At ordinary southern temperate latitudes, daylight is generally shorter around June and longer around December.

Learn the skill

1. Describe orbits and model limits

Planets revolve around the Sun at different distances and with different orbital periods. A drawn orbit is a representation of a path, not a physical track. A diagram rarely scales size, distance and time all at once.

Different things to compare

Planet diameter is not orbital distance. A planet’s rotation period is not its year. Read quantity labels and units before comparing.

Try it · If planets are drawn in one straight line, must they stay lined up as they orbit?

No. The arrangement may help display distances, but planets have different orbital positions and periods.

2. Explain the daily cycle with rotation

At most latitudes, rotation carries a place into and out of the sunlit part of Earth. The Sun does not need to circle Earth each day to explain the cycle. Polar regions can have continuous daylight or darkness at some times.

State the viewpoint

Earth’s eastward rotation appears anticlockwise from above the North Pole and clockwise from above the South Pole. The physical motion is the same.

Try it · Are clouds or the Moon required to cause night?

No. A place is in night when it is on the side facing away from the Sun. Clouds can reduce daylight but do not cause the daily cycle.

3. Combine tilt, revolution and latitude

Keep the model axis pointing approximately the same way in space as Earth travels around the Sun. Its tilt changes how long different latitudes spend in sunlight and the angle of incoming sunlight.

Southern Hemisphere comparison

Around December, southern temperate places generally have longer daylight than around June. The Northern Hemisphere has the opposite astronomical seasonal pattern.

Try it · Does daylight saving create an extra hour of sunlight?

No. It changes clock labels, not Earth’s tilt or the duration of sunlight. Compare records using a consistent time basis.

Common mistakes

“Earth’s tilt turns to point at the Sun throughout the orbit.” Keep the axis approximately parallel to itself over the year; its direction relative to the Sun then changes.

“Summer happens because Earth is closer to the Sun.” Distance alone cannot explain opposite seasons in the hemispheres. Tilt changes sunlight angle and duration.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain two positions of Earth

2 minutes: locate the Sun and parallel axes in the model. 4 minutes: describe which hemisphere tilts towards it at each position. 2 minutes: connect that to daylight length at southern temperate latitudes. 2 minutes: state one model limitation.

Adjust the challenge Support: use towards/away and longer/shorter labels. Extend: explain why a model with a pole always aimed at the Sun gives a misleading yearly cycle.

Use the supplied drawing or a cool battery-light model with adult guidance. Never look directly at the Sun or aim a light at eyes.

Three tricky questions

1. Does one complete orbit around the Sun cause each daily sunrise?

No. Earth’s rotation is the main motion explaining the daily light/dark cycle; revolution is the yearly motion.

2. Can we say every place has exactly 12 hours of day and 12 hours of night all year?

No. Daylight varies with latitude and date, including polar exceptions. The simple half-lit globe does not imply equal local time in each half.

3. Why should the axes be parallel in the two-position diagram?

Over one annual orbit the direction is approximately unchanged in space. This allows the tilt towards or away from the Sun to change with orbital position.

FAQ

Do all Australian places follow identical four-season weather patterns?

No. The astronomical model explains sunlight patterns; local weather and recognised seasonal patterns also depend on latitude and environment.

Are the diagram’s sizes and distances to scale?

No. Earth is greatly enlarged and the two positions are shown together for comparison, not as two real Earths.

What students learn in AC9S6U02

Learning intention: Use models to connect rotation, revolution and axial tilt to day, night, seasons and changing daylight length.

Curriculum focus: Describe the movement of Earth and other planets relative to the Sun and model how Earth’s tilt, rotation on its axis and revolution around the Sun relate to cyclic observable phenomena, including variable day and night length.

Success criteria

  • distinguish rotation from revolution
  • explain day and night using rotation
  • explain seasons and daylight variation using fixed tilt plus revolution while evaluating model limits

Investigation and reasoning routine
  1. Identify the observer and phenomenon.
  2. Choose rotation, revolution or tilt plus revolution.
  3. Track the location through light and shadow.
  4. Use because-language to connect motion and observation.
  5. State what the model represents and what is not to scale.

match motion and phenomenon

Task: A globe turns once while a fixed lamp represents the Sun. A marked city moves from the lit side to the dark side. Which movement is mainly responsible?

Model reasoning: Earth rotating on its axis Rotation carries locations into and out of sunlight, producing day and night.

causal explanation

Task: A globe turns once while a fixed lamp represents the Sun. A marked city moves from the lit side to the dark side. Which explanation is scientifically accurate?

Model reasoning: Rotation carries locations into and out of sunlight, producing day and night.

model interpretation

Task: A globe rotates beside a fixed lamp. What does this model represent well?

Model reasoning: The changing lit and unlit halves as Earth rotates. Models highlight selected relationships rather than copying every feature of reality.

model limitation

Task: A globe rotates beside a fixed lamp. Which limitation should be stated?

Model reasoning: The globe and lamp are not to scale and the turn may be much faster than one day. A useful model can still omit scale, time or three-dimensional motion.

reject misconception

Task: A student claims, “The Sun travels around Earth each day.” Which correction is best?

Model reasoning: Earth’s rotation makes the Sun appear to move across the sky; the daily cycle is not a daily orbit of the Sun around Earth.

predict cyclic pattern

Task: The marked city continues rotating from the dark half toward the lit half. What should the model predict?

Model reasoning: Sunrise will occur as the city turns into the illuminated half. Cyclic phenomena follow repeatable motion and orientation.

Curriculum coverage and concept boundary

Three distinct ideas

Earth rotates about once each day, producing day and night. Earth revolves around the Sun about once each year. Its axis stays tilted in nearly the same direction as it revolves.

Seasons and daylight

A hemisphere tilted toward the Sun receives more direct light and longer daylight; six months later it is tilted away. Distance from the Sun is not the cause of seasons.

Planet motion and models

Other planets also rotate and revolve with different periods. Globes, lamps, diagrams and simulations reveal relationships but usually distort sizes, distances, speeds or time.

Southern Hemisphere reasoning

Australia has longer days and summer when the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun. Northern and Southern Hemisphere seasons are opposite at the same orbital position.

Important questions and answers
  • What causes day and night? Earth’s rotation carries places into and out of sunlight.
  • What causes seasons? Earth’s fixed axial tilt combined with revolution around the Sun.
  • Does Earth’s tilt flip each season? No. The axis stays nearly parallel to itself as Earth moves around its orbit.
Assessment-style questions and review hints
  • In December, which orientation best explains summer in Australia? Hint: Use hemisphere, tilt, angle and daylight.
  • A lamp lights half a rotating globe. Why does a dot experience night? Hint: Track the dot through the lit and unlit halves.
  • Why can one still orbital diagram not show a complete revolution? Hint: Distinguish useful representation from omitted time.
  • Saturn takes much longer than Earth to orbit the Sun. What follows? Hint: Match year with revolution, not rotation.
  • Which model correctly shows Earth’s axis at four orbital positions? Hint: Look for parallel axis lines.
  • Why are daylight hours nearly equal at the equator across the year? Hint: Compare latitude effects without claiming exactly 12 hours every day.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: highlight the changed feature, observation and comparison before writing a claim.
  • Core: solve an unseen context and justify the answer with precise evidence.
  • Extend: evaluate a limitation, competing explanation or misleading model while staying within Year 6 science.
Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Explain one daily and one yearly cycle with the correct motion, then name one limitation of a globe-and-lamp model.

Evidence of mastery: The student distinguishes rotation, revolution and tilt, explains southern-hemisphere daylight and seasons causally, and evaluates a model without treating it as exact scale.
