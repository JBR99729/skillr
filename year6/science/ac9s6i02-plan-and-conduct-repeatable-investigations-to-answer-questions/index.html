Plan a comparison another group can repeat

WORKED EXAMPLE

“Water some plants more” is not a complete plan

Illustration shows contextual materials, not the treatment groups, measurements or results of an actual experiment.

This invented model starts with twelve already-emerged seedlings of the same species, each 20 mm tall. Four plants receive each daily water amount. Pot, soil, drainage, light, temperature and duration are matched; the pots are assigned to groups and their positions are randomised. Heights are measured from soil level to the shoot tip in the same way on day 9. These are teaching records, not a real class experiment or measurements from the illustration. A usable method must specify dose, schedule, measurement reference points, recording and care arrangements. Replicate plants help reveal variation; they do not repair an unfair setup.

Plan elementDetail needed
Change2, 6 or 12 mL per pot per day in the model
MeasureHeight change; number alive at the stated endpoint
ControlSpecies, starting size, pot, soil, drainage, light, temperature and time
ReplicateFour matched plants per setting; record each separately
Reduce position biasRandomly assign matched plants; randomise positions without changing other conditions

Illustrations provide context. Use the supplied labelled records and models for exact values; do not measure the artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Choose variables that answer the question

Identify the deliberately changed variable, measured outcomes and conditions to keep comparable. A controlled variable is not the dependent outcome.

Two outcomes, one comparison

Daily water volume changes. Height increase and the number alive are outcomes. Initial height, duration and environmental conditions are controlled.

Try it · If one group also receives a different soil, can height differences isolate water volume?

No. Soil and water both differ, so their separate effects cannot be identified from that comparison.

2. Specify procedure, recording and safety

State volumes, timing, reference points, equipment and repeat records. Decide how to handle spills, a moved pot or a missed dose, and record deviations honestly rather than silently correcting results.

Potential problemPlan response
Spilled water or wet floorStable tray; wipe spills with adult guidance
Soil handlingUse approved material and wash hands
Missed doseRecord the deviation and consider its effect
Inconsistent height endpointUse soil level to shoot tip every time
Try it · Does recording the same number four times replace four separate plant measurements?

No. Measure and record each plant. Copying a preferred value would hide variation rather than create replication.

3. Plan permissions before work on Country/Place

Before field investigation, use school and appropriate local First Nations and land-manager guidance to establish where and what is permitted. Respect refusals and restrictions; do not treat consultation as automatic consent.

Check each activity

Access, collecting, recording a knowledge holder and publicly sharing knowledge may require different permissions. Identify the relevant authority through local guidance before proceeding.

Try it · If access to a path is approved, is collecting plants automatically allowed?

No. Check the proposed collecting activity separately through the appropriate teacher and local authority, or use an approved non-invasive alternative.

Common mistakes

“More repeats remove every kind of error.” They cannot remove a consistent setup bias or a confounding variable.

“A repeatable procedure must produce identical values.” It provides enough detail to repeat the method; biological and measurement variation can remain.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Audit a short investigation plan

2 minutes: list changed, measured and controlled variables. 4 minutes: add exact dose timing, reference points and separate records. 2 minutes: pair one risk with a control. 2 minutes: explain a permission check needed if the study moves to Country/Place.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the supplied plan headings. Extend: distinguish replication from repeating a reading of the same plant and explain why each can be useful.

A paper plan is sufficient. Actual studies need adult-approved plant care, equipment, hygiene and required local permissions before starting.

Three tricky questions

1. Why randomly assign matched plants to groups?

It helps avoid systematically placing stronger plants in one treatment group. It does not guarantee every starting difference is removed.

2. Should a missed watering be hidden so the method looks consistent?

No. Record what happened and assess its effect on the evidence or follow-up plan.

3. Does a downloaded cultural resource grant permission to publish any local knowledge?

No. Use the resource within its terms and check relevant local authority and knowledge-holder permissions for recording or sharing.

FAQ

Must every investigation use this fair-test design?

No. Select observation, survey, modelling or a controlled comparison according to the question.

What details help someone repeat a measurement?

Define the quantity, equipment, unit, reference points, timing, conditions and how each reading is recorded.

What students learn in AC9S6I02

Learning intention: We are learning to plan safe, fair and repeatable investigations that can answer a scientific question.

Curriculum focus: plan and conduct repeatable investigations to answer questions including, as appropriate, deciding the variables to be changed, measured and controlled in fair tests; describing potential risks; planning for the safe use of equipment and materials; and identifying required permissions to conduct investigations on Country/Place

Success criteria

  • identify the changed, measured and controlled variables in a fair test
  • write enough method, measurement and repetition detail for another group to repeat an investigation
  • distinguish validity, reliability, measurement resolution and repeat-measurement precision when evaluating a plan
  • identify risks, safe controls and required permissions, including respectful work on Country/Place
Key vocabulary
independent variable
the factor deliberately changed in a fair test
dependent variable
the outcome measured or observed
controlled variable
another factor kept consistent so it does not confound the test
validity
how well a method fairly tests the intended question
reliability
how consistently a pattern appears across repeated evidence
precision
how closely repeated measurements agree with one another
resolution
the smallest increment an instrument can detect or display
hazard
a source or situation with potential to cause harm
risk control
an action that reduces the likelihood or consequence of harm
confounding variable
an uncontrolled factor that changes alongside the tested factor
control group
a baseline condition without the tested treatment
Concept model and worked thinking

Reliable routine

  1. Write the answerable investigation question.
  2. Identify changed, measured and controlled variables.
  3. Seek permissions and assess hazards, harms and controls.
  4. List equipment with quantities and suitable measuring resolution.
  5. Write an ordered method with exact setup, timing, units and repetitions.
  6. Prepare a labelled table, conduct consistently and record raw data.
  7. Check means, spread and anomalies; evaluate reliability, validity and limitations.

variables

Task: A class changes the length of a paper helicopter blade and measures its fall time. What is the changed variable?

Model reasoning: Blade length is deliberately changed. Fall time is the measured outcome, while height and paper type should be controlled.

variables

Task: Students test whether water temperature affects how quickly sugar dissolves. What is the measured variable?

Model reasoning: The outcome recorded is the time taken for the sugar to dissolve; water temperature is the changed variable.

controls

Task: When testing how ramp height affects toy-car distance, which factor should be controlled?

Model reasoning: Using the same car prevents car mass or wheel condition from providing another explanation for distance changes.

fair tests

Task: Describe a fair-test plan to compare fertiliser amount and bean growth; treat this as a paper planning task, not a fertiliser-use instruction.

Model reasoning: Use matched beans with comparable starting sizes, the same pots, soil, water, light, temperature and duration. Change only the teacher-selected fertiliser treatment, use several plants per setting and measure growth consistently. Any actual treatment must follow an approved safe plant-care plan.

equipment

Task: Which instrument and unit best measure 35 mL of water?

Model reasoning: A measuring cylinder is designed to measure liquid volume, and millilitres suit this quantity.

resolution

Task: Give an example of a length record justified by a ruler with millimetre divisions; no particular measured object is supplied.

Model reasoning: For example, a measured length of 83 mm can be recorded as 8.3 cm to the nearest millimetre. This is an example of supported detail, not a reading from an absent diagram. Extra digits would claim unsupported precision.

repeatability

Task: Which method detail most helps another group repeat a seed-germination test?

Model reasoning: Specify the actual volume, frequency and duration, together with seed count, conditions and the germination criterion. A planning example is “add the approved measured volume at the same time each day for seven days”, with that volume stated in the final method; this is not a universal seed-care dose.

repetitions

Task: Why test three paper bridges of each design instead of one?

Model reasoning: Repeated trials reveal natural variation and show whether the same overall pattern recurs; exact equality is not required.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The planning chain

A strong investigation links an answerable question to a prediction, variables, permissions, risk controls, equipment, numbered method, measurements, repetitions, table and evidence-based conclusion.

Variables and fair tests

The independent variable is deliberately changed; the dependent variable is measured; controlled variables are other factors kept consistent. A control group provides a baseline without the treatment where appropriate.

Make the method repeatable

State materials and quantities, exact setup, ordered actions, timing, measurement endpoints, units, instrument resolution and number of trials. “Do it again” cannot repair a vague method.

Measure and record well

Choose equipment suited to the quantity and record only the detail its scale or display supports. Prepare table headings with the changed condition, trial numbers, measured quantity and unit before collecting data.

Reliability, validity, precision and resolution

Reliability concerns whether a pattern is supported when an investigation is repeated; validity concerns whether the design fairly answers the question; precision concerns close agreement among repeated measurements; resolution is the smallest increment an instrument detects or displays. More repeats reveal spread but do not fix a confound.

Repetitions and unusual results

Use at least three trials where practical, calculate a useful mean and inspect spread. Investigate anomalies, repeat if possible and document decisions instead of silently deleting inconvenient values.

Risk and safe practice

Identify the hazard, possible harm and specific control. Use age-appropriate equipment, supervision and protective measures. Stop when conditions differ from the approved safe plan.

Permissions on Country/Place

Before fieldwork, seek required administrative and cultural permissions. Consult relevant Traditional Owners or land councils and follow guidance about sensitive locations, access, collecting, photography, data and sharing.

Choosing an approach

Fair tests suit causal questions; systematic field observations suit patterns in place; research supplies existing evidence; digital tools support data collection; simulations explore unsafe or inaccessible systems. Each approach has limits.

Plan critique

Ask: Can the question be answered? Is one factor changed? Are alternatives controlled? Are equipment, units and steps explicit? Are repeats sufficient? Are safety and permissions addressed? Does the table capture the needed evidence?

Common misconceptions
  • The controlled variable is the result. The dependent variable is measured; controlled variables are other influential conditions kept consistent.
  • All variables must be identical. The independent variable must change deliberately; the other influential conditions should remain consistent.
  • Repeatable means every result is exactly the same. Small variation is expected. Repeatability means enough detail to reproduce the method and examine whether a consistent pattern occurs.
  • Repeating a test makes it valid. Repeats support reliability but cannot repair a confound or systematically unfair comparison.
  • Public access removes the need for permission on Country/Place. Access, collecting, recording and sharing may require separate administrative and culturally informed permissions.
Important questions and answers
  • How many repetitions are enough? Use at least three where practical, and more when natural variation or measurement uncertainty is substantial.
  • Does a precise instrument guarantee a good test? No. Fine resolution does not guarantee valid design, accurate calibration or reliable results.
  • Should an unusual result be deleted? Not automatically. Check, repeat and document a defensible evidence-based decision.
  • Who can give cultural guidance for fieldwork on Country/Place? The relevant Traditional Owners or land council; authority and appropriate practice are specific to place.
Assessment-style questions and review hints
  • Name the changed, measured and controlled variables precisely.
  • Use quantities, units, timing and measurement endpoints in methods.
  • Separate comments about validity, reliability, precision and instrument resolution.
  • Link every risk control to a stated hazard and possible harm.
  • State and follow required permissions before work on Country/Place.
  • Use repeats and means without hiding spread or unexplained anomalies.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: work with one short example, highlighted evidence and a structured response frame.
  • Core: complete an unseen example independently and justify the decisive evidence.
  • Extend: compare plausible alternatives, explain limitations and create a new example within the Year 6 boundary.
Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Plan a safe paper-helicopter investigation with variables, controls, units, repetitions and enough procedural detail for another group to repeat it.

Evidence of mastery: The student plans a safe, permitted, fair and repeatable investigation, records suitable measurements and evaluates validity, reliability, repeat-measurement precision and instrument resolution as distinct qualities.
