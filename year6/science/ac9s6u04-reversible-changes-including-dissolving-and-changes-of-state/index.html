YEAR 6 SCIENCE · AC9S6U04

Compare changes using substance and recovery evidence

Learning intention: Explain state changes and salt recovery, then contrast changes such as cooking and rusting that form new substances.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Recovering salt differs from scraping off rust

Illustration shows contextual materials, not the treatment groups, measurements or results of an actual experiment.

Ice can melt and water can freeze again. In ordinary table-salt dissolving, salt remains present even when individual dissolved particles cannot be seen; evaporation can recover it. Rusting forms different substances from iron in the presence of water and oxygen. Scraping rust off does not simply reverse that reaction or restore the lost iron.

Learn the skill

1. Explain reversible changes of state

Melting and freezing change state without changing the substance’s identity. Evaporation and condensation can also be paired. A change caused by heating is not automatically irreversible.

Direction matters

Ice → liquid water is melting; liquid water → ice is freezing. Water vapour → liquid water is condensation.

Try it · Does an ice cube become a new substance when it melts?

No. It remains water in a different state. Cooling under suitable conditions can freeze it again.

2. Distinguish dissolving from disappearing

When ordinary table salt dissolves, its particles disperse in water. It has not vanished and it has not melted. A filter does not normally remove dissolved salt; removing water by evaporation can leave salt behind.

What is recovered?

Open evaporation can recover the salt, but it does not collect the water vapour. Recovering water as well requires an appropriate collection and condensation process.

Try it · If a clear solution leaves salt crystals after evaporation, did new salt have to be created?

No. The salt was already present in the solution. The recovery supports that it had not disappeared.

3. Use evidence for new substances

Cooking and rusting include changes that form new substances. Some events combine physical and chemical changes. Colour, gas or temperature changes may be clues, but a single clue does not prove every unknown change is a reaction.

Avoid an unreliable rule

Tearing paper is hard to undo, yet it need not form a new substance. Difficulty reversing alone does not identify a chemical change.

Try it · Does cooling toasted bread restore it to the original bread?

No. Cooling removes heat but does not reverse the food reactions that formed new substances.

Common mistakes

“All bubbles prove a new gas substance was made.” Boiling water also produces bubbles of water vapour. Use the specific process and evidence.

“Every metal forms rust.” Rust is associated with iron-containing materials; other metals can have different corrosion products.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write evidence-based comparisons

2 minutes: sort the four supplied cases. 4 minutes: explain one state-change pair and one salt-recovery method. 2 minutes: contrast rusting with melting. 2 minutes: explain why tearing paper challenges an oversimple reversal rule.

Adjust the challenge Support: use same substance/new substances and a specific recovery process. Extend: identify a familiar event containing both physical and chemical changes.

Use supplied observations or teacher-selected samples. Do not taste solutions, handle rusty sharp objects, heat materials or operate cooking equipment without an approved adult-led procedure.

Three tricky questions

1. Is salt required for iron to rust?

No. Iron, oxygen and water are involved. Salt can accelerate rusting under some conditions but is not a required ingredient.

2. Would an ordinary filter separate dissolved salt from its solution?

Not normally. Dissolved particles pass through an ordinary classroom filter; evaporation can recover the salt.

3. Does irreversible mean no advanced process could ever recover iron?

No. Here it means ordinary classroom reversal does not restore the original material. Later chemical processing is a different process, not scraping the rust away.

FAQ

Is every dissolving process identical?

No. This example concerns ordinary table salt in water. Some other substances can react when mixed.

Can heating cause more than one kind of change at once?

Yes. Toasting can involve physical water loss and chemical food reactions. Describe the particular change you are explaining.

What students learn in AC9S6U04

Learning intention: Use evidence about substance identity to compare reversible physical changes with irreversible changes that form new substances.

Curriculum focus: Compare reversible changes, including dissolving and changes of state, and irreversible changes, including cooking and rusting that produce new substances.

Success criteria

  • classify changes using evidence rather than appearance alone
  • explain dissolving and changes of state as reversible physical changes
  • recognise cooking and rusting as changes that produce new substances
Key vocabulary
reversible change
a change that can be undone by a physical process so the original substance is recovered
irreversible change
a change not reversed by simple physical processes, often because new substances form
dissolving
spreading a solute through a solvent to form a solution
change of state
melting, freezing, evaporation or condensation without changing substance identity
new substance
matter with properties and composition different from the starting material
evidence
observations used to support a classification
Investigation and reasoning routine
  1. Describe the before-and-after observations.
  2. Ask whether the substance identity changed.
  3. Look for combined evidence of a new substance.
  4. Test whether a physical process can recover the original material.
  5. State a classification with evidence or say evidence is insufficient.

classify change

Task: An ice cube melts and the liquid water is frozen again. How should this change be classified?

Model reasoning: A reversible physical change with no new substance formed. Only the state changes; the substance remains water.

use evidence

Task: An ice cube melts and the liquid water is frozen again. Which observation is the strongest evidence for the classification?

Model reasoning: The liquid can freeze back into solid water. This evidence addresses whether the original substance remains or a new substance forms.

recovery or reversal

Task: An ice cube melts and the liquid water is frozen again. Which follow-up action best investigates this change?

Model reasoning: Cool the liquid below its freezing point. This action tests reversibility, recovery, properties or the conditions involved in the change.

particle reasoning

Task: An ice cube melts and the liquid water is frozen again. Which particle-level description is most appropriate at Year 6?

Model reasoning: A change of state changes particle motion and arrangement, not substance identity.

reject misconception

Task: A student says, “All heating creates a new substance.” Which response is best?

Model reasoning: Melting can be reversed by cooling and does not create a new substance.

evaluate evidence

Task: The water looks cloudy after melting, but no further observations are recorded. What is the most scientific conclusion?

Model reasoning: Cloudiness alone is insufficient evidence that a new substance formed. Scientific classification should match the available observations and acknowledge missing evidence.

Curriculum coverage and concept boundary

The decisive comparison

Melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation and many dissolving processes keep the same substances and can be reversed physically. Cooking and rusting produce new substances and are not undone by simple cooling or reshaping.

Dissolving is not disappearing

A dissolved solute remains in the solution. Water can often be evaporated to recover crystals, although classroom safety and substance properties determine the method.

Evidence of reaction

A lasting new colour, gas, odour, temperature change or residue may support new-substance formation, especially when several observations agree. One visible change alone is not always decisive.

Separate simultaneous changes

A candle can melt physically and burn chemically. Metal can bend physically and later corrode chemically. Analyse each process rather than applying one label to the whole scene.

Important questions and answers
  • Is every heated change irreversible? No. Melting is reversible by cooling; cooking and burning involve new substances.
  • Does dissolving destroy a solid? No. Solute particles remain dispersed and may be recovered.
  • Is “hard to undo” enough evidence of a chemical change? No. The key question is whether a new substance formed.
Assessment-style questions and review hints
  • Wet clay is reshaped and later returned to a lump. What is the best classification? Hint: Ask whether a new substance formed.
  • Copper sulfate solution is safely evaporated by a teacher and crystals remain. What does this show? Hint: Follow both solute and solvent.
  • A candle wax pool forms while the wick burns. Which analysis is best? Hint: Analyse each observed change separately.
  • A powder is mixed with water and the beaker feels cooler. What can be concluded? Hint: Avoid certainty from a single sign.
  • Which comparison best tests whether water is needed for rusting? Hint: Change one proposed cause.
  • Which pair correctly labels liquid water → gas → liquid water? Hint: Track the starting and ending states.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: highlight the changed feature, observation and comparison before writing a claim.
  • Core: solve an unseen context and justify the answer with precise evidence.
  • Extend: evaluate a limitation, competing explanation or misleading model while staying within Year 6 science.
Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Classify melting ice, dissolving salt and rusting iron. For each, state whether a new substance forms and give one piece of evidence.

Evidence of mastery: The student compares unfamiliar changes using substance identity, recovery methods and cautious evidence, including cases where physical and chemical changes occur together.
