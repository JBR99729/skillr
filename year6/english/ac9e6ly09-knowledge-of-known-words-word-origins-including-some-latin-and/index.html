What students learn in AC9E6LY09

Learning intention: We are learning to use meaning, word structure, word history and spelling generalisations to build and check unfamiliar words.

Curriculum focus: Use knowledge of known words, word origins including some Latin and Greek roots, base words, prefixes, suffixes, letter patterns and spelling generalisations to spell new words including technical words.

Success criteria

  • I can identify a base, root, prefix and suffix when the word supports that analysis.
  • I can select an affix that matches the intended meaning.
  • I can apply final-e, y-to-i and consonant-doubling generalisations accurately.
  • I can use Greek and Latin roots and known-word analogies to build technical words.
  • I can verify uncertain spelling and meaning with an appropriate dictionary.
Key vocabulary
morpheme
the smallest meaningful part of a word
base word
a form to which an affix can be added; it may be a standalone English word
root
a core meaning element, often from Greek or Latin, that may not stand alone
prefix
a meaningful element added before a base or root
suffix
a meaningful element added after a base or root
word sum
a display of meaningful parts and any spelling change used to build a word
etymology
the history and origin of a word
spelling generalisation
a pattern that is often useful but may have exceptions
technical word
a word with a precise meaning in a subject or field
verify
confirm spelling and meaning using reliable evidence
Concept model and worked thinking

Reliable routine

  1. State the exact meaning needed in the sentence and select an affix that contributes that meaning.
  2. Segment the proposed word into meaningful parts; remember that a base may stand alone while a Greek or Latin root may not.
  3. Compare the parts with genuine known relatives or root families rather than relying on sound alone.
  4. Apply a relevant spelling generalisation: an unchanged join, final-e change, y-to-i change or consonant doubling.
  5. For -able and -ible, use a clear base or word-family evidence, then verify when the conventional ending remains uncertain.
  6. Read the completed word in context and check that all morphemes support the intended meaning.
  7. Verify uncertain conventional spelling, pronunciation, origin or technical meaning in an appropriate dictionary.

prefix meaning

Task: Which word means “to read again”?

Model reasoning: The prefix re- means again, so reread means read again.

prefix meaning

Task: Which word best completes the sentence? The advertisement knowingly made a ___ claim after the test disproved it.

Model reasoning: Dishonest means not honest, which fits a claim knowingly presented after it was disproved.

prefix meaning

Task: A signal that does not use spoken words is ___.

Model reasoning: Non- means not or without, so nonverbal means not using spoken words.

prefix meaning

Task: Which word means “connected wrongly”?

Model reasoning: Mis- can mean wrongly, so misconnected means connected in the wrong way.

negative prefix

Task: Choose the conventional spelling for “not active”.

Model reasoning: Inactive is the established negative form of active.

negative prefix

Task: Choose the conventional spelling for “not legal”.

Model reasoning: Illegal is the conventional negative form of legal.

negative prefix

Task: Which sentence uses a negative prefix accurately?

Model reasoning: Illegible is the conventional word meaning not able to be read.

prefix analysis

Task: Why should research not automatically be analysed as re- + search meaning “search again”?

Model reasoning: Letter sequences count as affixes only when the structure and meaning support the analysis.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Meaning comes first

Choose an affix because it expresses the intended relationship. Re- often means again, mis- wrongly and non- not or without. Negative forms such as inactive, impossible, illegal and irregular are conventional rather than freely interchangeable.

Base words and roots

A base can take an affix and may stand alone, as help does in helpful. A root carries core meaning but may not stand alone, as spect in inspect and spectator. Matching letters alone does not prove a meaningful word structure.

Spelling at morpheme boundaries

Many joins are unchanged. A final silent e is often dropped before a vowel suffix but may be retained to preserve meaning or sound. A consonant + y often changes to i, except that y is usually kept before -ing. A final consonant is often doubled after a short stressed final vowel before a vowel suffix.

Choosing -able or -ible

Both endings often mean “able to be”. -able commonly follows a clear base, while many -ible words are Latin-derived. This tendency cannot settle every word, so use word-family evidence and verify uncertain conventional forms.

Greek and Latin clues

Roots such as bio (life), geo (Earth), tele (far), graph (write or record), port (carry), spect (look) and dict (say) guide spelling and meaning. One root provides only part of a technical definition; combine every morpheme with subject context.

Known-word analogy and verification

Compare an unfamiliar word with a genuine relative that shares meaning and structure, not merely a similar sound. Then check the proposed word in context and verify uncertain spelling, pronunciation, origin and precise meaning in a reputable learner or subject dictionary.

Common misconceptions
  • Every word beginning with re has a prefix meaning again. The structure and meaning must support that analysis; research does not simply mean search again.
  • In-, im-, il-, ir-, un-, dis- and non- can be exchanged freely. Their meanings and conventional forms differ; use an established word family and context.
  • All words meaning “able to be” end in -able. Some established words end in -ible; the clear-base tendency helps, but uncertain forms need verification.
  • A base never changes when a suffix is added. Some joins change final e or y, or double a final consonant.
  • A spelling generalisation has no exceptions. A generalisation is useful evidence, not an absolute law.
  • Every Greek or Latin root is a standalone English word. Roots such as spect carry meaning but usually do not stand alone in English.
  • One root gives the complete definition of a technical word. Combine every morpheme with sentence and subject context.
  • A dictionary replaces word reasoning. Morphological reasoning builds a justified hypothesis; a dictionary confirms conventional form and precise meaning.
Important questions and answers
  • How do I choose a prefix? Name the exact meaning first, then select the established prefix form that expresses it in that word family.
  • When does final y change to i? A final consonant + y often changes to i before a suffix, but y is usually kept before -ing.
  • When is a final consonant doubled? It is often doubled before a vowel suffix when a short stressed final syllable ends vowel-consonant, as in admit/admitted.
  • How do I choose -able or -ible? -able often follows a clear base, while many -ible words are Latin-derived; verify uncertain conventional spellings.
  • What is the difference between a root and a base? A base can take an affix and may stand alone; a root carries core meaning but may not be a standalone English word.
  • What should I verify in a technical word? Check its conventional spelling, precise subject meaning, and any uncertain origin or pronunciation.
Assessment-style questions and review hints
  • Begin with the meaning required by the sentence.
  • Mark morpheme boundaries before changing any letters.
  • Use a genuine word-family relative rather than a word that merely sounds similar.
  • Check whether final e, final y or a final consonant changes at the suffix boundary.
  • Treat -able/-ible and negative-prefix patterns as evidence, not exceptionless rules.
  • Explain the contribution of every root used in a technical word.
  • Verify uncertain spelling and meaning with a reputable learner or subject dictionary.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: work with one short example, highlighted evidence and a structured response frame.
  • Core: complete an unseen example independently and justify the decisive evidence.
  • Extend: compare plausible alternatives, explain limitations and create a new example within the Year 6 boundary.
Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Construct one negative word, one suffix word requiring a boundary change and one technical word containing a Greek or Latin root. Show the parts, explain the meaning and justify or verify each spelling.

Evidence of mastery: The student independently selects meaning-appropriate affixes, distinguishes roots from bases, applies or deliberately rejects a boundary change, uses genuine word-family or origin evidence, and verifies an uncertain conventional or technical form.

Learn: Word origins, morphemes and technical spelling

Learning intention: Spell unfamiliar and technical words by combining known words, bases, prefixes, suffixes, Greek and Latin roots, letter patterns and spelling generalisations, then verify both meaning and conventional spelling.

Five-step routine

  1. Identify the intended meaning and say the word.
  2. Separate prefixes, base or root, and suffixes.
  3. Use known relatives and origin knowledge as evidence.
  4. Apply any spelling change at the morpheme boundary.
  5. Write the word, check it in context and verify conventionally.

Common misconceptions

  • Knowing one root gives the complete definition of a technical word. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • A Greek or Latin root must be a standalone English word. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • One vaguely similar word proves a root’s meaning and spelling. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • A dictionary replaces the need to reason about how a technical word is formed. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence
