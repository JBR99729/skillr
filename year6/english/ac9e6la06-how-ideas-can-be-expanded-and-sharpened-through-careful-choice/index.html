What students learn in AC9E6LA06

Students learn to understand how ideas can be expanded and sharpened through careful choice of verbs, elaborated tenses and a range of adverb groups. They move from recognising the feature to explaining how it works in context, then applying it deliberately in their own reading, speaking, writing or multimodal creation.

Core idea: In English, choices in language, structure, literature and mode create meaning for a particular audience and purpose. A strong answer names the choice, gives evidence and explains the effect.

Success looks like

  • identify the relevant feature, strategy or text choice
  • use precise English metalanguage
  • support the answer with specific evidence
  • explain the effect in context
  • apply or revise the choice for a new audience and purpose
Key vocabulary
choice
a deliberate language, structure or text decision
evidence
a specific example that supports an explanation
purpose
what the text is designed to achieve

Use these terms only when they help explain the actual example. Avoid dropping metalanguage into an answer without showing the evidence.

Common misconceptions
  • Feature spotting only — Connect the choice to meaning, purpose and audience.
  • Vague effect language — Replace 'it makes it better' with a precise explanation.
  • Evidence omitted — Quote or describe a specific example.
  • No transfer — Apply the idea to a fresh text or created example.
Guided learning activities

1. Annotate a model example

Students highlight the exact words, sentence parts, text features, visual choices or spoken moves connected to the descriptor.

2. Compare two versions

Students compare a weaker and stronger version, then explain which choices improve meaning, clarity, credibility, cohesion or audience effect.

3. Create, justify and revise

Students create a short response or text using the focus skill, justify the key choice and revise one part after feedback.

Revision Notes

Exam routine: feature or strategy → evidence → effect → context → check.

Support, core and extend
  • Support: provide a short model, limited choices and sentence stems such as “The feature is …”, “The evidence is …”, and “This affects meaning by …”.
  • Core: use an unseen example and require evidence plus a context-specific explanation.
  • Extend: compare two texts, modes, audiences or drafts and evaluate which choices are most effective.

Learn: precise verbs, elaborated tenses and adverb groups

Writers sharpen meaning by choosing a verb that names the action precisely, expanding the verb group to show time or attitude, and adding an adverb group to explain how, when, where, why or to what degree something happens.

Verb choices and tense

  • Simple: “The team tests the bridge” presents a regular/current action.
  • Progressive: “The team is testing” presents an action in progress.
  • Perfect: “The team has tested” links a completed action to the present.
  • Perfect progressive: “The team has been testing” emphasises duration and continuing relevance.
  • Modality: “might have been testing” adds possibility.

Adverb groups

Single adverb: “The current surged suddenly.” Adverb phrase/group: “The current surged with unexpected force.” Adverbial clause: “The current surged when the gate opened.” Each adds a different kind of information.

Worked revision

Weak: “The bird went down fast.” Sharper: “The falcon dived suddenly towards its prey.” “Dived” specifies movement; “suddenly” adds manner/timing; the final group adds direction.

  1. Locate the verb group.
  2. Check tense against the text's time frame.
  3. Ask whether progressive or perfect aspect changes the intended timing.
  4. Replace general verbs only when the new verb is more accurate.
  5. Add adverb information that matters; remove decoration that does not sharpen meaning.

Common traps: Vivid does not mean exaggerated; longer verb groups are not always better; tense shifts need a reason; adverbs can modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs or whole clauses.
