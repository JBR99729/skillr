What students learn in AC9E6LA01

Learning intention: We are learning to choose and explain language that suits an audience, relationship and situation.

Curriculum focus: Understand that language varies as levels of formality and social distance increase.

Success criteria

  • place language choices on a formality continuum rather than treating language as only formal or informal
  • rewrite a message for a different audience while preserving its meaning
  • explain how address, modality, humour, dialect and patterns of choices position relationships and authority
Key vocabulary
register
a variety of language chosen for a situation and purpose
formality
the degree to which language follows conventional, explicit and socially distanced patterns
social distance
how familiar, close or institutionally separated participants are in a context
modality
language showing possibility, probability, recommendation or obligation
term of address
a name, title or group label used to address someone
dialect
a systematic language variety associated with a community, place or social group
code-switching
shifting language variety or register to suit context, audience or identity
sarcasm
language whose intended critical meaning contrasts with its literal wording
Concept model and worked thinking

Reliable routine

  1. Identify the real audience, purpose, setting and relationship.
  2. Place the language on a continuum rather than using a binary label.
  3. Notice a pattern across address, grammar, vocabulary, modality, detail, humour and dialect.
  4. Explain how that pattern positions closeness, authority, solidarity or distance.
  5. Test the interpretation against context and avoid stereotypes or conclusions based on one word.
  6. For a rewrite, preserve meaning and deliberately adjust the pattern for the new audience.

audience and formality

Task: Mia is emailing the principal to request permission for a lunchtime chess club. Which opening best suits the audience?

Model reasoning: “Dear Principal Okafor” and “I am writing to request” acknowledge the principal’s role and create an appropriately formal, respectful tone.

social distance

Task: Which message best suits a close friend who has borrowed your ruler?

Model reasoning: The polite but conversational request suits a close relationship without sounding ceremonial or controlling.

formality continuum

Task: Which sentence is most formal?

Model reasoning: “Would you be able to assist me with this matter?” uses indirect modality and formal vocabulary.

terms of address

Task: At the start of a school awards ceremony, which address best recognises the audience and occasion?

Model reasoning: The inclusive list of groups and the greeting “welcome” acknowledge a large, less familiar audience respectfully.

direct audience rewrite

Task: Rewrite “Send me the excursion form today” for a polite email to a caregiver. Which version is best?

Model reasoning: The rewrite keeps the deadline but softens the command through “Could you please” and names the required action precisely.

direct audience rewrite

Task: A formal notice says, “Students are required to assemble in the hall at 9.00 am.” Which version best suits a class group chat among friends?

Model reasoning: “Meet in the hall at 9!” retains the essential information in concise, conversational language.

authority accepted

Task: A teacher says, “Please begin packing away.” Which reply most clearly accepts the teacher’s authority?

Model reasoning: The title, agreement and immediate action show willing acceptance of the teacher’s direction.

authority negotiated

Task: Which reply respectfully negotiates a coach’s instruction to run another lap?

Model reasoning: The speaker acknowledges the instruction, offers a reason and proposes a safe alternative instead of simply refusing.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The central idea

Language choices sit on a continuum from highly conversational to highly formal. Effective choices depend on audience, purpose, social distance, authority, culture and urgency—not on a rule that formal always means respectful.

Audience and relationship rewrites

Preserve the message’s purpose, then adjust address, directness, modality, vocabulary, detail, contractions and humour. A close peer may receive “Can you send the photo?”, while an unfamiliar organiser may receive “Could you please send the event photograph by Friday?”

Authority and agency

Language can accept authority (“Certainly, I’ll do that”), negotiate it (“Could we change the time because…?”), or respectfully resist it (“I cannot agree because…; I can offer…”). Evidence comes from the whole response, not simply politeness words.

Formality continuum

Conversational language often uses shared context, ellipsis, contractions, slang and direct address. Formal language often makes context explicit, uses titles, complete structures, precise vocabulary and softened or institutional requests. Urgency can justify direct commands in any relationship.

Humour, address and belonging

Terms of address and humour can reduce distance, create solidarity or exclude outsiders. Sarcasm is risky when trust is low or authority is unequal because literal and intended meanings conflict.

Dialect and respect

Dialects are systematic varieties that can express identity, place and belonging. Analyse what features do in context; never describe a speaker’s dialect as broken or intellectually inferior. Register choice adds options rather than erasing identity.

Evidence rule

Build claims from patterns: title + indirect modal request + precise detail may signal formal distance; kinship address + contraction + shared joke may signal closeness. One word rarely proves the relationship, and conclusions should remain appropriately cautious.

Misconceptions and repairs

Formal is not automatically kind; informal is not automatically rude; humour is not always unsuitable; age does not determine register; and Standard Australian English is not a measure of intelligence. Replace absolutes with contextual, evidence-based explanations.

Important questions and answers

Q: Can a formal text sound warm? A: Yes—professional wording can combine with inclusive address, thanks or gentle humour. Q: Can direct language be respectful? A: Yes—clarity and urgency can justify it. Q: How much evidence is enough? A: Usually two or more connected features plus relevant context.

Assessment hints and mastery

Name the feature, quote or paraphrase exact evidence, explain its relationship effect, and qualify the claim using context. Mastery means adapting one message for contrasting audiences and analysing authority, modality or distance with a pattern of evidence.

Common misconceptions
  • Formal language is always more respectful. Respect depends on purpose, culture and relationship; formal wording can be cold or hostile, while considerate conversational language can be respectful.
  • Informal language is careless or inferior. Informal registers and dialects are systematic resources that can suit close relationships and particular communities.
  • One formal word proves a text is formal. Use a pattern of address, modality, grammar, vocabulary and context before deciding.
  • Humour never belongs in a formal setting. Well-judged humour can build rapport, but sarcasm or insider jokes may exclude or embarrass audiences.
  • Respecting authority means always agreeing. A speaker may negotiate or resist respectfully by acknowledging the role, giving reasons and offering a constructive next step.
Important questions and answers
  • Can a formal text sound warm? Yes. Professional wording can combine with inclusive address, thanks or gentle humour.
  • Can direct language be respectful? Yes. Clarity and urgency can justify a direct command, especially for safety.
  • How much evidence supports a claim about relationship? Usually two or more connected language features plus relevant context.
  • Should a student’s home dialect be corrected into a “better” form? No. Teach additional register choices for particular contexts while respecting the student’s dialect and identity.
Assessment-style questions and review hints
  • Identify the audience, purpose, setting and relationship before labelling the register.
  • Name and quote a pattern of at least two relevant language features.
  • Explain the relationship effect instead of merely listing features.
  • Distinguish accepting, negotiating and respectfully resisting authority.
  • Use cautious language when context allows more than one interpretation.
  • In rewrites, preserve meaning while changing address, modality, directness, detail and tone.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: work with one short example, highlighted evidence and a structured response frame.
  • Core: complete an unseen example independently and justify the decisive evidence.
  • Extend: compare plausible alternatives, explain limitations and create a new example within the Year 6 boundary.
Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Write the same request for a close peer and an unfamiliar authority figure. Underline at least three changed language features, then explain how the pattern changes social distance.

Evidence of mastery: The student preserves meaning while adapting language for contrasting audiences, distinguishes accepted, negotiated and resisted authority, and justifies a contextual interpretation with a pattern of evidence without ranking dialects.

Learn: Formality, social distance and relationships

Learning intention: Recognise that formality changes along a continuum as audience, purpose, familiarity, authority and social distance change, then adapt language without treating formal as automatically better or informal as automatically rude.

Five-step routine

  1. Name the relationship, audience and purpose.
  2. Place the language on a formality continuum.
  3. Locate vocabulary, grammar, modality and address choices.
  4. Explain how the choices construct distance, authority or solidarity.
  5. Adapt the wording while preserving the intended meaning.

Common misconceptions

  • Respectful language requires accepting the other person’s conclusion. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • One polished version should suit every audience equally well. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • Social distance never changes once people first meet. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.
  • Formal Standard Australian English is the only respectful or intelligent way to communicate. Recheck the example, then replace the absolute claim with a precise, limited explanation.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

  • Apply register and vocabulary to an unfamiliar example. Hint: Keep the meaning stable while changing register-specific vocabulary.
  • Apply grammar and ellipsis to an unfamiliar example. Hint: Use ellipsis only when the audience can recover the omitted meaning easily.
  • Apply solidarity, humour and inclusion to an unfamiliar example. Hint: Use humour only when it is respectful, understood by the audience and appropriate to the purpose.
  • Apply respectful disagreement to an unfamiliar example. Hint: Target the reasoning, represent the other view fairly and support the alternative.

Support, core and extend

  • Support: say or read the example together, highlight the decisive feature and use a sentence frame for the explanation.
  • Core: analyse an unseen example, justify the choice with evidence and apply the same reasoning independently.
  • Extend: compare two plausible choices, explain the limitation of each and create a new example inside the Year 6 boundary.

Exit ticket and mastery evidence

Complete the five-step routine with a new example. Mastery is shown when the student identifies the relevant evidence, explains it accurately, rejects a plausible misconception and transfers the learning without changing the intended meaning.
