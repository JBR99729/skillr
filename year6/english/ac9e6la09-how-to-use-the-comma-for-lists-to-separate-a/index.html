What students learn in AC9E6LA09

Students learn to understand how to use the comma for lists, to separate a dependent clause from an independent clause, and in dialogue. They move from recognising the feature to explaining how it works in context, then applying it deliberately in their own reading, speaking, writing or multimodal creation.

Core idea: In English, choices in language, structure, literature and mode create meaning for a particular audience and purpose. A strong answer names the choice, gives evidence and explains the effect.

Success looks like

  • identify the relevant feature, strategy or text choice
  • use precise English metalanguage
  • support the answer with specific evidence
  • explain the effect in context
  • apply or revise the choice for a new audience and purpose
Key vocabulary
feature
a grammatical, spelling, punctuation or vocabulary choice that shapes meaning
context
the situation, audience and purpose for the language choice
effect
what the choice helps the reader, listener or viewer understand

Use these terms only when they help explain the actual example. Avoid dropping metalanguage into an answer without showing the evidence.

Learn: commas in lists, complex sentences and dialogue

A comma marks grammatical structure; it is not inserted simply wherever a reader might breathe. First identify the structure, then apply the matching convention.

Lists

Use commas between three or more parallel items: “We measured height, width and mass.” In Australian English, the final serial comma is often omitted in a simple list, but it may be used for clarity: “We packed bread and cheese, apples, and water.” Keep list items grammatically parallel.

Dependent and independent clauses

Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause: “Although the track was muddy, the walkers continued.” A following essential dependent clause usually has no comma: “The walkers continued although the track was muddy.” Never join two independent clauses with a comma alone; add a conjunction or make separate sentences.

Dialogue

  • Before attribution: “The gate is open,” said Priya.
  • After attribution: Priya said, “The gate is open.”
  • Question/exclamation: “Is it open?” asked Priya.
  • Interrupted quotation: “The gate,” Priya explained, “opens at nine.”

Editing routine

  1. Mark list items or clause boundaries.
  2. Locate the reporting clause in dialogue.
  3. Place the comma according to structure.
  4. Check capital letters and quotation marks.
  5. Read again for ambiguity—not for breaths.

Common traps: comma splices; commas between a subject and verb; mandatory serial commas in every Australian list; and placing a comma after a question mark in dialogue.
