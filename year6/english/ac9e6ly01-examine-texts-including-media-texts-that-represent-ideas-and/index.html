What students learn in AC9E6LY01

Students learn to examine texts including media texts that represent ideas and events, and identify how they reflect the context in which they were created. They move from recognising the feature to explaining how it works in context, then applying it deliberately in their own reading, speaking, writing or multimodal creation.

Core idea: In English, choices in language, structure, literature and mode create meaning for a particular audience and purpose. A strong answer names the choice, gives evidence and explains the effect.

Success looks like

  • identify the relevant feature, strategy or text choice
  • use precise English metalanguage
  • support the answer with specific evidence
  • explain the effect in context
  • apply or revise the choice for a new audience and purpose
Key vocabulary
purpose
the reason for reading or viewing
strategy
a deliberate action used to monitor and build meaning
evidence
specific information from the text that supports an answer

Use these terms only when they help explain the actual example. Avoid dropping metalanguage into an answer without showing the evidence.

Worked transfer

Common misconceptions
  • Answer hunting — Build meaning across the whole text, not from one isolated word.
  • Prediction never checked — Confirm or revise predictions using later evidence.
  • Purpose ignored — Choose a reading path and strategy that match the task.
  • Evidence copied without explanation — Explain why the selected detail supports the answer.
Guided learning activities

1. Annotate a model example

Students highlight the exact words, sentence parts, text features, visual choices or spoken moves connected to the descriptor.

2. Compare two versions

Students compare a weaker and stronger version, then explain which choices improve meaning, clarity, credibility, cohesion or audience effect.

3. Create, justify and revise

Students create a short response or text using the focus skill, justify the key choice and revise one part after feedback.

Revision Notes

Exam routine: feature or strategy → evidence → effect → context → check.

  • Apply the idea in a fresh sentence, text extract, discussion or multimodal example. Answer with precise evidence and a short explanation.
  • Revise one answer or created text using the success criteria. Answer with precise evidence and a short explanation.
Support, core and extend
  • Support: provide a short model, limited choices and sentence stems such as “The feature is …”, “The evidence is …”, and “This affects meaning by …”.
  • Core: use an unseen example and require evidence plus a context-specific explanation.
  • Extend: compare two texts, modes, audiences or drafts and evaluate which choices are most effective.

Learn: How context shapes representations

Learning intention: Identify what a text represents, locate language and visual choices, connect them to the historical, social or cultural conditions of creation, and make limited evidence-based claims rather than stereotypes.

How to think

  1. Name the exact feature or choice.
  2. Point to specific evidence.
  3. Explain what it changes for meaning, purpose or response.
  4. Check the claim against the whole text and context.
  5. Revise or transfer the choice deliberately.
FeatureOriginal exampleWhy it matters
creation contextA 1942 poster urges families to save food and fuel.Wartime scarcity and government campaigning help explain its urgent commands.
audienceA youth website explains drought through student interviews and short videos.The modes and informal explanations suit a young contemporary audience.
representation of eventA news report calls a protest a “disruption” rather than a “demonstration”.The noun choice frames the event through inconvenience rather than political purpose.
selectionAn article about a festival shows performers but no local residents.The chosen images emphasise spectacle while omitting community experience.
viewpointA diary describes an evacuation from one child’s limited perspective.The viewpoint provides emotional immediacy but not a complete public record.
technology contextA recent campaign uses vertical video, captions and rapid cuts.Platform conventions shape how the message is paced and accessed.
social valuesAn older advertisement presents household work as only women’s responsibility.The representation reflects dominant gender assumptions of its production context.
cultural positioningA travel account describes local customs as “strange”.The evaluative label centres the traveller’s norms and positions the community as unfamiliar.
source purposeA company brochure highlights jobs created but not pollution complaints.Promotional purpose helps explain the selective positive representation.
multimodal framingA low camera angle makes a political speaker appear powerful.The visual angle contributes to authority beyond the spoken words.
change over timeTwo museum labels from 1980 and 2025 describe the same collection differently.Changed knowledge and ethical expectations can alter terminology and emphasis.
qualified judgementThe documentary may favour redevelopment because it interviews developers first and gives residents little time.The claim identifies evidence and limits certainty appropriately.

Common mistakes

  • Knowing the purpose proves every claim is false. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • Only written words represent ideas. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • Newer representations are automatically unbiased. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • Mentioning context replaces the need for textual evidence. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.

Check your understanding

Choose one unfamiliar example. Identify the feature, quote or describe the evidence, explain its effect and make one justified revision. A complete answer must show why the evidence supports the claim.
