What students learn in AC9E6LE03

Students learn to identify and explain characteristics that define an author's individual style. They move from recognising the feature to explaining how it works in context, then applying it deliberately in their own reading, speaking, writing or multimodal creation.

Core idea: In English, choices in language, structure, literature and mode create meaning for a particular audience and purpose. A strong answer names the choice, gives evidence and explains the effect.

Success looks like

  • identify the relevant feature, strategy or text choice
  • use precise English metalanguage
  • support the answer with specific evidence
  • explain the effect in context
  • apply or revise the choice for a new audience and purpose
Key vocabulary
evidence
a short, specific detail from the text
viewpoint
the position from which events or ideas are represented
effect
the response or interpretation created by an author's choice

Use these terms only when they help explain the actual example. Avoid dropping metalanguage into an answer without showing the evidence.

Worked transfer

Take an unseen example linked to this outcome. First identify the relevant choice, then rewrite, annotate, compare or present it in a second context so the idea is not only memorised.

Teacher prompt: What changed, what stayed the same, and how do you know the new version still suits the audience and purpose?

Common misconceptions
  • Retelling replaces analysis — Use brief evidence and explain how the author created an effect.
  • Personal opinion needs no evidence — A strong response connects preference or interpretation to text details.
  • One feature proves the whole claim — Look for patterns across language, structure, viewpoint and context.
  • Context becomes stereotype — Keep claims specific to the text and evidence.
Guided learning activities

1. Annotate a model example

Students highlight the exact words, sentence parts, text features, visual choices or spoken moves connected to the descriptor.

2. Compare two versions

Students compare a weaker and stronger version, then explain which choices improve meaning, clarity, credibility, cohesion or audience effect.

3. Create, justify and revise

Students create a short response or text using the focus skill, justify the key choice and revise one part after feedback.

Revision Notes

Exam routine: feature or strategy → evidence → effect → context → check.

Support, core and extend
  • Support: provide a short model, limited choices and sentence stems such as “The feature is …”, “The evidence is …”, and “This affects meaning by …”.
  • Core: use an unseen example and require evidence plus a context-specific explanation.
  • Extend: compare two texts, modes, audiences or drafts and evaluate which choices are most effective.

Learn: Authorial style and voice

Learning intention: Identify recurring combinations of language, structure, viewpoint, imagery, humour, pacing and concerns across more than one text, then explain their effect without confusing author, narrator or genre.

How to think

  1. Name the exact feature or choice.
  2. Point to specific evidence.
  3. Explain what it changes for meaning, purpose or response.
  4. Check the claim against the whole text and context.
  5. Revise or transfer the choice deliberately.
FeatureOriginal exampleWhy it matters
recurring first-person voiceAcross two stories, the narrators reveal feelings but misunderstand other characters.The repeated close but limited viewpoint creates intimacy and uncertainty.
sentence patternAt each turning point, the author shifts from flowing description to short fragments.The repeated syntax speeds the pace and emphasises sudden decisions.
imagery motifWindows, doors and gates recur whenever characters face difficult choices.The repeated boundary imagery develops the author’s concern with freedom and restriction.
understatementAfter each disaster, a character quietly says, “That could have gone better.”The recurring understatement releases tension and builds dry humour.
weather imageryStorms gather as relationships worsen in several stories.The recurring weather pattern mirrors conflict and creates anticipation.
dialogue styleCharacters often interrupt themselves with dashes and unfinished thoughts.The broken dialogue creates hesitation and a recognisable conversational rhythm.
pacingThe author slows important discoveries with precise sensory details.Delayed action makes readers notice clues and share the character’s concentration.
theme patternResponsibility to family returns across adventure, mystery and realistic stories.A recurring concern across genres helps define the author’s individual style.
narrator distinctionA boastful narrator describes every mistake as someone else’s fault.The crafted narrator voice creates irony; it is not direct evidence about the author.
genre conventionA locked room and hidden clue appear in one mystery.These features may belong to the mystery genre unless handled distinctively across the author’s work.
cross-text evidenceTwo poems and a story all use circular endings and river imagery.Evidence across several texts supports a defensible claim about recurring craft.
style synthesisThe author combines limited narration, dry humour and boundary imagery.The distinctive combination matters more than any single technique.

Common mistakes

  • A fictional “I” always reports the author’s own life. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • Every feature in a book uniquely identifies its author. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • One quotation is enough to define an author’s style. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.
  • A list of techniques is a complete style analysis. Check the evidence and replace the absolute rule with a limited explanation.

Check your understanding

Choose one unfamiliar example. Identify the feature, quote or describe the evidence, explain its effect and make one justified revision. A complete answer must show why the evidence supports the claim.
