Year 6 Mathematics · AC9M6SP01

Year 6 Maths: Parallel Cross-sections of Objects

Choose the base, predict the cut face and explain what stays the same.

Ready to project and teach

Learning goals: compare cross-sections of right prisms

Predict the two-dimensional shape made by a flat slice through an object. Identify the base first, state the direction of the cut, then compare the shape and size of parallel sections.

  • Name a cross-section and distinguish it from an original outside face.
  • Explain why sections parallel to a right prism’s bases are congruent.
  • Use changing section size to distinguish a pyramid from a prism.
Get ready

Recall triangles, rectangles and other polygons; faces, edges and vertices; and the meaning of parallel. Congruent means the same shape and size, even after moving or turning. A prism’s bases are its two matching parallel ends, not necessarily the face resting on the table.

See the cutting plane

A cross-section is the region where a flat plane meets the solid. Imagine a very thin slice: describe the new cut face rather than naming the whole three-dimensional object. A right prism has two congruent parallel polygonal bases, joined by side faces perpendicular to those bases.

Move a cutting plane along the prism while keeping it parallel to the bases. The outline and dimensions stay the same. Thinking of the prism as a stack of identical copies of its base explains why.

A triangular prism therefore gives congruent triangular sections parallel to its triangular ends. A rectangular prism gives congruent rectangles parallel to its chosen rectangular ends. A cylinder gives congruent circles parallel to its circular ends, but it is not a polygonal prism.

Work through three comparisons

1. A rectangular prism

Choose an end measuring 6 cm by 4 cm. A plane halfway along the prism, parallel to that end, makes a 6 cm by 4 cm rectangle. Moving the plane closer to the end does not make that rectangle smaller: the prism has a uniform cross-section.

2. A triangular prism

A triangular end has side lengths 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm. A parallel slice has the same three side lengths. It is a triangle congruent to the end, not a rectangle simply because some outside faces are rectangles.

3. A pyramid is different

Compare two cuts through a square pyramid, both parallel to its square base. The cut nearer the apex is smaller. The sections are squares, but they are not congruent. Matching shape alone does not establish the prism relationship.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • “Every cut matches the base.” State that the plane is parallel to the chosen bases. A differently oriented cut can have a different shape.
  • “A cross-section is always a face already on the outside.” It is the cut region made inside the object.
  • “All squares are congruent.” Congruent squares must also have the same side length.
  • “A round cylinder is a prism.” Its parallel circular sections have the uniform-section property, but a prism has polygonal bases.
Try it with a model

Build a straight triangular prism from modelling clay, or stack identical triangular cards without changing their orientation. Predict three sections parallel to the triangular ends, then sketch and label them. Explain why their sizes should match.

Compare with a model that tapers towards a point. Keep the cutting direction the same and describe what changes. Use soft clay and adult-managed tools; an adult should make any cuts in food or harder materials.

Check the answer and reasoning

The prism’s parallel sections are congruent triangles. In the tapering model, the sections can keep a triangular shape while becoming smaller. Record cut direction, shape and size separately.

Predict, sketch and explain
  1. A right pentagonal prism is cut parallel to its pentagonal ends. Name and sketch the section.
  2. A cube has edges of 4 cm. Describe a slice parallel to a face.
  3. Two circular slices of a cone are made parallel to its base, one nearer the apex. Compare them.
Check the answer and reasoning
  1. A pentagon congruent to either pentagonal end.
  2. A square with side length 4 cm, provided the plane passes through the cube parallel to that face.
  3. Both are circles; the one nearer the apex is smaller. They are not congruent.
Use the evidence carefully

A learner says, “I found three equal square slices, so this object must be a cube.” Explain why that evidence is insufficient.

Check the answer and reasoning

A longer right square prism also has equal square sections parallel to its ends. The slices do not tell us that the prism’s length equals a base edge. Inspect the full object and the direction and location of the cuts.

Questions worth discussing
Can the same solid have different cross-sections?
Yes. Changing the direction or position of a cut can change the section. Specify the plane.
Why are parallel base sections of a right prism congruent?
The solid has the same base-shaped outline and dimensions throughout its length.
Why compare a prism with a pyramid?
It reveals that preserving a shape is different from preserving both shape and size.
Apply the idea to a new object

A packaging designer proposes a right hexagonal-prism tube. A divider will be placed halfway along it, parallel to its ends. Describe the divider and justify which dimensions must match the end.

Check the answer and reasoning

The divider must be a hexagon congruent to the chosen end cross-section; all corresponding side lengths and angles match. Its position halfway along does not halve its width. This answer concerns the ideal geometric model, before allowing for material thickness.

Exit check

Sketch a triangular prism, identify its bases and show a parallel cut. Name the section and explain both its shape and size. Then explain one difference if the object were a triangular pyramid.

Ready to move on: the sketch, cutting direction and explanation agree. Revisit the model if the answer names an outside side face or explains shape without size.

Teacher and parent guidance

Support: use physical stacks and let the learner trace matching sections. Core: compare right prisms and pyramids with labelled sketches. Extend: compare an oblique cut with a parallel-base cut through the same model without requiring advanced conic-section terminology.

Connect foam/clay models, adult-prepared fruit slices and drawings. Natural fruit may be uneven; discuss the difference between a real object and an ideal prism. For First Nations dwelling-design investigations, use an attributed, place-specific, community-approved resource or museum account, identify the particular structure and compare only geometry that is actually shown. Do not assume all dwellings have one design.
