Plan, create, edit and publish powerful Year 5 texts

Purpose and audience drive every good writing choice. Plan the message, organise ideas, develop them with precise language, revise meaning, edit conventions and publish written or multimodal texts that work for the reader.

Key concept: the writing cycle

AC9E5LY06: plan, create, edit and publish written and multimodal texts for imaginative, informative and persuasive purposes, developing ideas with suitable visual features, text structures, connectives, expanded noun groups, specialist vocabulary and punctuation including dialogue punctuation.

1 Purpose + audience
What am I trying to achieve, and who will read it?2 Plan
Choose ideas, evidence and an appropriate structure.3 Draft + develop
Turn the plan into paragraphs, scenes or sections.4 Revise
Improve ideas, order, detail and cohesion.5 Edit
Correct sentences, spelling and punctuation.6 Publish
Check layout, visuals, captions and final accuracy.

Key distinction: revision improves what the text says and how it works; editing corrects how the final wording is written.

Three purposes, three structures

Imaginative

Goal: engage the reader through character, setting, events and crafted language.

Typical shape: orientation → complication → development → resolution.

Example: “Steam curled above the night-market stalls as Mina tightened her grip on the missing-wallet poster.”

Informative

Goal: explain or report accurate information clearly.

Typical shape: overview → grouped sections with headings → conclusion or summary where useful.

Example: “Mangroves tolerate salty conditions and their tangled roots create shelter for many young animals.”

Persuasive

Goal: convince an audience using a position, relevant reasons and evidence.

Typical shape: position → developed reasons/evidence → conclusion or call to action.

Example: “Installing refill stations would reduce single-use plastic and make drinking water easier to access.”

Build strong paragraphs

A useful paragraph has a controlling idea. The topic sentence introduces it; supporting details explain, prove or illustrate it; connectives show relationships; the ending completes the idea or leads logically to the next paragraph.

Weak

Native plants are good. They are plants. Bugs live near plants. Our shed is green. They do not need as much water.

Revised

Native plants are practical choices for school gardens. Because they suit local conditions, many species need less watering once established. In addition, their flowers and foliage can provide food and shelter for local insects. These benefits make native planting both water-wise and useful for biodiversity.

Why the revision is stronger: one clear focus, relevant supporting details, a connective with a real logical job, precise vocabulary and no irrelevant shed detail.

Do not insert a connective just because it sounds formal. It must accurately describe the relationship between the ideas.

Develop language precisely

Expanded noun groups

Start with the noun and add only details that help the reader: bird → the small bright-blue bird near the creek.

Specialist and technical vocabulary

Use the correct subject word when it improves accuracy: evaporation, habitat, erosion, renewable energy. Explain a term if the intended audience may not know it.

Strong verbs and sensory detail

Choose verbs that carry meaning: walked → shuffled / strode / raced. In imaginative writing, precise sensory details can show mood without explaining it directly.

Dialogue punctuation
  • “Watch the tide,” Dad warned. The comma belongs inside the closing quotation mark before the reporting clause.
  • “Are we ready?” asked Noah. A question mark stays inside the quotation marks because it belongs to the spoken words.
  • Mia said, “We should leave now.” Use a comma before a quotation that follows a reporting clause.
  • Start a new paragraph when a different character speaks so the reader can track speakers easily.
Multimodal texts: visuals must do a job

Images, diagrams, graphs, captions, headings and layout are part of meaning. Choose them because they help the audience understand—not merely because they decorate the page.

  • A labelled volcano cross-section can explain structures faster than an unrelated photo.
  • A graph can make a comparison visible if labels and units match the written discussion.
  • A caption should add context or interpretation instead of saying only “This is a photo”.
  • Before publishing, check relevance, readability and whether an image is permitted to be used.
Revise before you edit
  1. Purpose: Is the position, explanation or story goal clear?
  2. Organisation: Does each section have one clear job? Are ideas in the best order?
  3. Development: Are important ideas supported with evidence, examples or detail?
  4. Cohesion: Do topic sentences, references and connectives guide the reader?
  5. Language: Are nouns, verbs and technical terms precise for the audience?
  6. Edit: Check sentence boundaries, grammar, spelling and punctuation.
  7. Publish: Read the final layout as the audience will see it.
Common traps
  • Correct but irrelevant detail: delete or relocate it if it does not develop the paragraph focus.
  • Narrow topic sentence, broad details: rewrite the topic sentence so it genuinely covers the paragraph.
  • Fancy connective, wrong relationship: choose meaning before formality.
  • Editing too early: do not polish sentences you may later delete or reorganise.
  • Technical vocabulary overload: precision matters more than difficulty.
  • Decorative multimodal features: every visual should contribute to purpose or comprehension.
Guided check

Draft: “The playground needs shade. Shade is good. Shade is shade. My cousin has a skateboard.”

  1. State the likely persuasive purpose and audience.
  2. Remove the irrelevant sentence.
  3. Replace repetition with two concrete benefits.
  4. Add a connective only if it correctly shows a relationship.
  5. Write a concluding sentence that returns to the position.

Possible revision: “Our playground needs more shade because students spend long periods outdoors during hot weather. Shade structures or trees could create cooler lunch areas and reduce direct sun exposure. For these reasons, adding shade would make outdoor spaces more comfortable and practical for students.”

Mastery checklist
  • I can adapt language and structure to purpose and audience.
  • I can plan and group related ideas before drafting.
  • I can choose topic sentences, details and conclusions that belong together.
  • I can use connectives according to meaning.
  • I can use expanded noun groups and precise specialist vocabulary.
  • I can punctuate dialogue accurately.
  • I can choose visual features that add meaning.
  • I can explain the difference between revising and editing.
  • I can independently improve an unseen draft.
