AC9E5LA04 • Year 5 English

Make ideas flow by linking them and choosing purposeful starting points

Cohesion is more than adding connectives. Writers guide readers by showing logical relationships and by deciding what information appears first in a sentence or paragraph.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
What students learn

Students learn that cohesive texts help readers understand how ideas connect and where the message is going. Conjunctions can show relationships between clauses, while the starting point of a sentence or paragraph can foreground time, place, cause, contrast, a group of people or the main message.

Success criteria

  • choose conjunctions that match addition, contrast, cause/result, time and condition
  • explain the relationship created by a conjunction
  • move a phrase to the beginning of a sentence to change emphasis
  • choose paragraph openings that signal the main message and guide progression
  • use known → new information to maintain a clear topic thread
  • recognise that a connective does not automatically make a text cohesive
Layer 1: direct IXL-supported cohesion

The direct research evidence is strongest for coordinating and subordinating conjunctions.

RelationshipUseful wordsExample
AdditionandMia labelled the diagram, and she added a caption.
Contrastbut, althoughAlthough the task was difficult, Kai finished it.
Cause/resultbecause, soThe tank was empty, so the pump stopped.
Timewhen, after, until, whileAfter the glue dried, we tested the model.
Conditionif, unlessUnless you save the file, your changes may be lost.

Choose the connective from the meaning relationship, not from what merely sounds familiar.

Layer 2: starting points and prominence

Compare:

  • The class planted seedlings near the fence on Friday.
  • On Friday, the class planted seedlings near the fence.

The event is still the same, but the second sentence gives greater prominence to time. Likewise, “Beside the creek, volunteers planted native grasses” makes location the reader’s entry point.

This part of the descriptor goes beyond the direct IXL task evidence. SkillrHub therefore teaches it as a curriculum extension, not as an IXL-style task claim.

Paragraph openings as signposts

A strong paragraph opening tells readers what is important and often signals the relationship to what came before.

  • The main problem is… → introduces an issue
  • One practical solution is… → shifts to a response
  • However,… → signals contrast
  • As a result,… → signals consequence
  • For example,… → signals support or illustration
  • For younger students,… → foregrounds a particular group

Good cohesion is a pathway through the message: each sentence connects to what readers already know and adds something new.

Worked examples

Cause made prominent

Because the soil was dry, the leaves wilted. The cause is placed first, so readers interpret the result through that cause immediately.

Result made prominent

The leaves wilted because the soil was dry. The result appears first; the cause is supplied afterwards.

Paragraph progression

“The creek was polluted. As a result, fish numbers fell. To address the problem, volunteers planted reeds and removed litter.” The openings guide readers from situation → consequence → response.

Common misconceptions
  • Add a connective = cohesion solved. The connective must match the logic.
  • Longer sentences are more cohesive. Length does not guarantee clear relationships.
  • Every sentence should begin the same way. Repetition can sound mechanical and hide progression.
  • Moving a phrase changes the fact. Often the basic event stays the same while emphasis changes.
  • Paragraph openings are decoration. They can foreground information and signal how the message develops.
Teacher prompts
  • What relationship do these two clauses have?
  • Which conjunction expresses that relationship most accurately?
  • What information does the reader meet first?
  • If we move this phrase to the front, what becomes more prominent?
  • What should the next paragraph opening tell the reader?
  • Does this sentence grow from information already introduced?
  • Are we guiding the reader from problem → consequence → response, or along another pathway?
