AC9E5LA04 • Year 5 English

Make ideas flow by linking them and choosing purposeful starting points

Cohesion is more than adding connectives. Writers guide readers by showing logical relationships and by deciding what information appears first in a sentence or paragraph.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest

What students learn

Students learn that cohesive texts help readers understand how ideas connect and where the message is going. Conjunctions show relationships between clauses, while sentence and paragraph starting points can foreground time, place, cause, contrast, a group of people or the main message.

Success criteria

  • choose conjunctions that match addition, contrast, cause/result, time and condition
  • explain the relationship created by a conjunction
  • move a phrase to the beginning of a sentence to change emphasis
  • choose paragraph openings that guide progression
  • use known → new information to maintain a clear topic thread

Cohesion through conjunctions

RelationshipUseful wordsExample
AdditionandMia labelled the diagram, and she added a caption.
Contrastbut, althoughAlthough the task was difficult, Kai finished it.
Cause/resultbecause, soThe tank was empty, so the pump stopped.
Timewhen, after, until, whileAfter the glue dried, we tested the model.
Conditionif, unlessUnless you save the file, your changes may be lost.

Choose the connective from the meaning relationship, not from what merely sounds familiar.

Starting points and prominence

Compare The class planted seedlings near the fence on Friday with On Friday, the class planted seedlings near the fence. The event is the same, but the second sentence gives greater prominence to time. Moving information changes the reader’s entry point and therefore the emphasis.

Paragraph openings as signposts

  • The main problem is… → introduces an issue
  • One practical solution is… → shifts to a response
  • However,… → signals contrast
  • As a result,… → signals consequence
  • For example,… → signals support

Good cohesion creates a pathway through the message: each sentence connects to what readers already know and adds something new.

Worked examples

Cause first

Because the soil was dry, the leaves wilted. The cause is foregrounded before the result.

Result first

The leaves wilted because the soil was dry. The result appears before its explanation.

Paragraph progression

“The creek was polluted. As a result, fish numbers fell. To address the problem, volunteers planted reeds and removed litter.”

Common misconceptions

  • Adding any connective does not automatically create cohesion.
  • Longer sentences are not necessarily clearer.
  • Every sentence should not begin in exactly the same way.
  • Moving a phrase often changes emphasis, not the underlying fact.
  • Paragraph openings can guide the reader; they are not decoration.
