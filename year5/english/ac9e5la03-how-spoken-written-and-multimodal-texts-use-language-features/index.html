How purpose shapes text structure, stages and language features

Identify what a text is trying to do, how its ideas are organised, and how stages, language and multimodal features help it achieve that purpose.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
What students learn

Texts are organised differently because they have different purposes. Students learn to identify a text’s main purpose, recognise dominant structures such as sequence, compare–contrast, cause–effect and problem–solution, and explain how language features and characteristic stages help readers, listeners or viewers follow the message.

Success criteria

  • separate topic from purpose
  • identify the dominant whole-text structure
  • use signal words as clues without relying on one word alone
  • describe characteristic stages in common text types
  • link a language or visual feature to the text’s purpose and organisation
  • explain how a structure helps the audience understand ideas
Purpose: what is the text trying to do?
PurposeTypical jobExample clue
InformExplain or provide informationfacts, definitions, grouped details
PersuadeInfluence a belief or actionclaim, reasons, recommendation, imperative
EntertainEngage through story, humour or imaginative experiencecharacters, events, crafted tension or humour

Trap: topic is not purpose. Two texts can both be about sharks while one informs and the other entertains. A safety notice can contain information yet mainly persuade if its central aim is to make readers act.

Texts can contain more than one relationship. Choose the label that best describes how the whole text is mainly organised.

Stages and phases

Characteristic stages help a text do its job. For example:

  • Narrative: orientation → complication → resolution
  • Persuasive text: position → reasons/evidence → response to another view → conclusion/recommendation
  • Information report: introduction/classification → grouped sections about aspects of the topic → summary where useful
  • Procedure: goal → materials/resources → ordered steps → check/result where useful

These stages are curriculum-directed extensions from the descriptor. The direct IXL evidence observed focused more strongly on purpose and written text structures than on full spoken or multimodal stage sequences.

Language and multimodal features
  • Time connectives make sequence visible.
  • Contrast connectives help readers compare ideas.
  • Causal connectives make reasons and results explicit.
  • Headings show how information is grouped.
  • Parallel columns or tables can make comparison easier.
  • Images, arrows and captions can show sequence or relationships visually.
  • Characters, setting and dialogue help organise and develop imaginative texts.

For multimodal texts, ask how layout, image order, captions, sound or spoken delivery work with words. Direct IXL examples in the research were mainly written, so SkillrHub does not claim direct IXL evidence for the full spoken/multimodal component.

Worked thinking

Example 1 — dominant structure

A text compares two animals in columns using “both”, “unlike” and “whereas”. Even if one sentence mentions what happened “later”, the dominant organisation is compare–contrast because most ideas are arranged as similarities and differences.

Example 2 — purpose

“Please bring a reusable bottle to reduce plastic waste.” It contains information, but the imperative asks readers to act, so the main purpose is persuade.

Example 3 — structure within structure

A storm explanation may use cause–effect links inside an overall sequence of formation stages. Choose the structure that best describes the whole passage.

Common misconceptions
  • Topic = purpose. The same topic can serve different purposes.
  • One signal word decides the structure. Use the whole text.
  • Any order means sequence. Every text unfolds in order; sequence is only the structure when time/stage order is the main relationship.
  • Two subjects automatically mean compare–contrast. A text about two animals may mainly explain one shared cause.
  • Visual features are decoration only. Layout and image order can organise meaning.
Teacher prompts
  • What is the text mostly trying to make the audience know, think, feel or do?
  • What relationship connects most of the ideas?
  • Which signal words support your answer?
  • Is that clue local, or does it describe the whole text?
  • What stage are we in, and what job does that stage perform?
  • How does this heading, image sequence, caption or connective guide the audience?
  • Could the same topic be organised differently for another purpose?
Curriculum coverage and research note

AC9E5LA03: describe how spoken, written and multimodal texts use language features and are typically organised into characteristic stages and phases, depending on purposes in texts.

The research provides strong direct IXL evidence for purpose, ordering, compare–contrast, cause–effect, problem–solution and identifying text structures. Spoken and multimodal stage/phase demands were not directly observed, so those are taught as curriculum extensions rather than represented as direct IXL task copies.
