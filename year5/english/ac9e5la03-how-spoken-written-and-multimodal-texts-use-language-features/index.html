AC9E5LA03 • Year 5 English

AC9E5LA03: describe how spoken, written and multimodal texts use language features and are typically organised into characteristic stages and phases, depending on purposes in texts

Identify what a text is trying to do, how its ideas are organised, and how stages, language and multimodal features help it achieve that purpose.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetBrowse app practice →Browse app practice →

What students learn

Texts are organised differently because they have different purposes. Students identify a text’s main purpose, recognise dominant structures such as sequence, compare–contrast, cause–effect and problem–solution, and explain how language features and characteristic stages help readers, listeners or viewers follow the message.

Success criteria

  • separate topic from purpose
  • identify the dominant whole-text structure
  • use signal words as clues without relying on one word alone
  • describe characteristic stages in common text types
  • link a language or visual feature to purpose and organisation
  • explain how a structure helps the audience understand ideas

Purpose: what is the text trying to do?

PurposeTypical jobExample clue
InformExplain or provide informationfacts, definitions, grouped details
PersuadeInfluence a belief or actionclaim, reasons, recommendation, imperative
EntertainEngage through story, humour or imaginative experiencecharacters, events, crafted tension or humour

Trap: topic is not purpose. Two texts can both be about sharks while one informs and the other entertains.

Four important ways ideas are organised

StructureMain relationshipCommon signals
Sequenceevents, stages or steps in orderfirst, next, afterwards, finally
Compare–contrastsimilarities and differencesboth, unlike, whereas, however
Cause–effectwhy something happens and what resultsbecause, therefore, as a result
Problem–solutionan issue and ways to address itproblem, challenge, response, solution

Texts can contain more than one relationship. Choose the label that best describes how the whole text is mainly organised.

Stages, language and multimodal features

  • Narrative: orientation → complication → resolution
  • Persuasive text: position → reasons/evidence → response to another view → conclusion/recommendation
  • Information report: classification → grouped sections → summary where useful
  • Procedure: goal → materials/resources → ordered steps → check/result

Headings, captions, arrows, image order, sound, spoken delivery and layout can all organise meaning in multimodal texts.

Worked examples

Dominant structure

A text compares two animals in columns using “both”, “unlike” and “whereas”. Even if one sentence says “later”, the dominant organisation is compare–contrast.

Purpose

“Please bring a reusable bottle to reduce plastic waste.” It contains information, but the imperative asks readers to act, so the main purpose is persuade.

Common misconceptions

  • Topic and purpose are not the same.
  • One signal word does not decide the structure.
  • Any chronological order is not automatically a sequence structure.
  • Two subjects do not automatically create compare–contrast.
  • Visual features can organise meaning; they are not only decoration.
