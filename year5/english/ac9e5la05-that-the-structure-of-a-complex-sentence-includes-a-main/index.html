AC9E5LA05 • Year 5 English

AC9E5LA05: understand that the structure of a complex sentence includes a main clause and at least one dependent clause, and understand how writers can use this structure for effect

A complex sentence contains a main clause and at least one dependent clause. Writers choose subordinators and clause order to show time, cause, condition or contrast and to shape emphasis.

What students learn

A clause contains a subject and predicate. A main clause can stand alone as a complete thought. A dependent clause cannot stand alone in the same meaning and is often introduced by a subordinating conjunction such as because, although, when, while, if, unless, until or after.

Success criteria

  • identify main and dependent clauses
  • distinguish a clause from a phrase
  • distinguish simple, compound and complex sentences
  • choose subordinators that express the intended relationship
  • punctuate an opening dependent clause clearly
  • explain how clause order changes emphasis or effect

Main clause, dependent clause or phrase?

TypeExampleTest
Main clauseThe hikers rested.Can stand alone.
Dependent clausebecause the track was floodedHas a subject and verb but leaves the thought incomplete.
Phraseunder the old bridgeNo complete subject–predicate relationship.

Trap: sentence length does not decide sentence type.

Subordinators show relationships

  • Time: when, while, after, before, until
  • Cause: because, since
  • Condition: if, unless
  • Contrast: although, though

Changing the subordinator can change the meaning: Because gives a reason; although creates contrast.

Simple, compound and complex sentences

  • Simple: one independent clause — “The dog barked.”
  • Compound: two independent clauses — “The dog barked, and the baby woke.”
  • Complex: a main clause plus at least one dependent clause — “When the dog barked, the baby woke.”

Test whether each clause can stand alone rather than relying only on conjunction words.

Clause order, punctuation and effect

Cause first

Because the road was flooded, the bus turned back. The reason is foregrounded.

Result first

The bus turned back because the road was flooded. The result is presented before its explanation.

Contrast first

Although Priya felt nervous, she stepped onto the stage. The opening dependent clause creates an expectation that the main clause then challenges.

When a dependent clause comes first, a comma normally separates it from the main clause.

Common misconceptions

  • Long sentences are not automatically complex sentences.
  • A phrase is not a clause unless it has the required clause structure.
  • The first clause is not always independent.
  • Compound and complex sentences are not the same.
  • Subordinators are not interchangeable because they express different relationships.
