Build complex sentences with main and dependent clauses

A complex sentence contains a main clause and at least one dependent clause. Writers choose subordinators and clause order to show time, cause, condition or contrast and to shape emphasis.

Topic GuideClassroom ViewWorksheetPracticeTest
What students learn

A clause contains a subject and predicate. An independent/main clause can stand alone as a complete thought. A dependent clause cannot stand alone in the same meaning and is often introduced by a subordinating conjunction such as because, although, when, while, if, unless, until or after.

Success criteria

  • identify main and dependent clauses
  • distinguish a clause from a phrase
  • distinguish simple, compound and complex sentences
  • choose subordinators that express the intended relationship
  • punctuate an opening dependent clause clearly
  • explain how clause order changes emphasis or effect

Trap: length does not decide sentence type. “The enormous, brightly painted bus with six windows stopped outside the library” is still simple because it contains one independent clause and no dependent clause.

Subordinators show relationships
  • Time: when, while, after, before, until
  • Cause/reason: because, since
  • Condition: if, unless
  • Contrast/concession: although, though, while in some contexts

Changing the subordinator can change the meaning. “Because the water was cold…” gives a reason; “Although the water was cold…” creates a contrast.

Simple, compound and complex
  • Simple: one independent clause — “The dog barked.”
  • Compound: two independent clauses joined appropriately — “The dog barked, and the baby woke.”
  • Complex: a main clause plus at least one dependent clause — “When the dog barked, the baby woke.”

Do not confuse a coordinating conjunction such as and or but with a subordinator. Test whether each clause can stand alone.

Clause order and effect

Cause first

Because the road was flooded, the bus turned back. The reason is foregrounded before the result.

Result first

The bus turned back because the road was flooded. The result is presented before its explanation.

Build expectation

Although Priya felt nervous, she stepped onto the stage. The opening dependent clause creates an expectation that the main clause then challenges.

Direct IXL evidence was strongest for identifying clause types and varied subordinators. The curriculum’s “use for effect” component is therefore taught through age-appropriate transfer rather than claimed as a directly observed IXL task sequence.

Punctuation

When a dependent clause comes first, a comma normally separates it from the main clause: When the bell rang, the students packed away. When the dependent clause follows and is closely integrated, a comma is often unnecessary: The students packed away when the bell rang.

Common misconceptions
  • Long = complex. Count clause types, not words.
  • A phrase is a clause. Look for subject + predicate.
  • The first clause is always independent. A subordinator such as as or until can make the opening clause dependent.
  • Compound and complex are the same. Compound joins independent clauses; complex includes dependence.
  • Subordinators are interchangeable. They express different logical relationships.
Teacher prompts
  • Where is the subject and predicate?
  • Can this clause stand alone?
  • Which word makes the clause dependent?
  • What relationship does that subordinator express?
  • Is this sentence simple, compound or complex? Prove it.
  • What becomes prominent if we move the dependent clause first?
  • How does this clause order suit the writer’s purpose?
