Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Repeat a clear experiment, record every valid result and use frequencies to compare and estimate chances.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Set a repeatable protocol and predict

A trial is one repetition. State what is recorded, how the setup stays consistent and how many trials you will perform.

Plan draws from a bag with three red and one blue matching counter.

  1. Predict red will occur more often because there are more red counters.
  2. Draw without looking, record the colour, replace the counter and mix before the next draw.
  3. Repeat 20 times and record every valid draw. Replacement keeps the same colour mix.

Watch for this: Without replacement, later draws use a changed collection; it is a different experiment.

Try it: Why replace the counter after each draw in this plan?

Check the answer

To keep three red and one blue counter available on every trial.

2. Conduct and record actual results

Use a tally as each trial happens. Convert tallies to frequencies and check that the total equals the number of valid trials.

Read an illustrative 20-draw record: red 14, blue 6.

  1. 14 + 6 = 20, so the record accounts for all 20 draws.
  2. Red occurred 14 out of 20 times; blue occurred 6 out of 20.
  3. These are example data for learning. Your own actual 20 draws may differ.

Watch for this: Do not discard an unexpected result to make the table fit a prediction.

Try it: Conduct your own 20 replacement draws. What must the two colour counts total?

Check the answer

20; keep and report the actual counts rather than copying the illustrative record.

3. Use relative frequency to estimate likelihood

Relative frequency compares an outcome count with the total trial count. It allows comparison when experiment lengths differ.

Compare red 14 out of 20 with red 32 out of 40.

  1. 14/20 = 7/10; 32/40 = 8/10.
  2. The second record has a larger red proportion, even after accounting for its extra trials.
  3. In a spreadsheet put counts 14 and 6 in B2:B3. =SUM(B2:B3) gives 20; =B2/SUM($B$2:$B$3) gives the red proportion.

Watch for this: A larger raw count alone does not establish a greater relative frequency.

Try it: Which is the larger blue proportion: 5/20 or 8/40?

Check the answer

5/20 = 1/4 is larger than 8/40 = 1/5.

4. Compare setups and discuss variation

Repeat both an equal-chance and an unequal-chance experiment. Compare their records with the physical setups and with other groups.

Compare a fair die labelled 1–6 with a fair cube labelled 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3.

  1. Each face is equally likely on either fair cube.
  2. Number outcomes 1, 2 and 3 on the second cube are not equally likely: their face counts differ.
  3. Roll each cube repeatedly, record number frequencies and compare. Larger trial totals can make proportions more informative but never guarantee exact expected counts.

Watch for this: A short-run difference can be chance variation; it does not alone prove cheating or predict the next result.

Try it: Can ten fair coin tosses give seven heads?

Check the answer

Yes. Equal long-run chances do not force an exact five–five split in ten trials.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: List possible outcomes and distinguish a predicted chance from a recorded result.

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
