Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Choose a useful multiplication strategy, connect it to place value and check the size of the answer.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Use doubling and factor regrouping

Multiplying by four is doubling twice. Reordering and regrouping factors can make a product easier without changing it.

Find 246 × 4.

  1. 246 × 2 = 492.
  2. Double again: 492 × 2 = 984.
  3. For 25 × 16, regroup 25 × 4 × 4 = 100 × 4 = 400.

Watch for this: Doubling both original factors makes the product four times as large.

Try it: Use doubling to find 132 × 4.

Check the answer

132 → 264 → 528.

2. Partition every place-value contribution

An area model splits a product into smaller rectangles. Each partial product must be included exactly once.

Find 324 × 8.

  1. 300 × 8 = 2 400; 20 × 8 = 160; 4 × 8 = 32.
  2. Add the contributions: 2 400 + 160 + 32 = 2 592.
  3. These are the same place-value contributions combined by a written multiplication algorithm.

Watch for this: A tens digit represents tens, not ones.

3. Multiply by a two-digit number

Split the two-digit factor into tens and ones. Written rows record those two contributions.

Find 146 × 23.

  1. 146 × 3 = 438.
  2. 146 × 20 = 2 920, because twenty means two tens.
  3. 438 + 2 920 = 3 358.

Watch for this: The tens row is 2 920, not 292; its place-value shift is essential.

Try it: Work out 125 × 12.

Check the answer

1 250 + 250 = 1 500.

4. Choose efficiency and check with an estimate

Number features suggest a strategy. Compensation adjusts the full amount added by a convenient change. Use a calculator when the context makes it useful, after predicting the scale.

Find the cost of 7 items at $198 each.

  1. Use 7 × 200 = 1 400.
  2. The convenient price adds $2 for each of seven items, so subtract $14: total $1 386.
  3. The estimate about $1 400 makes $13 860 implausible; it does not prove every digit is correct.

Watch for this: State whether you are giving an estimate or the exact product.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Recall multiplication facts and partition whole numbers into hundreds, tens and ones.

Useful vocabulary

partial product
product of partitioned parts
distributive property
multiplying across a sum of parts
reasonableness
whether an answer fits an estimate and context

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • A partial product is omitted — Record every place-value combination.
  • Place values are misaligned — Track the value of tens and hundreds, not only digits.
  • Calculator replaces reasoning — Formulate and estimate before using a tool.
  • Estimate is treated as exact — Use it to check scale, not replace the product.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
