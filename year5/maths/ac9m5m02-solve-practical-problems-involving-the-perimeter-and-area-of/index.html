Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Trace the boundary for perimeter and cover the region for area, using the right units.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Trace every outside edge

Perimeter is the total distance around a boundary, including inward turns in an irregular outline. Internal dividing lines are not part of the outside perimeter.

Find the perimeter of a 7 m by 4 m rectangular garden.

  1. Trace 7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22 m.
  2. Equivalently, double one length plus one width: 2 × (7 + 4) = 22.
  3. A regular polygon has equal sides and equal angles; a rectangle need not be regular.

Watch for this: Perimeter uses length units such as metres, not square metres.

Try it: What is the perimeter of a 6 cm by 3 cm rectangle?

Check the answer

18 cm: 6 + 3 + 6 + 3.

2. Cover the inside with square units

Area measures how much surface is covered. Count equal squares without gaps or overlaps. Rows and columns make counting efficient.

Cover a 7 m by 4 m rectangular floor.

  1. Imagine four rows, each containing seven 1 m² tiles.
  2. There are 4 × 7 = 28 square metres.
  3. The 22 m boundary and 28 m² region are different kinds of measure.

Watch for this: A bare comparison such as “28 is bigger than 22” does not compare area and perimeter as the same quantity.

The 3 m × 2 m rectangle is removed from one corner. For area, subtract that missing rectangle. For perimeter, trace only the outer boundary and include the new internal corner edges.

Try it: How many 1 cm² squares cover a 6 cm by 3 cm rectangle?

Check the answer

18 squares, so 18 cm².

3. Split an irregular region or count grid cells

Decompose a shape into non-overlapping parts, or complete a rectangle and remove a missing region. Count partial squares to estimate curved boundaries.

Find the area of a 6 m by 4 m rectangle with a 2 m by 1 m corner removed.

  1. The complete rectangle contains 24 m².
  2. The missing corner contains 2 m².
  3. The remaining area is 24 − 2 = 22 m².

Watch for this: Do not count a region twice, and do not assume a dotted partition is a hole.

Try it: What remains if a 3 m by 2 m corner is removed from an 8 m by 5 m rectangle?

Check the answer

40 − 6 = 34 m².

4. Compare designs for a practical purpose

The same perimeter can enclose different areas. State whether you are buying boundary material or covering a surface.

Compare 7 m × 4 m and 6 m × 5 m garden beds.

  1. Both perimeters are 22 m.
  2. Their areas are 28 m² and 30 m².
  3. The second provides 2 m² more planting space with the same boundary length. A real design must also allow paths.

Watch for this: Equal perimeter does not imply equal area.

An approximation is defensible when the grid scale, partial-square rule and uncertainty are stated.

Try it: Two 1 m² squares touch along one full side. Find the combined area and perimeter.

Check the answer

Area 2 m²; perimeter 6 m. The shared side is internal.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Measure lengths and count equal square units; know rows and columns.

Useful vocabulary

perimeter
distance around a boundary
area
surface covered in square units
decomposition
breaking a shape into simpler parts

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Interior grid lines included in perimeter — Trace only the external boundary.
  • Linear units used for area — Write cm² or m².
  • Missing rectangle added instead of removed — Check the physical region represented.
  • Approximation reported as exact — State method and uncertainty.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
