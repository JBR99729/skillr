Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Choose an estimate that answers the practical question, then explain what it can and cannot establish.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Decide whether the answer can be approximate

An estimate is useful for planning or spotting an implausible answer. An exact payment or an exact number of tickets needs more precision.

Check a claimed cost of $1 450 for 6 items at $24 each.

  1. Use $25 per item: 6 × 25 = 150.
  2. The total should be near $150, so $1 450 is much too high.
  3. The estimate rejects the claim without giving the exact cost.

Watch for this: A close estimate is not proof of an exact answer.

Try it: Is 8 × 49 closer to 40, 400 or 4 000?

Check the answer

400, using 8 × 50 = 400.

2. Use compatible values and explain the choice

Choose nearby numbers that are easy for the operation. Keep any rounding place specified in the question.

Estimate 398 ÷ 8.

  1. 400 is close to 398 and divisible by 8.
  2. 400 ÷ 8 = 50, so the answer is about 50.
  3. Because the dividend was increased and the divisor stayed fixed, this is a little high.

Watch for this: Rounding both numbers is not always more helpful than changing just one.

Rounding upward gives a safe sufficiency estimate. For checking an exact total, nearest-dollar rounding may be closer but could underestimate the cash needed.

Try it: Estimate 1 198 ÷ 6 using a nearby compatible dividend.

Check the answer

About 200, using 1 200 ÷ 6.

3. Track the direction of a rounding change

For positive numbers, increasing an addend increases a sum; increasing a factor increases a product. For subtraction, increasing the amount removed makes the result smaller.

Compare estimates for 502 − 198.

  1. 500 − 200 = 300: the first amount went down while the amount removed went up, so this estimate is low.
  2. For division, increasing the dividend raises the quotient, while increasing the divisor lowers it.
  3. If both numbers change, inspect the effects; do not assume they cancel.

Watch for this: Both-up or both-down rounding has no universal direction for differences or quotients.

Try it: Is 600 − 200 above or below the exact value of 598 − 203?

Check the answer

Above: the first amount increased and the amount removed decreased.

4. Use conservative budgets and sensible payment rounding

Rounding prices upwards gives a cautious shopping budget. This planning choice is different from the final amount charged.

Four items each cost less than $5. Is $20 enough?

  1. Their total is less than 4 × $5 = $20, so $20 is sufficient.
  2. For Australian cash settlement, round the final total to the nearest 5c; a $12.42 total becomes $12.40.
  3. A card payment remains $12.42. Do not round each item as a charging rule.

Watch for this: An estimate must be labelled as an estimate, not as the receipt total.

Explain the strategy, precision and whether the estimate is expected to be high, low or balanced. Reasonable does not mean identical.

Try it: Three items each cost less than $8. Is $24 enough?

Check the answer

Yes; their combined cost is less than $24.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Round whole numbers and familiar decimal amounts; know the four operations.

Useful vocabulary

compatible numbers
nearby numbers that calculate easily together
upper estimate
estimate likely at or above the exact amount
precision
level of detail retained in an estimate

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Same rounding method used everywhere — Match estimation to purpose.
  • Financial estimate rounds down unsafely — A sufficiency check may need an upper estimate.
  • Exact answer accepted without comparison — Check scale and context.
  • Estimate reported without approximation language — Use about, approximately or a range.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
