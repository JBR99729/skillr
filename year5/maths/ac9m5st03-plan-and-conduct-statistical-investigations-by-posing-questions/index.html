Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Ask a useful question, collect relevant data, choose a display and communicate a conclusion with limits.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Pose a question and define the group

A statistical question anticipates variation. Decide whose answers or which observations are relevant and what each record will mean.

Investigate preferred lunchtime activities in one class.

  1. Ask “Which activity would you most like offered at lunch?”
  2. Define the target as this class and one response per student.
  3. Offer balanced options such as games, reading, drawing, sport and another activity.

Watch for this: “Don’t you agree sport is best?” leads respondents and does not give a balanced choice.

Try it: Why define the group before collecting responses?

Check the answer

It determines who should be included and how far the conclusion can apply.

2. Plan and collect consistently

Choose a survey, observation or experiment suited to the question. Set a recording rule, gather the data and check the total.

Conduct the class activity survey.

  1. Ask the same neutral question and offer the same choices to each participant.
  2. Record one choice per person; allow “no preference” or nonresponse to be recorded clearly.
  3. Check that frequency totals match the valid response count. Do not invent missing responses.

Watch for this: A worked example table is not evidence that you conducted the investigation.

3Sample/methodwho, how, one response each

5Interpretmode, shape, spread
6Reportconclusion and limitation

Nominal categories have no inherent order; ordinal categories have a meaningful order, such as strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Try it: Carry out a small survey with an agreed group. What should your record include?

Check the answer

The question, group, date, method, actual responses, category definitions and any missing responses.

3. Choose a display that answers the question

A frequency table or column graph compares category counts. A line graph suits measured change over time, not unordered favourite activities.

Display illustrative results: games 7, reading 4, drawing 5 and sport 8.

  1. The 24 responses can be organised in a frequency table.
  2. Draw four separated equal-width bars with a consistent scale, title and category/count axis labels.
  3. Sport has the most responses, but 16 respondents chose something else.

Watch for this: The most frequent choice does not necessarily have more than half of the responses.

Try it: Does sport have a majority of these 24 responses?

Check the answer

No. Eight is the largest category, but it is less than half of 24.

4. Interpret, communicate and improve

Answer the original question using actual evidence. State limitations and a sensible next action; do not generalise beyond the group studied.

Report the illustrative survey.

  1. “Sport was the most selected option in this class: 8 of 24 responses.”
  2. “Other classes may prefer different activities; absent students were not represented.”
  3. To plan for the whole school, gather broader, relevant responses before deciding.

Watch for this: A graph alone is not a complete conclusion; explain its meaning in the investigation.

Avoid claiming causation or generalising beyond the sample. Digital tools organise and display data but do not fix a biased question or sample.

Try it: What would you change if many students selected “another activity”?

Check the answer

Ask a follow-up neutral question to identify those activities, then revise categories and gather suitable evidence.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Sort categories, tally counts and interpret a labelled graph.

Useful vocabulary

nominal data
categories with no inherent order
ordinal data
categories with a meaningful order
spread
extent to which values vary

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Ordinal categories treated as equal numerical intervals — Order is meaningful, but distances may not be.
  • Leading question used — Wording should not push responses.
  • Sample treated as whole population — Limit the conclusion.
  • Software chart chosen automatically — Select a display that fits variable and question.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
