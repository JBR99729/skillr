Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Name common fractional units before combining or removing amounts.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Add or remove the same-sized parts

The denominator names the fractional unit; the numerator counts how many of those units you have.

Work out 5/8 − 2/8.

  1. Remove two eighths from five eighths.
  2. Three eighths remain: 5/8 − 2/8 = 3/8.
  3. For addition, 3/8 + 4/8 = 7/8 uses the same eighth-sized unit.

Watch for this: Adding denominators would change the size of the parts.

Try it: Find 2/7 + 4/7.

Check the answer

6/7: two sevenths plus four sevenths is six sevenths.

2. Rename related units before calculating

Split each larger fractional part into smaller equal parts of the same whole. Use strips, a number line or equivalent fractions.

Find 1/2 + 3/8.

  1. One half is four eighths: 1/2 = 4/8.
  2. Combine 4/8 + 3/8 = 7/8.
  3. A jump of four eighths followed by three eighths reaches seven eighths.

Watch for this: Only rename when needed: related denominators already have an efficient common unit.

Another worked model

Add 3/4 + 5/8 by renaming quarters as eighths

3/4 = 6/8
+ 5/8
11/8 = 1 3/8

Six eighths plus five eighths equals eleven eighths. The denominator remains 8 because the parts are still eighths.

Try it: Find 5/6 − 1/3.

Check the answer

5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.

3. Cross a whole with fractions and mixed numbers

A whole can be regrouped into fractional parts without changing the total.

Cut 3/4 m from a 2 1/4 m strip.

  1. Rename 2 1/4 m as 1 5/4 m.
  2. Remove 3/4 m: 1 5/4 − 3/4 = 1 2/4 m = 1 1/2 m.
  3. Check by adding the cut piece back: 1 1/2 + 3/4 = 2 1/4 m.

Watch for this: Keep the measurement unit. Equivalent unsimplified fractions are valid unless simplest form is requested.

Try it: Find 3/4 + 1/2.

Check the answer

5/4 = 1 1/4, because 1/2 = 2/4.

4. Choose a model for the situation

A clock can model fractional hours, a marked strip can model length and a cup can model capacity. The whole and operation must match the context.

Add half an hour and three-quarters of an hour.

  1. Half an hour is two quarters of an hour.
  2. 2/4 + 3/4 = 5/4 hours = 1 1/4 hours.
  3. Each quarter-hour is 15 minutes, so this is 75 minutes.

Watch for this: “Subtract a from b” means b − a. Do not automatically subtract in the order the quantities appear.

Choose a strategy based on the relationship between denominators and benchmarks. Simplify or rename the final result where useful.

Try it: A recipe uses 3/4 cup, then another 1/4 cup. What is the total?

Check the answer

One cup: four quarters make one whole cup.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Know equivalent fractions, related denominators and mixed numbers.

Useful vocabulary

common denominator
shared denominator representing equal-sized parts
complement
amount needed to complete a benchmark such as one whole
improper fraction
fraction whose numerator is at least its denominator

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Numerators and denominators both added — Keep the common denominator because part size does not change.
  • Fractions combined before renaming — Different denominators name different-sized parts.
  • Whole crossed but not interpreted — Rename an improper result as a mixed numeral when context benefits.
  • Same denominator assumed from appearance — Check actual denominator relationship and the same whole.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
