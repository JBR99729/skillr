Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Use one equal-group model to explain related multiplication and division facts.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Identify the total and the two factors

Multiplication combines equal groups. Division can recover the size of a group or the number of groups from that total.

Model 4 groups of 6 counters.

  1. 4 × 6 = 24 gives the total.
  2. 24 ÷ 4 = 6 shares that total among four groups.
  3. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 asks how many groups of six fit.

Watch for this: Swapping factors is commutativity; using division to undo multiplication is the inverse relationship.

Try it: Five groups contain 7 objects each. What division facts match?

Check the answer

35 ÷ 5 = 7 and 35 ÷ 7 = 5.

2. Write a connected family of facts

Turning an array swaps rows and columns while keeping the total. Connect both multiplication orders to their division facts.

Write the family for 3, 8 and 24.

  1. 3 × 8 = 24 and 8 × 3 = 24.
  2. 24 ÷ 3 = 8 and 24 ÷ 8 = 3.
  3. Every statement describes the same 24 objects.

Watch for this: When the two factors are equal, some statements repeat: 6 × 6 = 36 and 36 ÷ 6 = 6 give only two distinct equations.

Another worked model

Build a fact family from 8 × 14 = 112

The product is the whole and the factors are related parts of the multiplicative structure. Division checks multiplication and solves missing-factor problems.

Try it: How many distinct equations are in the 5, 5, 25 fact family?

Check the answer

Two: 5 × 5 = 25 and 25 ÷ 5 = 5.

3. Use inverse facts to calculate efficiently

A friendly multiplication can reveal a division without repeated subtraction. Think about the sizes of the groups.

Find 240 ÷ 20.

  1. 12 × 2 = 24, so 12 × 20 = 240.
  2. Therefore 240 ÷ 20 = 12.
  3. Check by recombining twelve groups of twenty.

Watch for this: Do not change the total while switching between related facts.

Try it: Use 14 × 6 = 84 to find 84 ÷ 14.

Check the answer

6, because fourteen groups of six make 84.

4. Explain halving and the zero boundary

Halving twice divides into four equal parts. Multiplication checks the shared result. Zero cannot be a divisor.

Find 68 ÷ 4 by halving.

  1. Half of 68 is 34; half of 34 is 17.
  2. Four seventeens make 68, so the answer is 17.
  3. 0 × 5 = 0 connects to 0 ÷ 5 = 0, but not to 0 ÷ 0.

Watch for this: Dividing by zero is undefined; do not manufacture a four-equation family with zero.

Another worked model

Scale a fact family while preserving structure

8 × 14 = 112→80 × 14 = 1 120→1 120 ÷ 80 = 14→1 120 ÷ 14 = 80

Scaling one factor by 10 scales the product by 10. The related division facts change consistently because the relationship is preserved.

Try it: Use halving twice to find 92 ÷ 4.

Check the answer

46, then 23. Check 4 × 23 = 92.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Know groups, rows, columns, a total and multiplication facts.

Useful vocabulary

inverse operations
operations that undo one another
fact family
set of related equations using the same values
factor
number multiplied to form a product

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Division fact uses a different product — Related facts must preserve the same whole.
  • Every order change is allowed — Multiplication is commutative; division generally is not.
  • Scaling factor but not product — If one factor changes, the product changes proportionally.
  • Fact recalled without explanation — Connect the equation to groups, arrays or missing factors.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
