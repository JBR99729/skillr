FAQ

Does every connected arrangement of six squares fold into a cube?

No. Some arrangements overlap when folded or leave a face uncovered.

Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Connect faces to an object, predict folds and build a net that closes correctly.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Connect each face to the object

A net is a connected flat arrangement of faces that folds into a three-dimensional object. Count hidden faces too.

Compare a triangular prism and a square pyramid.

  1. A right triangular prism has two matching triangular ends and three rectangular side faces.
  2. A square pyramid has one square base and four triangles meeting at an apex.
  3. The base is one of the faces, not an additional component.

Watch for this: Having the correct number of faces is necessary but does not prove that an arrangement will fold.

Try it: What faces does a cube need?

Check the answer

Six equal squares.

2. Track folds and opposite faces

Faces fold along shared edges. Imagine one face as the base, then lift neighbouring faces one at a time and check for overlap.

Fold the labelled cube cross shown.

  1. Use B as a base. Its neighbours A, C, E and F become four side faces.
  2. D folds over to close the remaining face opposite B.
  3. Opposite pairs are A–C, B–D and E–F. Six squares in one straight row cannot close a cube.

Watch for this: Faces touching only at a point do not provide an edge to fold along.

Try it: In this cross, which face is opposite E?

Check the answer

F; they fold from opposite sides of B.

3. Sketch, cut, fold and test

Use compatible edge lengths. Distinguish the outside cut boundary from internal fold lines. Joining tabs help assembly but are not extra faces.

Build a cube from the cross net.

  1. Choose a square side length, such as 3 cm, and draw all six squares to that size.
  2. Add small joining tabs where needed, cut the outside boundary and score or crease the shared edges.
  3. Fold, join and check that every edge closes without overlap or gaps; revise the drawing if it fails.

Watch for this: An adult can help with safe cutting while the learner makes the spatial decisions.

Try it: How would you check a proposed net before adding glue?

Check the answer

Fold it dry, match the free edges and check that every required face is present and closes correctly.

4. Design packaging for a purpose

A useful package must fit its contents and have a workable way to open and join. Label the dimensions before constructing it.

Plan a rectangular box for an item 6 cm long, 4 cm wide and 2 cm high.

  1. Use two 6 × 4 faces, two 6 × 2 faces and two 4 × 2 faces as the minimum mathematical face sizes.
  2. Arrange a connected net with matching shared edges; allow practical clearance for the item and material thickness.
  3. Make a paper trial and decide which face becomes the lid.

Watch for this: A face list is not a finished net; test the arrangement and opening.

Try it: What must change if a cardboard trial is too tight?

Check the answer

Increase the relevant inside dimensions or clearance and revise the matching faces and edges together.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Recognise faces, edges, vertices, prisms and pyramids.

Useful vocabulary

net
two-dimensional arrangement of faces that folds into a 3D object
face
flat surface of a polyhedron
edge
line segment where two faces meet

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Any six squares form a cube net — Some arrangements overlap or fail to close.
  • Tabs counted as faces — Tabs are construction aids.
  • Corresponding edges ignored — Meeting edges must have equal lengths.
  • Curved surface called a face without clarification — Use surface terminology appropriate to the object.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
