Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Read time and value scales, compare changes and distinguish observations from estimates and predictions.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Read the axes, units and plotted values

A line graph connects measurements over time. Identify the time unit, quantity unit and intervals before reading a point.

Read the illustrative tank-volume graph.

  1. The horizontal axis is time in hours; the vertical axis is volume in litres.
  2. At 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 hours the plotted volumes are 10, 14, 14, 8 and 12 litres.
  3. The line passes through each labelled observation.

Watch for this: The graph’s height represents the quantity, not elapsed time.

Try it: What volume was recorded at 2 hours?

Check the answer

14 litres.

2. Compare changes over equal periods

Rising sections show increases, falling sections decreases and horizontal sections no change. Compare numerical changes over equal time intervals.

Compare the first three one-hour intervals.

  1. From hour 0 to 1, volume increases by 4 L.
  2. From 1 to 2, it stays at 14 L.
  3. From 2 to 3, it decreases by 6 L: the largest change in magnitude among these intervals.

Watch for this: A steep-looking line can mislead if axes or time intervals differ; check both scales.

Another worked model

Construct a temperature-over-time line graph

time (min)05101520temperature (°C)2024313536change+4+7+4+1

Plot time on the horizontal axis, temperature on the vertical axis and connect points because values are ordered through time.

Try it: How much does the volume increase from hour 3 to 4?

Check the answer

4 L: 12 − 8.

3. Estimate between observations carefully

A point on a connecting segment can give an estimate between recorded times. The actual quantity may have changed differently between observations.

Estimate the volume at 3.5 hours.

  1. The segment rises from 8 L at hour 3 to 12 L at hour 4.
  2. Halfway on this straight segment is about 10 L.
  3. This is an estimate, not an additional measured observation.

Watch for this: A line does not guarantee that real change was constant between the recorded points.

Try it: Was 10 L at 3.5 hours directly measured in the example?

Check the answer

No; only whole-hour values were supplied.

4. Match stories and limit conclusions

Use a graph’s pattern to support a story, then check whether the labels and context allow the conclusion.

What could explain the tank graph?

  1. An increase could show filling; a level section could show no net change; a decrease could show water leaving.
  2. These are plausible explanations, not proven causes.
  3. The graph stops at 4 hours and does not determine the volume at 10 hours. Predictions from past trends can fail if conditions change.

Watch for this: A larger final value does not mean the quantity increased throughout.

Another worked model

Compare full and discontinuous vertical scales

0–100 scalesmall changes look modest
30–40 scale with breaksame changes look dramatic
responsible uselabel break clearly and discuss actual values
conclusionvisual steepness alone is insufficient

A discontinuous scale can reveal detail but can also exaggerate differences. Quote numerical change and inspect axis intervals.

Try it: Can you conclude the tank was always filling from hour 0 to 4?

Check the answer

No. The graph has a constant section and a falling section.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Read labelled axes and subtract to compare measurements.

Useful vocabulary

line graph
graph connecting data values across an ordered variable
vertical axis
axis displaying the measured response
discontinuous scale
axis with an explicitly omitted interval

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Categories connected as if continuous — Use line graphs only when order or continuity is meaningful.
  • Axis break ignored — Inspect labels before judging steepness.
  • Line assumed to prove values between points — Interpolation is an estimate unless measured.
  • Trend described as cause — The graph shows relationship, not necessarily cause.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
