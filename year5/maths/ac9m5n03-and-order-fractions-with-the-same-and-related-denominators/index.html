Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Use equal-sized wholes, equivalent fractions and number lines to compare fractions and mixed numbers.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Keep the whole and the fractional unit clear

A fraction wall shows parts of an area or strip. A number line shows a point measured from zero. Equal fractions share a line position when the whole unit is fixed.

Compare 3/8 and 5/8.

  1. Both count eighths of the same whole.
  2. Five of these equal parts are more than three, so 3/8 < 5/8.

Watch for this: Different-sized source wholes can make a picture comparison misleading.

Try it: Which is greater: 7/10 or 4/10?

Check the answer

7/10, because seven tenths exceed four tenths.

2. Rename related denominators

Use a multiple of the smaller denominator to make the units match. Multiply numerator and denominator by the same number.

Compare 2/3 and 5/6.

  1. Each third splits into two sixths: 2/3 = 4/6.
  2. Compare 4/6 with 5/6: 2/3 < 5/6.

Watch for this: Changing only the denominator changes the amount.

Another worked model

Compare 3/4 and 5/8 using a related denominator

3/4 = 6/8
5/8

Because 3/4 = 6/8, it is greater than 5/8. Scaling numerator and denominator by the same factor preserves value.

Try it: Compare 3/4 and 7/8.

Check the answer

3/4 = 6/8, so 3/4 < 7/8.

3. Move between mixed numbers and improper fractions

An improper fraction can include complete wholes. A mixed number records the complete wholes and the remaining fractional part.

Locate 11/4.

  1. Eight quarters make two wholes; three quarters remain.
  2. 11/4 = 2 3/4, three quarter-steps after 2 and before 3.

Watch for this: A mixed number such as 2 3/4 means 2 + 3/4, not 2 × 3/4.

Try it: Write 3 2/5 as an improper fraction.

Check the answer

17/5: three wholes are fifteen fifths, then add two fifths.

4. Order a mixed set and justify the positions

Compare whole-number parts first. When these match, compare the fractional parts with common units.

Order 1 1/2, 7/4 and 1 1/4.

  1. Rename in quarters: 6/4, 7/4 and 5/4.
  2. From least to greatest: 1 1/4, 1 1/2, 7/4.
  3. All lie between 1 and 2; 7/4 = 1 3/4 is furthest right.

Watch for this: Keep the original labels in the final ordered list so the answer matches the question.

Another worked model

Order mixed numerals on a number line

1 1/43/21 5/89/42 1/2
13

Compare whole-number parts first, then fractional parts. Use common denominators or convert mixed and improper forms when necessary.

Try it: Order 2 1/3, 2 5/6 and 2 1/6.

Check the answer

2 1/6 < 2 1/3 < 2 5/6, using 1/3 = 2/6.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Know equal parts, numerator, denominator and multiplication facts.

Useful vocabulary

related denominators
denominators connected by multiplication or common factors
benchmark fraction
familiar value such as 0, 1/2 or 1 used for comparison
mixed numeral
whole number combined with a proper fraction

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Larger denominator means larger fraction — With the same numerator, more equal parts means smaller parts.
  • Numerators compared when denominators differ — First establish common-sized parts or use a benchmark.
  • Mixed numeral whole part ignored — Compare whole numbers before fractional remainders.
  • Different-sized wholes used — Fraction comparison assumes the same whole.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
