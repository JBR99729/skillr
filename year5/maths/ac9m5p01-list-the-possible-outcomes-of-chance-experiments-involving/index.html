Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: List possible results systematically and use the chance setup to judge whether the results are equally likely.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Define what counts as an outcome

An outcome is one possible recorded result. The same experiment can have different outcome lists depending on what is recorded.

Draw one card from a well-shuffled standard 52-card deck.

  1. Recording the exact card gives 52 possible outcomes.
  2. Recording the suit gives four outcomes; recording its colour gives red or black.
  3. These outcome descriptions answer different questions about the same draw.

Watch for this: Do not mix exact cards and colours in one inconsistent outcome list.

Try it: List the outcomes when recording a fair six-sided die’s top number.

Check the answer

1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, each listed once.

2. Explain an equal-chance setup

Equally likely means each specified outcome has the same chance. Justify it from the physical setup or stated fair model.

Draw one of five different names from a hat.

  1. Use one same-sized folded slip per person and mix the slips thoroughly.
  2. With no way to favour a slip, each person has the same chance.
  3. Two slips for one person would change the name chances even if every slip is mixed fairly.

Watch for this: Possible does not mean equally likely. A random procedure can still produce unequal category chances.

Recognise when outcomes are not equally likely

everyday eventrequires evidence, not simple counting

Do not use favourable/total counting unless elementary outcomes are equally likely or the probability structure is otherwise known.

Try it: A fair coin has which equally likely face outcomes?

Check the answer

Heads and tails.

3. Distinguish individual objects from colour outcomes

Individual matching counters can have equal chances while repeated colours have unequal chances. Spinner colour chances depend on the coloured shares of the whole.

Draw from a bag containing three red and one blue matching counter.

  1. There are four individual counters, with one blue opportunity and three red opportunities.
  2. The possible colour results are red and blue, but red is more likely.
  3. A fair spinner with four equal sectors, three red and one blue, models the same colour comparison.

Watch for this: Two colour names do not create equal chances.

Try it: Four equal spinner sectors are red, red, blue and green. Which colour is most likely?

Check the answer

Red, because two of the four equal sectors are red.

4. Judge fairness and keep evidence separate

A game is fair between players when their winning rules give equal chances under the stated setup. A small run of wins does not prove the setup is biased.

On a fair die, one player wins on 1 or 2; another on 3, 4, 5 or 6.

  1. The first player has two equally likely number outcomes.
  2. The second has four, so the rules favour the second player.
  3. Giving each player three different numbers would balance their chances.

Watch for this: Promotional collections or computer recommendations should not be assumed unbiased merely because several labels are possible; ask how selections are made.

Try it: Player A wins on odd and player B on even. Is this fair on a fair six-sided die?

Check the answer

Yes: each has three equally likely number outcomes.

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Event names assumed equally likely — Repeated counters or unequal sectors change probability.
  • Outcomes omitted or duplicated — List systematically.
  • Probability fraction uses event names as denominator — Use total equally likely outcomes.
  • Probability exceeds 1 — Valid probability lies from 0 to 1.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
