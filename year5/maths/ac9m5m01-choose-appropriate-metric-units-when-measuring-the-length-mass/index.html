Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Choose a suitable unit and instrument, then use smaller units when the measurement needs finer detail.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. Match the attribute and the scale of the object

Length measures distance, mass measures how much matter and capacity measures how much a container can hold. Choose a unit that makes the result useful.

Choose units for a pencil, a schoolbag and a drink bottle.

  1. Centimetres or millimetres suit the pencil’s length.
  2. Kilograms suit the loaded bag’s mass; millilitres suit a small bottle’s capacity.
  3. For length: kilometre > metre > centimetre > millimetre.

Watch for this: Amount currently in a container is not necessarily its full capacity.

Try it: Which unit suits the distance between towns: millimetres or kilometres?

Check the answer

Kilometres; they suit the large distance.

2. Read intervals and align the instrument

Check the labelled unit and the size of each interval. Align a ruler’s zero with the start, or subtract the two endpoint readings.

An object starts at 2 cm and ends at 8.4 cm on a ruler.

  1. Its length is the difference, not the final reading.
  2. 8.4 − 2 = 6.4 cm.
  3. Read a scale with 10 equal intervals between 100 g and 200 g as 10 g per interval.

Watch for this: The physical edge of the ruler may not be its zero mark.

Try it: What is the length from 3 cm to 9.7 cm?

Check the answer

6.7 cm, the difference between the endpoints.

3. Combine units to show finer detail

A metre contains 100 centimetres; a centimetre contains 10 millimetres. One kilogram is 1 000 grams and one litre is 1 000 millilitres.

Record a jump of 2 m and 37 cm.

  1. The two complete metres are 200 cm.
  2. 200 cm + 37 cm = 237 cm, or 2.37 m.
  3. A mass of 1 kg 80 g similarly means 1 080 g, not 180 g.

Watch for this: Mixed units cannot simply be joined as digit strings.

Try it: Write 1 L 250 mL in millilitres.

Check the answer

1 250 mL.

4. Measure, compare and explain the needed accuracy

Use a tool whose graduations suit the job, and repeat a careful measurement. Smaller units can record finer detail; they do not fix poor technique.

Measure a book’s width to decide whether it fits a shelf space.

  1. Estimate its width, then align a ruler correctly.
  2. Read centimetres and remaining millimetres, keeping your viewing angle consistent.
  3. Compare with the actual shelf gap, leaving any required clearance.

Watch for this: Writing extra decimal digits does not create a more accurate observation.

Try it: Why might millimetres be useful for a 1 cm-wide shelf clearance?

Check the answer

They show small differences that whole-centimetre readings can miss; careful alignment is still necessary.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Recognise metres, centimetres, millimetres, grams, kilograms, litres and millilitres.

Useful vocabulary

metric unit
standard SI-linked unit used for measurement
benchmark
familiar reference measurement used for estimation
precision
detail supported by the unit and instrument

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • Unit chosen from object name rather than attribute — One object can have length, mass, capacity and temperature.
  • Largest unit assumed most accurate — Precision depends on scale and purpose.
  • Different units compared directly — Convert to a common unit.
  • Estimate has no benchmark — Reference a known measurement or grouping.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
