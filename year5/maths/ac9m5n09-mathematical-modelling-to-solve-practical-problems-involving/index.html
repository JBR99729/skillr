Learn the skill

Four focused steps

Learning goal: Turn a practical goal into a mathematical plan, calculate, then check and explain the result in context.

Study a worked example, explain the reasoning and try its short check before moving on.

1. State the goal and the missing information

A model is a mathematical description of a situation. Identify what must be found, relevant quantities, units and constraints before choosing operations.

Plan enough notebooks for 28 students, sold in packs of 6.

  1. Assume each student needs one notebook and no spare notebooks are already available.
  2. 4 packs contain only 24; 5 packs contain 30.
  3. The model requires five packs, with two notebooks spare.

Watch for this: A supplied expression alone does not show how the situation was formulated.

Try it: What extra information is needed to calculate the purchase cost?

Check the answer

The price per pack, and whether any delivery or other charges apply.

2. Represent the relationships and choose operations

Use an equal-group diagram, bar model, table or number sentence to show why each calculation is needed.

Six groups need 4 markers each; 7 usable markers are already available.

  1. Six groups of four require 6 × 4 = 24 markers.
  2. Subtract the 7 already available: 24 − 7 = 17 more.
  3. The sentence 6 × 4 − 7 = 17 records the situation.

Watch for this: The word “each” describes a relationship; read the whole problem before choosing an operation.

Another worked model

Use a modelling cycle

understand situation→define quantities/constraints→represent→formulate operations→solve→validate→interpret/recommend

Digital tools help compare many scenarios after the formula is understood. State assumptions and test whether reasonable changes affect the recommendation.

Try it: Five tables need six pencils each, with eight pencils already available. How many more?

Check the answer

5 × 6 − 8 = 22 more pencils.

3. Build a budget and use a spreadsheet

Distinguish sales income, expenses and money remaining after expenses. A spreadsheet can make changing assumptions visible.

A class sells 40 tickets at $3 each and spends $45 on materials.

  1. Income: 40 × $3 = $120.
  2. Net proceeds after materials: $120 − $45 = $75.
  3. In a spreadsheet put ticket count in B2, price in C2 and expenses in D2; =B2*C2-D2 computes net proceeds.

Watch for this: This model assumes all 40 tickets sell and there are no other costs. A digital calculation still needs a checked model.

Try it: If only 30 tickets sell with the same price and expenses, what remains?

Check the answer

$45: 30 × $3 − $45.

4. Validate, communicate and revise

Check units, arithmetic and whether the answer meets the original goal. Change assumptions when the practical evidence changes.

At $3 per ticket and fixed costs of $45, does selling 40 tickets raise at least $80 after costs?

  1. The original plan gives $75, below the $80 target.
  2. At 42 tickets the net is $126 − $45 = $81; at 41 it is $78.
  3. If capacity allows, 42 ticket sales meet the target under the stated assumptions. Communicate the minimum and the assumptions.

Watch for this: An open plan may have several valid solutions; justify one rather than claiming it is universally best.

Try it: What happens to the 42-ticket plan if expenses rise by $5?

Check the answer

Net proceeds become $76, so the plan no longer meets the $80 target.

Vocabulary and teaching help

Before this lesson: Select addition, subtraction, multiplication or division from equal-group and comparison situations.

Useful vocabulary

constraint
condition a valid solution must satisfy
assumption
stated simplification accepted for the model
validation
checking calculations, constraints and whether the model answers the question

Help a learner get started

Open one focus at a time. Read the worked steps together, ask the learner to explain what each quantity means, then let them try the short check before opening its answer. Use paper, objects or a drawing when that makes the idea clearer.

Common errors and quick fixes

  • All numbers used automatically — Select only relevant quantities and relationships.
  • Constraint checked after recommendation only — Build constraints into the model.
  • Tool output accepted without formula check — Explain cells, operations or equations.
  • Final number lacks interpretation — State what should be done and why.

Keep the challenge meaningful: A practical construction, investigation or digital activity needs the learner’s actual work. A correct spoken answer alone does not demonstrate that practical skill.
