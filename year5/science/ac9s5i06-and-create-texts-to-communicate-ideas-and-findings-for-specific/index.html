YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5I06

Explain the same science to different audiences

Learning intention: Choose language, structure and visual or digital tools for a clear purpose and audience while keeping findings accurate.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two audiences, one unchanged result

A younger reader may need “a dark area where the card blocks some light”. A classmate may use “shadow” and a labelled path model. Both versions must preserve the same evidence: in the invented dataset, greater torch-to-card distance from 10 to 30 cm is associated with shorter shadows in the fixed setup.

Illustrations provide context. Use the supplied labelled records and models for exact values; do not measure the artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Choose audience, purpose and structure

State who will read or hear the text and what they should understand. A poster, short report or spoken explanation can work if its structure serves that purpose.

A specific brief

Explain to a younger class why a card makes a shadow. Introduce the torch and card, define the shadow and use one labelled picture.

Try it · Does “make a slideshow” identify an audience or scientific purpose?

No. It identifies a format. State who it is for and what scientific idea or finding it should communicate.

2. Use useful vocabulary and faithful evidence

Define unfamiliar terms and connect a claim to relevant records. Simpler words should keep the scientific meaning and limits. Technical words alone do not make an explanation better.

A short report model

In the supplied model, shadows at a torch distance of 10 cm were 19–21 cm high; at 30 cm they were 13–14 cm. The card stayed 10 cm from the screen. These records support shorter shadows at greater torch distances in this setup.

Try it · May a younger-audience version say “the card makes light vanish” to sound simpler?

No. Say it blocks some light from reaching the screen. Simplification must preserve the mechanism.

3. Select and review visual or digital tools

Choose a labelled diagram for paths, a table for exact records or a graph for a pattern. Check labels, units, copied values and whether the tool helps the audience understand.

ToolMeaning check
Annotated setup picturePositions and labels match the stated method
GraphCorrect axes, units and data series
Digital reportReadable text, faithful values and clear data status
Try it · Would an attractive animation excuse changing a table value?

No. The evidence must stay faithful. Check every displayed value and label against the source records.

Common mistakes

“More technical words always means clearer science.” Use terms that help the audience, and explain unfamiliar ones.

“Different audiences need different facts.” Adapt language and organisation while preserving the same evidence and accurate scientific meaning.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain it twice and compare

2 minutes: choose a scientific finding from the supplied model. 3 minutes: write two sentences for a younger class. 3 minutes: write a short evidence-based report for classmates. 2 minutes: compare meanings, numbers and limitations.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the report model and define one term. Extend: justify choosing a diagram instead of a graph for an explanation of blocked paths.

Do not identify real learners or share photographs, voices or work without the appropriate permission. A paper report is an equally valid option.

Three tricky questions

1. Can an everyday word be appropriate in a scientific explanation?

Yes, if its meaning is clear and accurate in the context. Define a scientific term when it adds useful precision.

2. Why label the supplied dataset as invented?

Readers should not mistake a teaching model for an experiment actually conducted by a class or for measured evidence from the illustration.

3. How should feedback improve a science report?

Check whether the audience understands the idea, whether evidence supports the claim and whether wording, labels or values need correction.

FAQ

Must I always use a computer?

No. Choose digital tools when they help the purpose; a clear paper diagram or spoken explanation may be suitable.

How can I check two audience versions?

Compare the scientific claim, data, units, method and limits. Then check that the language and organisation suit each audience.

What students learn in AC9S5I06

Students construct coherent scientific reports, explanations and presentations, integrate labelled representations, cite relevant evidence and adapt detail for audiences without changing the science.

Scientific communication connects purpose, evidence and explanation in a form the intended audience can follow and check.

A good visual is referenced and interpreted in the text or speech; it is not an isolated decoration.

Adapt vocabulary support and amount of detail, not data values or scientific meaning. Attribute external sources and images.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: write and create texts to communicate ideas and findings for specific purposes and audiences, including selection of language features, using digital tools as appropriate.

  • E1: exploring how language features such as vocabulary and sentence structure help shape a text and give it meaning
  • E2: acknowledging and exploring First Nations Australians’ ways of representing and communicating information about anatomical features
  • E3: developing a digital presentation to share information about the structural features or behaviours of animals and plants in a particular habitat
  • E4: constructing a persuasive text for local council to argue the use of an erosion mitigation strategy in a local area
  • E5: co-authoring a scientific report on an investigation into the behaviours of light using appropriate vocabulary, data representations and sentence structures
  • E6: exploring whether there is a ‘correct’ way of representing particles and creating an animation to teach other students about the particulate nature of matter

Common misconceptions
  • Results shown without finding — State the pattern and its relevance.
  • Visual repeats all text — Use it to organise or extend meaning.
  • Audience adaptation simplifies inaccurately — Preserve scientific relationships.
  • Source omitted — Attribute external evidence and media.
