WORKED EXAMPLE

A shadow test needs more than equipment

The same upright card stays 10 cm from the screen. Only the torch-to-card distance changes. The supplied numbers are invented model records, not measurements from the illustration or an actual class. A repeatable plan must also state how to set the torch distances, read shadow height, keep alignment and record each run. Repeating a procedure does not mean every reading will be identical.

Plan featureSpecific instruction
ChangeTorch front to card plane: 10, 20 and 30 cm
MeasureShadow top to bottom on the screen, nearest centimetre
Keep the sameCard and its orientation; card-to-screen distance 10 cm; torch, alignment and lighting
RepeatReset and record three runs at each distance
SafetyStable equipment; no torch beam towards eyes; clear walkway

Illustrations provide context. Use the supplied labelled records and models for exact values; do not measure the artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Specify changed, measured and controlled variables

Choose variables that match the question. In a fair comparison, change the torch-to-card distance and measure shadow height. Keep other relevant conditions the same.

Endpoint precision

Measure torch distance from the same front reference point to the card plane. Use the same shadow top-to-bottom rule in every run.

Try it · Would “put the torch a bit farther away” make the procedure repeatable?

No. Give distances, reference points and a measurement rule so another group can reconstruct the setup.

2. Plan repeats and manage risks

Describe setup, steps, recording and reset actions. Repeats reveal variation; they cannot fix a method that changes several relevant variables together. Match each hazard to a control and a stop or change decision.

HazardControl or decision
Equipment can fallStable bases; stop and reset if a stand moves
Beam could enter an eyeAim at screen below eye level; adult supervises
Trip on setupKeep the route clear; relocate if needed
Try it · If all three runs use a different card height, do repeats repair the unfair method?

No. The card change remains a confounding variable. Repair the procedure, then collect comparable repeat records.

3. Check Country/Place permissions before fieldwork

For an investigation on Country/Place, use school and local community guidance to identify the appropriate First Nations authority and land-manager requirements before access. Explain the proposed activity and respect conditions or refusal.

Different actions need checking

Permission to walk an approved route does not automatically permit collecting, recording a knowledge holder or sharing knowledge. Ask through the appropriate teacher and local authority before those actions.

Try it · If a park route is public, may we assume permission to collect plants or publish cultural knowledge?

No. Public access is not blanket permission for collecting, recording or sharing. Check the relevant local requirements and use an approved alternative if needed.

Common mistakes

“Repeatable means all results must match.” The procedure can be repeated even when observations vary. Record the variation honestly.

“School approval covers every cultural and access permission.” Identify the relevant permissions and local protocols separately; do not invent consent.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Audit and improve a plan

2 minutes: read the supplied shadow plan. 4 minutes: add a reset step, exact reference points and a recording table. 2 minutes: pair a risk with a control. 2 minutes: explain what extra permissions would need checking for a proposed field investigation.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the changed/measured/controlled headings. Extend: explain why adding more runs cannot remove a systematic setup error.

Paper planning is enough. Actual experiments or field visits need teacher-approved equipment, supervision and required local permissions before starting.

Three tricky questions

1. Does every investigation have to be a fair test?

No. Observation, surveying and modelling may be appropriate. Where a fair test is used, choose the variables and controls that answer its question.

2. Why record an unexpected result instead of replacing it with the prediction?

It is evidence about what happened. Record it and investigate possible causes without inventing a preferred measurement.

3. A walk is approved but collecting samples is not. What should the plan say?

Stay within the approved activity. Seek the relevant permission through the teacher or omit collecting and use an approved non-invasive alternative.

FAQ

What makes a procedure repeatable?

It provides enough detail about materials, quantities, positions, steps, conditions and measurements for another group to follow it.

Who grants permission on Country/Place?

It depends on the place and proposed activity. Work through school and appropriate local community and land-manager guidance; no person speaks for every Country.

What students learn in AC9S5I02

Students write detailed methods another group can repeat, identify variables and controls, select measurement tools, plan repetitions and risk controls and justify any digital data collection.

Repeatability requires an explicit sequence, quantities, units, controls and recording method—not merely a general description.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: plan and conduct repeatable investigations to answer questions, including, as appropriate, deciding the variables to be changed, measured and controlled in fair tests; describing potential risks; planning for the safe use of equipment and materials; and identifying required permissions to conduct investigations on Country/Place.

  • E1: considering different ways to approach investigations, such as researching, using trial and error, experimental testing, field observations, using digital tools to record observations or development of virtual simulations
  • E2: planning and recording the method to be used in an investigation so that it could be repeated by someone else
  • E3: making decisions on the variables to be changed, measured and controlled in fair tests, such as measuring the length and size of a shadow formed by different light sources
  • E4: using a map or aerial photographs to predict local sites likely to be affected by erosion, and collaboratively planning a field excursion to collect observations
  • E5: explaining rules for safe processes and use of equipment and materials, and potential risks to themselves or others when conducting an investigation
  • E6: consulting with First Nations Australians to identify local areas that require permission before accessing
  • E7: consulting with First Nations Australians to guide the planning of scientific investigations, considering potential risks for field investigations

Common misconceptions
  • Repeatable means same result guaranteed — It means the method can be followed consistently.
  • Equipment list replaces method — Specify how tools are used.
  • Controls omitted because obvious — Record quantities and conditions explicitly.
  • Digital sensor assumed self-correcting — Check placement, calibration, units and export settings.
