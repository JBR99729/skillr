YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5H02

Use science to compare practical responses

Learning intention: Identify a problem, use relevant scientific evidence, compare responses and explain a decision for an individual or community.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Choose a response to muddy runoff

Illustrated dry-land setting; not a species-identification image. Use the labelled text for the named species examples.

A hypothetical school finds sediment washing from a bare garden slope onto a path after rain. Scientific knowledge suggests that water flow can detach and transport soil. Covering exposed soil and slowing runoff may reduce sediment movement. A decision also needs evidence about safety, access, cost and maintenance; no one response is automatically best everywhere.

Suitable ground coverCan protect soil and slow surface flowWill it suit the site and be maintained?

Learn the skill

1. Define a problem using evidence

Describe what happened, where and under what conditions. Separate an observation from a guess about its cause. Science helps identify a mechanism that can be checked.

Observation and explanation

Observation: sediment appears on the path after rain. Possible explanation: runoff carries material from the bare slope. Check where the water and sediment come from.

Try it · Does a photograph of a muddy path prove which slope supplied the sediment?

No. It records the condition at that time. You need evidence about the flow path and sediment source.

2. Compare responses against criteria

Use scientific knowledge to predict how a response may work, then compare relevant practical criteria. A solution that slows erosion but creates a trip hazard needs redesign.

CriterionEvidence to seek
Sediment reductionComparable before/after or model records
Safe accessAdult site and route check
MaintenanceWho will maintain the response and how?
Try it · Is the most expensive option automatically the most scientific choice?

No. Compare how the mechanism works and evidence against the agreed criteria, including cost and safety.

3. Make a bounded decision and monitor it

State the response, evidence and reasons. Identify uncertainties and a way to check whether it helps. A classroom model can inform a decision without proving every outcome at the real site.

Decision frame

Choose ___ because the evidence suggests ___. It must also meet ___. We will check ___ and revise if ___.

Try it · Can a successful tray model guarantee a real slope will never erode?

No. Real rain, soil, roots, slopes and scale differ. Use the model as one source of evidence and monitor the actual approved response.

Common mistakes

“Scientific knowledge makes every decision for us.” Science informs likely effects; people also consider needs, practical limits and values.

“One successful test proves the solution always works.” State the tested conditions and monitor the chosen response.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write an evidence-based recommendation

2 minutes: identify the runoff problem. 4 minutes: compare two responses using mechanism and two criteria. 2 minutes: write a recommendation. 2 minutes: state one uncertainty and one monitoring action.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the decision frame. Extend: explain a trade-off between two otherwise useful responses.

Use the hypothetical case. Do not change drainage, enter runoff water, disturb soil or install structures without adult site approval.

Three tricky questions

1. Can two communities reasonably choose different responses to similar problems?

Yes. Conditions, resources, priorities and evidence may differ. Each decision should explain its reasons and likely effects.

2. Why should a response be checked after it is chosen?

Monitoring can show whether it helps under real conditions and reveal unintended effects or a need for maintenance.

3. Is a personal preference enough to claim that a method reduces erosion?

No. A scientific effect needs relevant evidence. Preferences may affect the final choice but do not establish the effect.

FAQ

Can an individual use scientific knowledge?

Yes. An individual can use evidence to choose an action, just as a community can compare responses to a shared problem.

Does a model count as evidence?

It can provide evidence about the model under its conditions. Explain its relevance and limits before applying it to a real setting.

What students learn in AC9S5H02

Students connect scientific explanations to possible solutions, compare benefits, risks and uncertainties and recognise that decisions also involve values, cost, equity, ethics and environmental consequences.

Science informs what may happen and how a solution works; decisions also require transparent criteria and consideration of who or what is affected.

Scientific evidence does not automatically select one option. State uncertainties and value judgements rather than hiding them as scientific facts.

A solution can shift a problem elsewhere or create long-term effects. Monitoring and revision are part of responsible decision-making.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: investigate how scientific knowledge is used by individuals and communities to identify problems, consider responses and make decisions.

  • E1: considering how decisions are made to farm particular crops or animals depending on local habitats, such as considering their ability to withstand drought or cold weather
  • E2: examining how communities use knowledge of erosion processes to design landscape features that reduce erosion in fragile environments
  • E3: examining how knowledge of erosion is used by park rangers to design rules such as keeping to the path and not climbing sandstone, and built features such as channel drains on paths, railings and barriers to protect the park environment and First Nations Australians’ heritage sites
  • E4: researching the impacts of light pollution and exploring how communities have used scientific knowledge to reduce light pollution, such as through the use of covered bulbs facing downwards in streetlights, automated systems to turn off streetlights and motion sensors on outdoor lights at home and in public places
  • E5: investigating how and why people used properties of light to design signal lamps to communicate via Morse code and where they continue to be used

Common misconceptions
  • Science alone determines values — Evidence informs but does not replace ethical choices.
  • Good intention guarantees good outcome — Monitor unintended effects.
  • Risk means solution must be rejected — Compare likelihood, consequence and mitigation.
  • Stakeholders limited to decision-makers — Include affected people, future users and environments.
