Follow light from its source

Learning intention: Identify emitters, trace straight paths, explain shadows and distinguish reflected from refracted light.

Learn the skill

1. Distinguish sources from reflectors

A light source produces and emits light. A lit torch and the Sun are sources. A book and the Moon can be visible because they reflect light from a source.

Trace the direction

Torch → book → eye. Each arrow follows the light. The eye receives light; it does not send a seeing ray out.

Try it · Is the bright Moon producing the visible light we usually see from it?

No. It reflects sunlight. Bright appearance alone does not make an object an emitter.

2. Explain a shadow using blocked paths

In a simple one-source setup, an opaque object blocks direct light. The shadow is on the side away from the source. Its outline and size depend on the positions, orientation and shape, not the object’s surface details.

A controlled comparison

Keep the same card and card-to-screen distance. Move only the torch and compare shadow heights. State which distance changes before predicting a size change.

Try it · Would red and blue opaque cards of the same shape necessarily make differently coloured shadows?

No. If both block the light similarly, their colours do not determine shadow colour. A shadow is reduced illumination, not a coloured copy of the object.

3. Contrast reflection and refraction

Reflection redirects light back from a surface. Refraction occurs as light crosses between materials where its speed changes; an angled path can bend. It continues straight within a uniform material.

Boundary check

A ray crossing straight along the normal into water changes speed but not direction. “Every entry into water bends” is too broad.

Try it · Does light reflected by a mirror have to pass through the mirror first?

No. The reflected path turns back from the reflecting surface. A refracted transmitted path crosses into another material.

Common mistakes

“Our eyes send light out to see.” Trace light from a source, to an object if needed, and then into the eye.

“A shadow is the object’s reflection.” A shadow is a region receiving less light because paths are blocked. Reflection sends light away from a surface.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain a safe shadow comparison

2 minutes: sketch torch → card → screen. 4 minutes: use an adult-approved battery torch to compare two torch positions while the card and screen stay fixed, or predict with a paper model. 2 minutes: label blocked and unblocked paths. 2 minutes: explain one limit of your model.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the supplied path words. Extend: explain why changing both the torch position and card height would make the comparison difficult to interpret.

Never point light at eyes. Do not look at the Sun or use lasers, candles or mains-powered equipment. Keep stands stable and walkways clear.

Three tricky questions

1. Can an opaque object still reflect light?

Yes. Opaque means it does not transmit the light through it. Many opaque surfaces reflect some incident light, allowing us to see them.

2. Will a shadow always be exactly the same size as its object?

No. Source size and the relative positions and orientation of source, object and screen affect the shadow.

3. A ray enters water straight along the normal. Must its direction change?

No. Its speed changes but its direction need not. At an angled boundary a transmitted ray can bend.

FAQ

Why does a straw in water appear bent?

Light from the submerged part changes direction as it crosses material boundaries on its way to the eye, changing its apparent position.

Do rays show every detail of light?

No. Straight arrows are a useful model for paths and direction. They do not show every property of light.

What students learn in AC9S5U03

Students trace rays from a source, explain reflection from surfaces, refraction when light changes medium, absorption by materials and shadow formation when opaque objects block light.

Light travels in straight lines within a uniform medium and changes direction or energy distribution when it interacts with materials.

A shadow forms where direct light cannot reach. It is not a material produced by the object.

Material labels such as transparent, translucent and opaque describe degrees of transmission in a context; real materials may transmit some wavelengths differently.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: identify sources of light, recognise that light travels in a straight path and describe how shadows are formed and light can be reflected and refracted.

  • E1: distinguishing between natural (such as glow worms, the sun and stars) and artificial (such as light bulbs or candles) sources of light
  • E2: investigating the shadows that are formed when light is completely or partially blocked by an object, such as when using a sundial or shadow puppets
  • E3: drawing ray diagrams to show how the path of light from a source reflects off surfaces into the eye
  • E4: observing refraction of light using prisms or water droplets and examining the rainbow effect produced
  • E5: exploring how 'holograph' videos use the refractive properties of light to create an image that appears to be 3-dimensional
  • E6: exploring the use of reflection of light by mirrors such as in periscopes and mirror mazes
  • E7: recognising First Nations Australians’ understanding of refraction as experienced in spearfishing and in shimmering body paint, and reflection as evidenced by materials selected for construction of housing

Common misconceptions
  • Eyes send out light to see — Light from a source reaches objects and then the eye.
  • Shadow is reflected light — It is a region receiving less direct light.
  • Refraction is reflection — Refraction occurs as light passes between media.
  • Black objects contain darkness — They absorb more visible light under the conditions.
