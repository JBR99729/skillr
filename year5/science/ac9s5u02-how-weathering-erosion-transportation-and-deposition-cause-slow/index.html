Trace how Earth’s surface changes

Learning intention: Distinguish breakdown, removal, movement and settling, and explain how these processes can produce slow or rapid surface change.

Follow a fragment from rock to river bank

A root widens a crack and rock fragments loosen: weathering has broken material down in place. Rainwater removes loose pieces: erosion. Flow carries them downstream: transportation. Some sediment settles where the flow can no longer carry it: deposition. These processes can overlap; the sequence is a useful explanation, not a rule that every fragment must follow.

Learn the skill

1. Identify weathering in place

Weathering breaks down or changes rock at Earth’s surface. Growing roots, repeated freezing of water in cracks, and reactions between some rocks and acidic water can contribute. Conditions and rock type matter.

Freeze–thaw reasoning

Water enters a crack. If it freezes, expansion can widen the crack. Repeated suitable freezing and thawing can help loosen fragments.

Try it · If a rock cracks but the pieces stay there, must erosion have occurred?

No. Breakdown in place is weathering. Erosion involves removal; it may happen later or alongside the breakdown.

2. Separate removal from transport

Erosion removes material. Transportation carries it elsewhere by moving water, wind or ice. Name the material and the moving agent instead of calling all changes weathering.

One event, two descriptions

A flow lifts sand from a bank and carries it downstream. Lifting it away is erosion; the downstream journey is transportation.

Try it · Is sediment a process like erosion?

No. Sediment is material such as sand, mud or rock fragments. Erosion, transportation and deposition describe processes affecting it.

3. Explain settling and time scale

Deposition occurs when transported material is left behind. Small changes can accumulate over years; a powerful flow can move and deposit material quickly. A process name alone does not determine its speed.

Evidence suppliedSupported description
A bank loses a little soil after many showers over yearsSlow cumulative surface change
A flood shifts a sand bar during one afternoonRapid change during that event
Try it · Must all erosion be rapid and all weathering be slow?

No. Rates depend on conditions and materials. Use evidence about duration and amount of change; do not assign one speed to each process.

Common mistakes

“Weathering and erosion mean exactly the same thing.” Distinguish breakdown in place from removal, while recognising they can occur together.

“All landscape change is destruction.” Removal in one place can supply deposition elsewhere. Describe the change and its effects rather than assuming every change is harmful.

Adjust the challenge Support: match process cards to the table first. Extend: describe a location where erosion and deposition occur at different times.

Use a paper model. Do not visit floodwater, unstable banks or rock faces, or perform freezing or acid experiments without a teacher-approved plan.

Three tricky questions

1. A river leaves a pile of sand behind. Is the pile evidence only of erosion?

No. The pile is evidence of deposition. The sand may have been eroded and transported earlier, but the supplied observation is settling.

2. Can the same stretch of river lose sediment and gain sediment?

Yes. Flow conditions and positions change. Some material can be removed while other material settles, or the processes can occur at different times.

3. Does a two-minute tray model establish the natural rate of river erosion?

No. It can model a process, but the tray materials, flow, size and time scale differ from a river.

FAQ

Does weathering always break rock into visible pieces?

No. Chemical weathering can change rock substances, and some changes are too small to see immediately.

Why record before and after?

The comparison shows what changed and where material went. Add time and condition information to support explanations of rate.

What students learn in AC9S5U02

Students distinguish breakdown from movement and settling, model agents such as water, wind and ice and explain how process rate depends on material, slope, vegetation, climate and time.

Weathering breaks material down; erosion removes it, transportation moves it and deposition occurs when energy decreases and sediment settles.

The processes interact but are not synonyms. One landscape may experience weathering, erosion and deposition in different locations at the same time.

Models should preserve direction and cause but may accelerate time and simplify rock type, rainfall variability and scale.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: describe how weathering, erosion, transportation and deposition cause slow or rapid change to Earth’s surface.

  • E1: identifying types of weathering caused by mechanical means such as by wind abrasion, cycles of extreme heat or cold, and frost wedging; and biological means such as by plants and tree roots
  • E2: exploring how erosion can be caused by moving air or moving water and how substances such as surface soil are relocated, and identifying examples of erosion on a local or regional scale
  • E3: analysing the difference between weathering and erosion and comparing the timescales over which these processes can occur
  • E4: modelling the effects of erosion on a simulated landscape and exploring factors that mitigate its effects
  • E5: investigating how humans have changed local landscapes and predicting the effect these changes might have on rates of erosion
  • E6: considering how First Nations Australians are impacted by the rapid erosion of sand dunes and the resulting effect of saltwater on culturally significant freshwater swamps
  • E7: considering the effects of significant rainfall, such as a monsoon, on the transportation and deposition of river sediments in the Asia-Pacific region

Common misconceptions
  • Weathering and erosion are the same — Weathering is breakdown in place; erosion removes material.
  • Deposition destroys land only — It builds features such as deltas, beaches and bars.
  • Change occurs only during disasters — Slow repeated processes also reshape landscapes.
  • Model time equals real time — Classroom models accelerate and simplify processes.
