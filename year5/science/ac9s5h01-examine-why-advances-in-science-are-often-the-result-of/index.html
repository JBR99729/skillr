YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5H01

Explain how scientific work builds on others

Learning intention: Use a real historical example to connect different contributions, earlier findings and shared evidence to an advance in science.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Penicillin needed connected contributions

  1. 1928: a useful observation

    Fleming notices an antibacterial effect associated with a mould.

  2. Late 1930s onward: linked investigation

    Oxford researchers connect chemical preparation, apparatus and sensitive biological testing.

  3. Build and check

    Later methods address questions that the first observation did not answer.

In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed a mould associated with an area where bacteria did not grow. Later, an Oxford team investigated preparation and biological effects. Ernst Chain and colleagues contributed chemical work; Norman Heatley developed sensitive tests and useful apparatus; Howard Florey coordinated biological investigations. These are selected connected contributions, not a complete list or an equal division of all work.

Learn the skill

1. Link complementary expertise

Collaboration is more than several people being present. Explain how one contribution makes another task possible, or answers a different necessary question.

Preparation and testing

A preparation method supplies material. A sensitive activity test checks whether the prepared material still has the useful effect. Both kinds of work are needed.

Try it · Would naming four scientists explain why collaboration mattered?

No. Link their different contributions: for example, preparation supplies material and a test gives evidence about its activity.

2. Explain what later work added

Building on earlier work can mean following up a finding or improving a method. A promising observation does not automatically answer preparation, effectiveness and safety questions.

An advance needs a link

Earlier observation → new question → additional preparation and testing. The arrows show a connection between work, not a guarantee of success.

Try it · Did Fleming’s first observation by itself establish a ready-to-use medicine?

No. More work on preparation and appropriate testing was needed. The observation was a valuable lead rather than every later stage.

3. Use evidence when giving credit

Check what people actually contributed. Awards and headlines may recognise selected people while leaving out other contributions. Different emphasis in two accounts does not by itself prove a contradiction.

Check the claim

“One person did every part” needs evidence about every stage. The supplied role record supports several linked contributions instead.

Try it · Does winning an award prove a scientist worked alone?

No. Recognition does not establish sole contribution. Use records of methods, evidence and roles to evaluate the claim.

Common mistakes

“Collaboration means everyone does the same job.” Different expertise and methods can complement one another.

“A later discovery makes earlier work unimportant.” Later work can depend on an earlier observation, instrument or method while also adding something new.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Map contribution to advance

2 minutes: read the supplied role table. 4 minutes: make three contribution cards and connect them with “helps because” arrows. 2 minutes: explain what later work added to the early observation. 2 minutes: repair an inaccurate one-person headline.

Adjust the challenge Support: use preparation → test → evidence as a frame. Extend: distinguish recognition in an award from a complete historical contribution record.

This is a history and reasoning activity. Do not grow mould, handle cultures or make or test medicines.

Three tricky questions

1. Two accounts highlight different contributors. Must one be false?

No. They may have different purposes and select different parts of a larger story. Check their specific claims against evidence.

2. How could sharing a reliable test help another team?

Another team could use the method to compare preparations or check a claim, provided the procedure and conditions are clear.

3. Is teamwork alone evidence that a scientific claim is correct?

No. Collaboration can improve methods and checking, but claims still require suitable evidence and critical examination.

FAQ

What is an assay?

It is a test used to measure or compare a particular activity. Here it means checking the biological activity of a preparation.

Were only the people named here involved?

No. This short example selects some contributions to explain collaboration; it is not a complete history.

What students learn in AC9S5H01

Students describe science as a collaborative evidence-based process in which explanations are proposed, tested, communicated, critiqued and revised rather than produced once by a lone authority.

Scientific knowledge becomes more reliable through transparent evidence, repeatable methods, critical review and willingness to revise explanations.

Revision is not proof that science failed; it is how explanations become better aligned with available evidence.

Credit sources and distinguish consensus based on evidence from popularity or authority alone.

Learning routine: Observe → Model → Investigate → Analyse → Explain → Evaluate

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: examine why advances in science are often the result of collaboration or build on the work of others.

  • E1: researching how the recent discovery of a biofluorescent flying squirrel led to discoveries of more fluorescent mammals, such as wombats, bilbies, echidna and bandicoots as scientists collaborated with other scientists across fields of science and internationally
  • E2: researching why European naturalists and scientists first thought the platypus was a faked animal, and how scientists such as those in the Platypus Conservation Initiative are collaborating in ongoing research to understand the features and behaviours of platypuses
  • E3: investigating how contemporary soil erosion management practices adapt and build on First Nations Australians’ fire management and agricultural practices
  • E4: exploring why developing new erosion mitigation techniques such as contour banks and strip cropping requires geologists, hydrologists and farmers to collaborate
  • E5: exploring how understanding of light and optics has developed by comparing the ideas of Plato, Euclid, Ptolemy, Ibn al-Haytham and Roger Bacon

Common misconceptions
  • One scientist creates final truth — Knowledge develops through many contributions.
  • New evidence makes old work worthless — Earlier models may remain useful within limits.
  • Agreement proves correctness — Consensus strength depends on evidence and methods.
  • Critique means personal attack — Scientific critique addresses claims, evidence and reasoning.
