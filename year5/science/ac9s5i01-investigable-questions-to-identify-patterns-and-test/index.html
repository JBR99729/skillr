YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5I01

Turn observations into testable questions

Learning intention: Use observations to ask a question about a pattern or relationship, then predict an outcome with a scientific reason.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

From a shadow pattern to a new test

The same upright card stays 10 cm from the screen. Only the torch-to-card distance changes. The supplied numbers are invented model records, not measurements from the illustration or an actual class. Across these model runs, shadow heights are smaller when the torch is farther from the card. That pattern can guide a new investigable question, but a prediction for an untested distance remains a prediction.

Learn the skill

1. Separate observation from prediction

An observation describes what was recorded. A prediction describes an outcome expected before a new test. Keep preference and explanation distinct from both.

What is supplied?

At 10 cm the model shadow heights are 20, 21 and 19 cm. At 30 cm they are 13, 14 and 13 cm. These are records in the supplied invented dataset.

Try it · Is “I think the next shadow will be shorter” already an observation?

No. It is a prediction about a future or untested result. The observation is what was actually recorded in the supplied data.

2. Specify a relationship that can be investigated

Name what changes, what you measure and relevant conditions. “What happens to shadow height when torch-to-card distance changes, with the same card and card-to-screen distance?” can guide a comparison.

Make the question useful

Changed: torch-to-card distance. Measured: shadow height in centimetres. Controlled: card, screen distance, torch, alignment and room lighting.

Try it · Does “Which shadow is nicest?” test the same scientific relationship?

No. It asks about preference and does not define a measurable relationship between torch distance and shadow height.

3. Give a reasoned, bounded prediction

Use a relevant pattern or model to justify an expected outcome. Do not claim a new result is already known, or invent an exact value without enough support.

Prediction for a new position

With the same setup, I predict the shadow at 25 cm will be shorter than at 10 cm, because the supplied distance pattern and spreading light-path model support that direction.

Try it · Must a reasoned prediction always be correct?

No. It should have a relevant reason and be testable. New evidence may require you to revise the explanation.

Common mistakes

“A prediction is a measured fact.” State whether a value is recorded or expected before testing.

“A question must begin with why to be scientific.” Many useful investigations ask what happens when a named variable changes.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a question–prediction pair

2 minutes: read the model table. 3 minutes: write one observable pattern. 3 minutes: pose a question using one changed variable and one measured outcome. 2 minutes: make a prediction with a reason and a stated condition.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “What happens to ___ when ___?” Extend: name evidence that would challenge your prediction.

This activity can use supplied data only. For a practical, use a stable battery torch under adult guidance and never point it at eyes.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a prediction be useful without an exact number?

Yes. A justified direction or comparison can be useful when the evidence does not support an exact value.

2. Would changing card height as well as torch distance answer the proposed fair-test question?

It would make the effect of torch distance difficult to isolate. Keep relevant conditions controlled.

3. Can one pattern prove what will happen at every possible distance?

No. State the tested range and setup. Other distances or arrangements may need investigation.

FAQ

Do investigations always change a variable deliberately?

No. Some investigate patterns through observations. Match the method to the question.

Where does a scientific reason come from?

Use relevant prior observations, evidence or a scientific model, and show the connection to the prediction.

What students learn in AC9S5I01

Students move from observations to focused questions naming variables, select measurable outcomes and write predictions that state direction and scientific reasoning.

A strong investigation question identifies a relationship that can be explored through controlled observation or measurement.

The prediction should be testable and based on evidence or a scientific explanation, not a preferred result.

Define criteria for words such as best or strong and isolate one relationship per investigation.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: pose investigable questions to identify patterns and test relationships and make reasoned predictions.

  • E1: posing questions that can be investigated scientifically, such as: ‘Do all animals which live in desert habitats have ways to survive without water?’
  • E2: acknowledging and using information from First Nations Australians to guide the formulation of investigable questions about structural features and behaviours of living things
  • E3: posing investigable questions about landscape features and how they were changed by weathering, erosion, transportation or deposition
  • E4: asking questions and making predictions to test relationships, such as: ‘Will there be more erosion of steeper slopes? Will this organisation of mirrors enable me to see around corners? Are animals that camouflage well more likely to survive predation?’
  • E5: making reasoned predictions about the habitat a plant or animal lives in or the observable effect of light interacting with an object

Common misconceptions
  • Question asks opinion — Use measurable evidence.
  • Prediction gives no direction — State what is expected to increase, decrease or differ.
  • Several variables change together — Focus the relationship.
  • Prediction rewritten after results — Record it before testing and evaluate later.
