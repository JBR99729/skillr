YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5U04

Explain states of matter with particles

Learning intention: Use particle arrangement and motion to explain the observable shape, volume, flow and compressibility of solids, liquids and gases.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Same-sized model particles, different arrangements

Blue dots represent the same substance. Brown marks symbolise motion. Models are not to scale; the box is a viewing region.

The dots represent particles of one substance at different states. In a solid they stay close and vibrate about positions; in a liquid they remain close but move past one another; in a gas they are widely spaced and move throughout the available space. The dots and arrows are a simplified model, not a photograph or a scale drawing.

Learn the skill

1. Use several observable properties

Compare shape, volume and behaviour under a gentle safe test. Being hard is not the definition of solid, and being pourable is not enough to establish liquid.

Rice is a useful counterexample

A pile of rice changes outline when poured. Each grain remains a solid with its own shape; a moving collection is not the same as a liquid.

Try it · Does a soft rubber eraser have to be liquid?

No. A solid can be soft or flexible. Use several properties rather than hardness alone.

2. Connect arrangement and motion to properties

Solid particles are not motionless. They vibrate about positions. Liquid particles remain close while moving past one another, so the liquid flows without needing the large gaps of a gas.

Keep the model consistent

Use the same dot size for the same substance. Show movement with a key: vibration about a position, close rearrangement, or widely spaced movement.

Try it · Should liquid particles be drawn far apart to explain pouring?

No. They remain close. Their ability to move past one another explains the changing shape.

3. Explain compression and critique the model

A gas can occupy a smaller volume mainly because gaps between its particles decrease. A liquid has much less empty space between particles and is difficult to compress in an ordinary classroom comparison.

What changes?

For a closed gas sample under the stated comparison, keep the same particle count and dot size. Draw smaller gaps in a smaller container region.

Try it · Does compressing a gas mean each particle becomes smaller?

No. In this model the particles retain their size; the spaces between them decrease.

Common mistakes

“Solid particles never move.” They vibrate about positions; fixed overall shape does not mean no particle motion.

“Heating makes individual gas particles swell.” The simple model changes motion and spacing, not particle size. The boundary conditions affect the sample’s response.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Draw, explain and repair a particle model

2 minutes: compare the three supplied models. 4 minutes: draw solid and liquid arrangements using equal-size dots. 2 minutes: add a movement key. 2 minutes: sketch a gas before and after compression, keeping particle number and size the same.

Adjust the challenge Support: start with supplied dot positions. Extend: explain why a sponge can contain a solid framework and gas-filled spaces.

Use drawings or large counters. Do not heat sealed containers, inhale materials or perform pressure experiments without a teacher-approved setup.

Three tricky questions

1. Why can both a solid and a liquid be hard to compress?

Their particles are already close, leaving little space to reduce in an ordinary comparison. A liquid can still flow because its particles can rearrange.

2. An empty-looking cup is lowered upside down into water. What may stop water entering fully?

Trapped air occupies space. Tilting can let bubbles escape and allow water to enter. The exact outcome depends on the setup and leaks.

3. Does drawing particles blue prove that the substance is blue at particle scale?

No. Dot colour is a model convention. State what the dots represent and do not treat every drawing feature as a real particle property.

FAQ

Can one object contain more than one state?

Yes. A sponge can contain a solid framework and gas spaces; a fizzy drink can contain liquid and visible gas bubbles.

Are the particle drawings to scale?

No. They deliberately enlarge particles and simplify arrangement and motion to explain observable properties.

What students learn in AC9S5U04

Students compare shape, volume, flow, compression and diffusion, then use a particle model to explain why solids, liquids and gases behave differently.

Matter is modelled as particles; state differences arise from particle spacing, arrangement and movement, not from particles changing size.

The model explains fixed shape, flow and compressibility. Particles are not drawn to scale and the spaces are model features, not air between particles.

Use the model consistently and state its limitations. A gas still has mass and occupies space even when invisible.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: explain observable properties of solids, liquids and gases by modelling the motion and arrangement of particles.

  • E1: classifying substances as solids, liquids and gases and investigating their properties
  • E2: exploring examples that demonstrate that gases have mass, such as blowing air through straws to move objects or using a balance to compare an empty balloon to one filled with air
  • E3: using role-play to model the arrangement and motion of particles in solids, liquids and gases
  • E4: observing a virtual demonstration of coloured gases being compressed and providing an explanation for the change in colour intensity
  • E5: exploring, through guided discussion, ideas about what is between particles
  • E6: recognising First Nations Australians’ knowledges and understandings of solids, liquids and gases and how these knowledges are applied in a range of processes and practices, including the extraction of oils, medical therapies and cooking

Common misconceptions
  • Particles in a solid do not move — They vibrate about fixed positions.
  • Gas has no mass — It occupies space and has mass.
  • Particles expand when heated — Heating can increase particle motion; spacing may change depending on the container and state. Particle size remains essentially unchanged in this model.
  • Liquid particles are far apart — They remain close but can rearrange.
