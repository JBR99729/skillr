YEAR 5 SCIENCE · AC9S5I05

Use evidence to judge a conclusion

Learning intention: Compare findings and methods, identify possible errors, select relevant evidence and propose a useful next investigation.

Learn the skill

1. Compare the findings fairly

Compare records for the same conditions, not just one favourite number. Describe agreements, differences and variation without assuming any difference proves a mistake.

Pattern across repeats

In Group A’s supplied records, every 10 cm torch-distance shadow is taller than every 30 cm torch-distance shadow. The runs also vary within each setting.

Try it · Do slightly different repeat readings automatically mean one is wrong?

No. Measurements can vary. Record them and examine the setup, reading method and size of the differences.

2. Check errors and method differences

Compare equipment, reference points, controls and recording. An accidentally shifted screen can affect the result; a deliberately different screen distance answers a different comparison and is not automatically an error.

Method issueWhy it matters
Screen shifts during runsMore than the intended variable changes
Different top-edge reading rulesHeights may not be measured consistently
A value is copied into the wrong rowThe data representation changes its meaning
Try it · Does doing more runs fix a screen that is wrongly positioned in every run?

No. A systematic setup issue can affect every repeat. Check and repair the method rather than relying on repetition alone.

3. Draw a reasoned conclusion and ask what next

Select evidence that answers the question and link it to a relevant explanation. State the tested conditions and a remaining uncertainty. A follow-up should change or observe something specific.

A supported conclusion

For Group A’s fixed setup, greater torch-to-card distance from 10 to 30 cm was associated with shorter shadows across the supplied runs. A follow-up could test card-to-screen distance while holding the torch-to-card distance fixed.

Try it · Can these records prove that every torch arrangement gives the same heights?

No. The evidence applies to the stated apparatus, distances and reading method. Other arrangements require appropriate evidence.

Common mistakes

“Different findings prove another group cheated.” First compare methods, conditions and records; variation and method differences can have valid explanations.

“A conclusion is just my prediction repeated.” Use relevant collected or supplied evidence, even if it challenges the prediction.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Hold an evidence conference

2 minutes: state Group A’s repeated pattern. 3 minutes: compare the two screen-distance methods. 3 minutes: write a conclusion supported by specific records. 2 minutes: name a possible error and a useful follow-up.

Adjust the challenge Support: use evidence → reason → limit. Extend: distinguish a deliberate method difference from an accidental procedural error.

Use the hypothetical records and stated methods. Do not invent missing Group B readings or remove inconvenient Group A results.

Three tricky questions

1. Should an unusual result always be deleted?

No. Keep an honest record, check for a supported reason and explain any decision about using it. Unusual does not automatically mean invalid.

2. Would “investigate more” be a sufficiently precise follow-up?

It is too vague. Name a variable, outcome or uncertainty and a suitable way to investigate it.

3. Why compare both methods and findings?

A difference in results can arise from a difference in procedure or conditions. Results alone may not explain the cause.

FAQ

What is a possible source of error?

An equipment, method, reading or recording issue that could make evidence less reliable. Identify its likely effect rather than merely saying “human error”.

Must a conclusion include every number?

No. Select enough relevant evidence to support the claim while preserving the complete records for checking.

What students learn in AC9S5I05

Students compare methods and data sets, identify random and systematic error possibilities, judge whether differences are meaningful and write conclusions supported by evidence and bounded by limitations.

Differences in findings can arise from natural variation, measurement uncertainty, uncontrolled variables or method errors and require investigation rather than automatic rejection.

Group C’s different lamp distance is a systematic method difference that can explain its larger temperature rise.

Error does not mean dishonesty. It describes uncertainty or bias introduced by instrument, method, environment or recording.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

Content description: compare methods and findings with those of others, recognise possible sources of error, pose questions for further investigation and select evidence to draw reasoned conclusions.

  • E1: comparing methods and findings with those of others to determine if the investigation was a fair test
  • E2: recognising errors that could have occurred during investigations including changing too many variables, incorrect or misreading of measurements, or changes in environmental factors
  • E3: comparing, in small groups, proposed reasons for findings and explaining their reasoning and posing further questions
  • E4: discussing the difference between data and evidence and examining how evidence is selected
  • E5: reflecting on inferences made from observations and analysis of the data to draw a reasoned conclusion

Common misconceptions
  • Different result equals mistake — Variation may be expected.
  • Outlier removed without reason — Investigate and document decision.
  • Systematic error fixed by averaging — A biased method remains biased.
  • Conclusion stronger than evidence — Use cautious scope and conditions.
