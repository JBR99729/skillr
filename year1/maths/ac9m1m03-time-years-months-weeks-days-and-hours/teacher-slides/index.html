These curriculum examples extend the central model into varied contexts. They are learning contexts, not extra definitions to memorise.

E1

naming, listing and using familiar units of time, such as hours, days, weeks, years

Learning connection: naming, listing and using familiar units of time, such as hours, days, weeks, years. Connect the example back to this key idea: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take.

comparing the number of days in the months of the year and explaining how the months cycle from one year to the next

Connect the example back to this key idea: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take.

E3

sequencing familiar events including the representation of time with pictorial timelines

Learning connection: sequencing familiar events including the representation of time with pictorial timelines. Connect the example back to this key idea: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take.

E4

discussing events and activities and deciding whether they would take closer to an hour, a day, a week, a month or a year; for example, it takes a day for the sun to rise and fall and rise again, but it takes less than an hour for me to walk to school

Learning connection: DISCUSSING events and activities and deciding whether they would take closer to an hour, a day, a week, a month or a year. Connect the example back to this key idea: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take.

E5

investigating durations of time represented in First Nations Australians’ seasonal calendars

Learning connection: INVESTIGATING durations of time represented in First Nations Australians’ seasonal calendars. Connect the example back to this key idea: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take.

Cultural safety: Use a community-approved source, name the specific Nation or community where the source does, and avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation.

Remember

  • Use calendar units

  • Sequence events
  • Describe duration

Quick questions

  • How many days are in a week?
  • Which is longer: a day or an hour?
  • Order breakfast, bedtime and lunch.
  • Which unit suits a birthday?

Return to the concept model if an answer is uncertain. Use the model to explain, not just to guess.

Assessment and reviewReady for practice
  • Using tiny units for long events — Use months or years for long events, not hours.
  • Mixing order words — Before and after describe sequence; duration describes how long.
  • Forgetting week length — One week has 7 days.
