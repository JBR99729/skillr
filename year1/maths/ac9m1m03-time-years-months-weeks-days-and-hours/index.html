Days, weeks, months and years

Learning goal: Time can be described using years, months, weeks, days and hours. Events can be ordered by when they happen and how long they take. Use this free guide to learn the idea, then practise on paper with the matching worksheet pack.

Learn the skill

1. Choose a useful time unit

Hours suit part of a day; days suit a short holiday; weeks, months and years suit longer periods. Always state the unit.

Try it · Which fits a child's age: hours or years?

Years. The unit should match the length of the event or period.

2. Follow the days of a week

A week has seven days. After Sunday comes Monday again; the cycle continues.

Try it · Today is Tuesday. What day is two days later?

Thursday. Wednesday is one day later; Thursday is two.

3. Use months and years

A year has twelve months. December is followed by January in the next year. Months do not all have the same number of days.

Try it · Do all months last 30 days?

No. Check a calendar; different months have different lengths.

Common mistakes

Assuming every month has four weeks. Count the dates on a calendar. Four weeks have 28 days, but many months have more than 28.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Make a calendar trail

Mark today on a calendar. Find the date one week later and circle the month that follows this one. Then choose a familiar annual event and find its month.

Adjust the challenge: Start by finding today and tomorrow. Extend by tracing a week that crosses into a new month.

FAQ

How many days are in a week?

Seven. The same order of weekdays repeats every week.

Small steps, clear examples

4. Put events in order

Before and after describe sequence. Put a familiar day on a timeline: breakfast, school, dinner, bedtime.

Try it: Place lunch, breakfast and bedtime in a usual day's order.

See answer and explanation

Breakfast, lunch, bedtime. Explain the sequence in the supplied day.

5. Compare how long events last

Duration is how long, rather than when. One day is shorter than one week; one week is shorter than one year.

Try it: Order an hour, a day and a week from shortest to longest.

See answer and explanation

An hour, a day, a week. Sequence and duration answer different questions.

Try a worked example together

Read the order of days

Work through the example

  1. Read the days in order. Tuesday follows Monday; Wednesday follows Tuesday.
  2. A week has seven days. The day after Sunday is Monday.
  3. Use a calendar to connect dates to their days. Months do not all have the same number of days.

Watch for this: A new week continues the order; it does not stop after Sunday.

Try it together

What day comes before Friday? What day comes after Sunday?

Answer and teaching guidance

Thursday comes before Friday. Monday comes after Sunday.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Using tiny units for long events — Use months or years for long events, not hours.
  • Mixing order words — Before and after describe sequence; duration describes how long.
  • Forgetting week length — One week has 7 days.
