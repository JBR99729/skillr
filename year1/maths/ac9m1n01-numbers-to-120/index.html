Learn the skill

Six small steps to numbers to 120

Start with the first focus, or open the one you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

1. Read and write numbers

A numeral is a number written with digits, such as 68. We can say it, write its name and show the same amount with objects.

68numeralsixty-eightnumber name6 tens + 8 onessame amount

Read it: 68 has six tens and eight ones, so say “sixty-eight”. For 105, say “one hundred and five”; the zero shows there are no extra tens.

Watch for this: 68 and 86 use the same digits in different places. They show different amounts.

Try it: Write the numeral for “seventy-four”. How many tens and ones does it have?

Check the answer

74: seven tens and four ones.

2. Show numbers with tens and ones

Put objects into groups of ten. Count the complete groups first, then count the loose objects.

  1. Count the four full groups: 10, 20, 30, 40.
  2. Count on for the seven loose counters: 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47.
  3. Say and write the total: forty-seven, 47.

Watch for this: A group counts as ten only when it contains ten objects.

Try it: Use counters or a drawing to show 32. How many full tens and loose ones do you need?

Check the answer

Three full groups of ten and two loose ones. 30 and 2 make 32.

3. Find one more and one less

One more is the next number when counting forwards. One less is the next number when counting backwards.

68one less69start here70one more

Start at 69. Counting forwards gives 70; counting backwards gives 68. After a number ending in 9, the next number starts a new ten.

Try it: Say one less and one more than 110. Then fill the gap: 108, 109, __, 111.

Check the answer

One less is 109; one more is 111. The missing number is 110. Each step changes the number by one.

4. Count past 99 to 100

The count keeps going after 99: 98, 99, 100, 101. Ten tens make one hundred.

Work through it: 99 has nine tens and nine ones. Add one more object to make ten full tens: 100. Add another one to make 101.

For 103, read one hundred and three: one hundred, no extra tens and three ones. It is three steps after 100 on the line.

Watch for this: Count the spaces between marks. The count does not restart at 1 after 99.

Try it: Count backwards: 102, 101, __, __. What is one more than 109?

Check the answer

102, 101, 100, 99. One more than 109 is 110: one hundred and ten.

5. Use a 120 number chart

This chart has ten numbers in each row. Find a known number and use its neighbours to find another.

  • Move right within a row: one more. 34 → 35.
  • Move left within a row: one less. 35 → 34.
  • Move down one row: ten more. 34 → 44.
  • Move up one row: ten less. 44 → 34.

At the end of a row, keep counting at the start of the next row: 39, 40, 41. Moving down from 40 gives 50, a different move.

Try it: Find 87. What is directly above it? What comes immediately after 99 when you keep counting?

Check the answer

77 is directly above 87: it is ten less. The next counting number after 99 is 100.

6. Compare and put numbers in order

Greater means a larger amount. Less means a smaller amount. Equal means the same amount.

Compare hundreds first, then tens, then ones. For 58 and 85, eight tens is more than five tens, so 85 is greater. The ones digit alone cannot tell you.

To order 89, 108 and 98 from smallest to greatest:

  1. 108 has a hundred. Put it after both numbers below 100.
  2. 89 has eight tens; 98 has nine tens. Put 89 first.
  3. The order is 89, 98, 108. On a number line, read this order from left to right.

Number signs: 89 < 98 means “89 is less than 98”; 108 > 98 means “108 is greater than 98”; 74 = 74 means the amounts are equal.

Try it: Order 42, 24 and 102 from smallest to greatest. Explain why 102 comes last.

Check the answer

24, 42, 102. Two tens is less than four tens. 102 has a hundred, so it is greater than both two-digit numbers.

An adult can read the directions aloud. Let the learner show an answer with objects, a drawing, pointing or a spoken explanation.
