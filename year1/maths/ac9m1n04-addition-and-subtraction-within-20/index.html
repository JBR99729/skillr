Addition and Subtraction within 20

Add and subtract within 20 using objects, number bonds and strategies such as making ten. To work out 8 + 5, move 2 from the 5 to make 10, then add the remaining 3: 13.

Learn the skill

1. Join and separate parts

Addition joins parts; subtraction can remove a part or find a missing part. Nine counters and four more make thirteen.

Try it · Show 13 − 4 with counters.

Remove four from thirteen; nine remain. 9 + 4 checks it.

2. Make ten

For 8 + 5, split 5 into 2 and 3. 8 + 2 makes 10; 10 + 3 makes 13.

Try it · Use ten to work out 9 + 4.

Split 4 into 1 and 3: 9 + 1 + 3 = 13.

3. Use a related fact to check

If 7 + 6 = 13, then 13 − 7 = 6. The same parts and whole connect addition and subtraction.

Try it · What related addition checks 15 − 8 = 7?

8 + 7 = 15. Both parts combine to make the whole.

Common mistakes

Changing the total when making ten. Move two counters from the group of five to the group of eight. Three stay behind, so 8 + 5 becomes 10 + 3.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Make ten with counters

Show 8 counters on a ten-frame and place 5 beside it. Move 2 into the frame to make 10, then count the 3 left beside it. Draw what you did.

Adjust the challenge: Start with 9 + 2. Extend by using the model to explain 13 − 5.

FAQ

How can making ten help with 8 + 5?

Split 5 into 2 and 3. Add 2 to 8 to make 10, then add 3 to make 13.

Small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

1. Join and separate parts

Addition joins parts; subtraction can remove a part or find a missing part. Nine counters and four more make thirteen.

Try it: Show 13 − 4 with counters.

See answer and explanation

Remove four from thirteen; nine remain. 9 + 4 checks it.

2. Count on or back

For 12 + 3, start at 12 and make three jumps: 13, 14, 15. Count jumps, not the starting point.

Try it: Count back four from 16.

See answer and explanation

15, 14, 13, 12. The answer is 12.

3. Make ten

For 8 + 5, split 5 into 2 and 3. 8 + 2 makes 10; 10 + 3 makes 13.

Try it: Use ten to work out 9 + 4.

See answer and explanation

Split 4 into 1 and 3: 9 + 1 + 3 = 13.

4. Use doubles and nearby facts

6 + 6 = 12. One extra gives 6 + 7 = 13. A known double helps with a nearby fact.

Try it: Use 5 + 5 to solve 5 + 6.

See answer and explanation

10 + 1 = 11. Six is one more than five.

5. Use a related fact to check

If 7 + 6 = 13, then 13 − 7 = 6. The same parts and whole connect addition and subtraction.

Try it: What related addition checks 15 − 8 = 7?

See answer and explanation

8 + 7 = 15. Both parts combine to make the whole.

Try a worked example together

Make ten and keep the remaining part

Work through the example

  1. Start with eight counters. There are two empty spaces in the first ten-frame.
  2. Move two of the five extra counters into those spaces. Three extra counters remain.
  3. Now there is one full ten and three more: 8 + 5 = 10 + 3 = 13.

Watch for this: The extra counters are split, not counted twice. Two and three still make five.

Try it together

Use the same idea to work out 9 + 5.

Answer and teaching guidance

Move one of the five to make ten. Four remain, so 10 + 4 = 14.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Counting all every time — Count on from the larger number when possible.
  • Mixing add and subtract — Read the story: are parts joining or is a part being removed/found?
  • Stopping at 10 — After making ten, remember to add the leftover part.
