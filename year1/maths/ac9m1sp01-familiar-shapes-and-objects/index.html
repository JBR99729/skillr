Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

1. Name features, not just appearances

A triangle is a closed shape with three straight sides. Its colour, size or turning does not change those features.

Try it: Is a turned triangle still a triangle?

See answer and explanation

Yes. It still has three straight sides and is closed.

2. Compare familiar shapes

A square has four equal sides and four square corners. A rectangle has four square corners; a square is a special rectangle.

Try it: Does turning a square make it a different shape?

See answer and explanation

No. Orientation changes how it looks, not its defining features.

3. Sort using a stated rule

Sort closed shapes by straight or curved edges, or by the number of sides. Keep the same rule and explain each placement.

Try it: Would colour decide a sort by number of sides?

See answer and explanation

No. Count sides; colour is irrelevant to that rule.

4. Connect flat shapes and solid objects

A box is a solid object; a rectangular face is a flat shape on it. A ball is like a sphere, rather than a flat circle.

Try it: Does a drawing of a box make the box itself flat?

See answer and explanation

No. The drawing shows a solid object; distinguish a face from the whole object.

5. Make and describe shapes

Use sticks to make a closed triangle or join two triangles to make another shape. Check sides, corners and how the parts meet.

Try it: Can three sticks with a gap make a closed triangle?

See answer and explanation

No. Join the endpoints to close the shape.

Try a worked example together

A shape keeps its properties when it turns

Work through the example

  1. Both triangles have three straight sides and three corners.
  2. The second triangle is turned. Its position changes, but its sides and corners do not.
  3. The circle has a curved edge and no corners. Describe what you can see before naming the shape.

Watch for this: Do not name a shape only by the way it points. A turned square is still a square.

Try it together

Turn a paper square. Is it still a square? Give a reason.

Answer and teaching guidance

Yes. It still has four equal sides and four square corners.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Sorting by colour only — Use mathematical features such as sides, corners, faces or curves.
  • Mixing 2D and 3D language — A square is flat; a cube is a solid object.
  • Naming without evidence — Say the feature that proves the name or group.
