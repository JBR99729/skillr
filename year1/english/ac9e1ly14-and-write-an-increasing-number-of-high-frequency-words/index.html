YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Read and write familiar words accurately AC9E1LY14

Learning intention: I can build a growing set of high-frequency words and use them in meaningful text.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Notice the usual and unusual parts

In said, s and d represent familiar sounds; ai represents the short e sound in this word. Read said, use it in “Mia said hello”, cover it, write it and check s-a-i-d. This teaches the complete spelling instead of guessing from the word’s outline. Practise a manageable set and revisit it over time.

Learn the skill

1. Learn the whole word

Say the target, match familiar sounds to letters and notice any unusual part. High-frequency means a word occurs often; it does not mean every word follows an easy spelling rule.

Have is h-a-v-e with a short a in usual pronunciation. One is the number word o-n-e. Pretty is p-r-e-t-t-y, with its first vowel sounding like short i.

Try it · Read have, one and pretty aloud before an adult supplies the words. Which letters need attention?

Observe actual reading. Check the final e in have, the complete unusual spelling of one and the e/tt/y in pretty. Support and reteach only the parts that need it; do not demand a timed response.

2. Read it in a complete message

Read through the written word and sentence. Context helps check meaning but should not replace attention to the letters.

“I found one shell.” One means a quantity here; won names winning in another sentence. They can sound alike while having different spellings.

Try it · Read “I have some paper.” Point to and read have and some again.

Hear accurate reading of both words. Their final e does not give the common long-vowel pattern here. If the adult pre-reads the targets, record supported practice rather than independent reading.

3. Write, uncover and check

After studying a word, cover it and write it. Compare every letter, correct a mismatch, then use it in a short sentence. Later, try without a recent visible model.

Look at they. Cover it, write it and check t-h-e-y. “They can play” uses it meaningfully.

Try it · Study said, cover it, write it and check. Use it in your own short sentence.

Look for s-a-i-d and a sensible use such as “Dad said hello.” Accept different complete sentences. Retrieval after study is supported rehearsal; later unseen checks show what has been retained.

Common mistakes

“Remember only the word’s shape.” Link sounds and the complete spelling, including unusual parts.

“Guess from a picture or first letter.” Read all the letters and check the message.

“Fast is the first goal.” Build accurate reading, then increasingly smooth recognition with practice.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build a small word collection

Choose a few needed words such as have, said and one. Read, discuss their spellings, cover and write them, then use them in a short message. Return to the collection later and add words when earlier ones are secure.

Adjust the challenge Starter: practise one adult-modelled word. Core: read and write a small taught set accurately. Stretch: use the words in a fresh sentence and retrieve their spellings on a later day.

Record reading and writing separately, with prompts and dates. A few correct words do not certify a complete repertoire. Do not use speed, accent or a single recent copy as mastery evidence.

Three tricky questions

1. Does high-frequency always mean regularly spelt?

No. Some common words include unusual sound–letter links.

2. Can one and won be swapped in “one shell”?

No. One is the number word for this meaning.

3. Must every learner find some equally difficult?

No. Choose practice from the child’s actual reading and writing evidence.

FAQ

Is there one fixed word list for this lesson?

No. The examples begin a growing useful repertoire; use taught words needed in reading and writing.

Is cover-write-check enough to claim independent spelling over time?

It rehearses retrieval after study. Revisit without a recent model to check retention.

  • Practise word alone forever — Use it in a sentence.

Explain and apply

Complete the sentence

Choose the high-frequency word: Mia was/were/be happy.

Answer and teaching guidance

was completes the sentence. Students should read the whole sentence and check that it sounds right.

Read without guessing

Read: The dog is small. Which familiar word can you practise reading accurately and then smoothly?

Answer and teaching guidance

The is one high-frequency word in this sentence. Practise accurate reading first, then smooth reading; other familiar words can be practised too. Do not impose a speed score.

Choose the right word

Complete: Can you/your/yes help me?

Answer and teaching guidance

you is correct. The sentence is asking another person for help.

Notice a spelling in a sentence

Write the sentence: I have some apples. Which word has an o that does not represent the common long o sound here?

Answer and teaching guidance

some is a high-frequency word that may not be easy to spell by sound alone, so it should be practised as a known word.
