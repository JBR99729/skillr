YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Make your message clear AC9E1LA01

Learning intention: I can choose words for what I need to communicate and check how the other person understands them.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A question and a useful reply

Jo asks, “Where is the glue?” Kai replies, “Beside the blue paper,” and points to that paper where both can see it. The question needs a place, and the reply supplies one. The gesture helps identify the place. If Jo cannot see the pointing, Kai needs clearer words instead.

Learn the skill

1. Ask and give the needed information

Start with what you want to find out. A useful reply answers that question. A closed question can invite yes or no; an open question invites more information.

Questions: “Is the bag here?” / “What did you put in the bag?” Reply to the second: “I packed a hat and a book.”

Try it · Which question invites a longer description? Why does the reply fit it?

“What did you put in the bag?” invites details about its contents. The reply names the packed things. “Is the bag here?” can be answered yes or no.

2. Notice what the message does

An offer says what you are willing to do; a request asks for an action. A command directs action, and an exclamation expresses a strong reaction. Similar words can have different jobs.

Messages: “I can carry that.” / “Could you carry that?” / “Put it on the bench.” / “What a huge box!”

Try it · Match each message to its job.

In order: offer, request, command and exclamation. A request can be phrased as a question. A calm command can end with a full stop; loudness alone does not decide the purpose.

3. Check words and other clues together

Words, facial expressions, signing and gestures can work together. A clue does not prove a hidden feeling. Ask when a reference or message is unclear.

Adult: put a pencil beside a book where both partners can see it. Say “Put it there” while pointing somewhere unclear, then invite clarification.

Try it · What could the listener ask before moving anything?

For example, “Do you mean this pencil, beside that book?” Name the object and place and check the response. Demonstrate the actual gesture; print alone does not show how it was received.

Common mistakes

“An offer and a request are the same.” Ask who is offering help and who is asking for action.

“A face proves how someone feels.” Use it as a clue and check the situation or ask.

“Everyone understands the same gesture.” Meanings and communication preferences can vary; check understanding.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One object, several messages

Place a safe classroom object between two partners. Ask where it is, give a place answer, offer help with it and request an action. Finish with a calm instruction. Take turns and check that the replies fit.

Adjust the challenge Starter: model one short exchange with two purpose choices. Core: choose and respond to different message purposes. Stretch: keep the purpose when the listener cannot see your pointing, then explain the words you added.

An adult can read and scribe; accept speech, signing or AAC. Do not require eye contact, smiling or touch. Observe the real exchange separately from a written answer. Cultural signs require a named community-endorsed source and local guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “Can I help you?” a request for the other person to carry your things?

It offers your help. Work out what the speaker is willing to do in this situation.

2. Can “Stand behind the line.” be a command when said calmly?

Yes. It directs an action; anger is not required.

3. If someone cannot see your point, is “there” always enough?

No. Name the place clearly or use a communication method the listener can access.

FAQ

Can we discuss a short film?

Yes. An adult supplies a suitable actual scene, pauses it and asks what the character said, what other clues were visible and what remains uncertain. Do not claim a film was watched from its description.

How should cultural communication be explored?

Use an actual named community-endorsed account and its local explanation. Ask what the account teaches about that specific context; do not invent or generalise a cultural sign.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: Information tells us something. A gesture is a body movement that helps send a message. An offer says what we can do for someone. A request asks someone to do something.

1. Words, faces and gestures

Notice the words and other clues together. A face may give a clue about a feeling; it does not prove that feeling.

Example

At the classroom door, Ari waves and says, “Hello!” The wave helps send the greeting.

Try it: Someone points and says, “Put it there.” What else might you need to know?

See answer and explanation

Which object and which place. Ask, “Do you mean this book, on that shelf?” Pointing helps only when the other person can see what it refers to.

2. Ask for information

Ask a question that helps you find out what you need. Listen to the reply.

Example

“Where is the lunch tub?” asks for a place. “When is lunch?” asks for a time.

Try it: You need to know when the class goes to the library. What could you ask?

See answer and explanation

“When do we go to the library?” Accept another clear time question. A yes/no question such as “Is it library time?” answers a narrower question.

3. Give useful information

Reply to what was asked. Add a useful detail rather than an unrelated fact.

Example

“Where is my hat?” → “It is on the hook beside your bag.” The reply gives a place.

Try it: A friend asks, “Who can help carry this box?” Give a useful reply.

See answer and explanation

For example, “I can help,” or name an available helper. A reply about the colour of the box does not answer who.

4. Offers and requests

An offer gives help. A request asks for an action. Either can be calm and respectful.

Example

Offer: “I can help you pack the blocks.” Request: “Could you help me pack the blocks?”

Try it: Turn “Could you carry my books?” into an offer to carry someone else’s books.

See answer and explanation

“I can carry your books,” or “Would you like me to carry your books?” The speaker offers their own help. Requests can also ask for help for another person.

5. Exclamations and commands

An exclamation expresses a strong reaction. A command directs an action. Look at the purpose, not just loudness or the end mark.

Example

“Wow, that rainbow is bright!” shows a reaction. “Stand behind the line.” tells someone what to do.

Try it: An adult calmly says, “Keep away from the road.” What is the message doing?

See answer and explanation

Directing an action to help keep someone safe. It can be a command even when spoken calmly. “Please” and punctuation alone do not decide the purpose.

6. Choose and check the message

Choose words that suit the situation. Ask again when words and other clues are unclear.

Example

Closed: “Is the book here?” can be answered yes or no. Open: “What happened in the story?” invites more information.

Try it: Show a partner where to put a pencil. Check that your words or gesture were understood.

See answer and explanation

Observe the actual exchange: a clear place is named or indicated, and the partner can respond. Accept speech, signing or AAC. An unseen point alone may not give enough information.

Try a worked example together

Choose words for the purpose

Work through the example

  1. The first speaker wants a place, so the reply gives a place.
  2. A request asks someone to do something. It can help you or another person, such as Sam.
  3. Words, expressions and gestures give clues. If clues seem mixed, ask rather than deciding how someone feels.

Watch for this: Commands need not be angry or loud. Accept signing, AAC and home-language support; do not require eye contact or smiling.

Try it together

Change “Can you help me?” into an offer to help someone else.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, “Can I help you?” The speaker now offers their own help.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Gestures and signs can mean different things in different communities. Do not require eye contact, smiling or a particular voice. Discuss a short, suitable film scene: what did the character say, what other clues were visible, and what could you ask to check? For First Nations Australian communication, use a named, community-endorsed account and its local guidance; do not invent, generalise or ask children to copy cultural signs.

Check: A partner asks where the glue is. Give a useful reply. Then ask one open question about the partner’s drawing.

See answer and explanation

The reply gives a clear location. The open question invites a description or explanation, such as “How did you make that picture?” Observe listening and the response rather than scoring only a written choice.

Help: Model one short exchange. Offer two purpose choices. Accept a pointed, signed, spoken or AAC response; an adult can scribe the child’s own words.

Next challenge: Keep the same message but change the audience: a friend, a teacher or someone who cannot see your pointing. Explain the words you changed.

Prepare the film and community-account activities

Adult: supply an actual suitable short film scene before asking about its words and visible communication clues. For First Nations Australian communication, choose an actual named community-endorsed account with local guidance, identify its community and speaker and use only the explanation it provides. Discuss how those words, symbols or gestures work in that specific account; do not invent a universal sign. These source-specific activities require the supplied source.

A short rehearsal before paper practice

Put a pencil beside a book where both partners can see it. Ask, “Where is the pencil?” Give a place answer. Then make a request to pass it and an offer to help put it away.

Model responses and what to notice

Information: “The pencil is beside the book.” Request: “Could you pass the pencil?” Offer: “I can help you put the pencil away.” Accept other clear, relevant wording. The request asks for an action; the offer says what the speaker can do. Observe the learner giving and responding to a message, rather than copying the model.

Support: Offer two purpose choices or model one exchange first. Accept speech, signing or AAC and adult scribing where appropriate. A gesture is optional; eye contact, smiling and touch are not required. Expressions give clues, not certain proof of feelings.

Keep a useful record: Save the learner’s own paper response and note whether the real exchange was independent or supported. Check vocabulary/dictation separately from communication understanding.
