Learn the skill

Six small steps, clear examples

These SkillrHub teaching focuses match Practice A–F in the printable pack.

Learning goal: I can discuss different texts and explain their jobs using clues in the words, pictures and layout.

Start together: Read the short messages aloud together. Compare their jobs and point to clues. Use school, home and community examples; decoding and handwriting are not this code’s target.

Useful words: Text: a message made with words, pictures or both. Purpose: the text’s job. Feature: a part we can notice, such as a heading, picture or step. Clue: something that helps us work out the job.

1. Find a text purpose

A text is a message made with words, pictures or both. Its purpose is its job: it might tell a story, share facts, give steps, invite someone or encourage a choice. Read the message, not just its topic.

Read two texts about a kite

Text A: “Jo took her kite to the park. The wind lifted it high.” Text B: “A kite has a frame and a tail.”

Work it through

A tells events involving Jo: it tells a story. B shares information about a kite. The topic is the same, but the texts do different jobs. A story can be invented or based on real events.

Try it together: Which text helps us learn what a kite has? Give a clue.

See answer and teaching guidance

B. The frame and tail are information about a kite. Accept a spoken, signed or supported explanation using this detail.

Watch for this: An animal or object in a text does not make it a fact text. Ask what the whole message does.

On paper: Practice A has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

2. Use instruction clues

Instructions tell someone what to do. A title may name the task. A list can tell what is needed. Doing words such as fold and draw tell the actions; numbered steps can show their order.

Make a name card

You need: paper and a pencil. 1. Fold the paper. 2. Write your name. 3. Stand the card on the table.

Work it through

The list names the materials. The steps show the actions in order. The whole text helps someone make a name card. A picture of a folded card could help show how to do the first step.

Try it together: What do the numbers help the reader do? How is the materials list different?

See answer and teaching guidance

The numbers help the reader follow the order. The list tells what is needed; it does not give the method.

Watch for this: Numbers can organise a story too. Check whether the words tell the reader to act or describe events.

On paper: Practice B has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

3. Use information clues

An information text helps a reader learn or find something out. A heading names a topic, a label names a part, and a contents list can show where to find a topic. Facts and useful details support the text’s job.

Find a topic

Contents: Birds — page 2; Frogs — page 5. On the bird page, a picture label says “magpie” and the sentence says “Birds have feathers.”

Work it through

The contents list points to the bird page. The label names the pictured bird. The sentence shares a fact. Each feature helps readers find or understand information.

Try it together: Where would you look for frog information? What does a picture label do?

See answer and teaching guidance

Start on page 5. A label names the pictured object, animal or part. Accept an explanation that links the feature with its use.

Watch for this: “I like frogs best” tells a liking, not a fact about frogs. Do not treat every sentence about an animal as factual information.

On paper: Practice C has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

4. Use persuasion clues

Some texts try to make us choose or do something. Look for the request and a reason supporting it. A request tells what the writer wants; a reason explains why someone might do it.

A class request

“Please choose the shady park for our class walk. We can rest in the shade.”

Work it through

The writer wants the class to choose the shady park. Resting in the shade is the reason. The words encourage a choice rather than give a walking route.

Try it together: Name the request and its reason. Would “Our class went to the park” have the same job?

See answer and teaching guidance

Request: choose the shady park. Reason: we can rest in the shade. “Our class went to the park” reports an event; it does not ask the reader to choose.

Watch for this: A poster can give information, warn or persuade. Its size, bright colour or the word “please” alone does not prove its purpose.

On paper: Practice D has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

5. Use invitation clues

An invitation asks people to come to an event. It usually tells what is happening, when it happens and where to go. These details help the invited people plan.

Come to a class show

“Come to our class show! Friday at 2 pm. School hall.”

Work it through

“Come” invites readers. “Class show” tells what; “Friday at 2 pm” tells when; “school hall” tells where. A factual note about halls would have a different purpose.

Try it together: The invitation says “Come to our show on Friday” but gives no place. What needs adding, and why?

See answer and teaching guidance

Add where the show is, for example “in the school hall”. Guests need to know where to go. Other event details may also need checking.

Watch for this: An attractive picture cannot replace missing event details. Read the message to check that people can actually attend.

On paper: Practice E has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

6. Explain with evidence

Tell someone the text’s job and point to a useful clue. Compare the whole messages. Similar purposes often share patterns, but one feature is a clue rather than a rule. A text can have more than one purpose.

Same numbers, different jobs

Text A: “1. Jo opened the box. 2. A toy bird popped out.” Text B: “1. Fold the paper. 2. Draw a bird.”

Work it through

A describes events: it tells a story. B directs the reader’s actions: it gives steps. Both use numbers, so we need the words as well. Use “It is for ___ because ___.”

Try it together: “Come to our book stall on Saturday. Every sale helps buy class books.” What jobs does this text do? Point to clues.

See answer and teaching guidance

It invites people to the book stall (“Come” and “Saturday”) and encourages buying by giving a benefit (“helps buy class books”). Accept a main-purpose answer with evidence, or a supported explanation of both jobs.

Watch for this: A title, picture or single word may fit several purposes. Read enough of the text and discuss the evidence.

On paper: Practice F has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

Teaching help: discuss the text and its clues

Model, compare, then discuss

  1. Read a text aloud. Think aloud: “What does this want the reader to know, do or enjoy?” Point to a clue.
  2. Compare two texts about one topic. Explain how the jobs and useful features differ.
  3. Let the child choose a text for a need, point to a clue and discuss why it fits.
  4. Use a fresh text for a short independent explanation. Record the support used.

Support: Use two short cards, adult read-aloud and “It is for ___ because ___”. Accept speech, sign, AAC, pointing plus explanation or adult scribing.

Core: Name a text’s job and give a relevant clue. Compare texts with similar purposes and notice recurring features.

Extend within Year 1: Compare one feature across different jobs, or explain two purposes in one short message. Formal essay terminology is unnecessary.

Exit ticket

Read: “Library closed today. Open again on Monday.” What is the text for? How do its details help?

Expected evidence and next step

It gives opening information so people know when they can use the library. The closing and reopening details support that job. If the child only says “It is about a library”, reread and ask what the reader can do with the information. Observe a discussion of two texts as well; a paper choice alone does not demonstrate discussion.

Boundary: Discuss familiar school and community texts and features indicating purpose. Accurate spelling, fluent decoding and advanced genre terminology are not the assessment target.

Use features to work out a text’s job

Work through the example

  1. Look at how the text is organised.
  2. These numbered actions are a clue that the text gives instructions.
  3. Check other clues too. A story can mention steps without being a procedure.

Watch for this: One feature is a clue, not always proof of the whole text’s purpose.

Try it together

What features might help you find facts about birds?

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, headings, labels and factual sentences in an information text. Explain their use.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Guessing from topic — Use features as evidence.
  • One feature only — Check more than one clue if possible.
  • Purpose word missing — Name the purpose clearly.
Try these
  1. Feature hunt — Find features in a real or teacher-made text.
    title / steps / facts
  2. Purpose sort — Sort sample text cards by purpose.
    entertain / inform / persuade
  3. Evidence sentence — Use a relevant feature as a clue, then check it against the whole text.
    It has steps, so...
Explain and apply

Match feature to purpose

A page has a title, numbered steps and a picture of ingredients. What is the text probably for?

Answer and teaching guidance

These features suggest an instruction text because the reader is being shown what to do in order.

Notice opinion clues

Read: Our class should plant more trees because they give shade. What is the writer trying to do?

Answer and teaching guidance

The writer is trying to persuade. The word should and the reason because they give shade are useful clues.

Find information clues

Read: Frogs lay eggs in water. Which feature shows this text is giving information?

Answer and teaching guidance

It gives a fact about frogs. Informative texts teach the reader about a topic.

Choose the best text

Which text would help you make a paper hat: a story, a poster or steps with pictures?

Answer and teaching guidance

Steps with pictures are best because they show the order and actions needed to make something.

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • What feature suggests a recipe?
  • What purpose might a story have?
  • How can a poster persuade?
  • Use a feature as evidence.

I am ready when I can

  • I can identify the purpose of texts used at school and in the community.
  • I can recognise that texts with similar purposes often use similar structures and features.
