YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Use clues to build and check meaning AC9E1LY05

Learning intention: I can use comprehension strategies and explain which clues support my understanding.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A prediction can change when we read more

Original opening: “Dark clouds filled the sky. Jo took a raincoat.” Rain is a reasonable prediction because of the clouds and raincoat, but it is not certain. Next part: “The clouds passed. Jo carried the coat home in sunshine.” Read the new information and revise the prediction instead of ignoring it.

Learn the skill

1. Picture, predict and check

Visualise what the words actually name. Before reading further, predict from a title, picture or text clue. Separate that prediction from what is already stated, then read on.

Original opening: “Two kittens sat under a bench. A dog came along the path.” Make a prediction before opening the answer to read the next part.

Try it · Name the details your mental picture must keep. Before hearing the next part, predict with a clue; then check it.

Keep two kittens, sitting under a bench, and a dog coming along a path. Different predictions are possible if explained. Next part: “The dog walked past, and the kittens stayed where they were.” Check your first idea against that new information. The words do not specify colours.

2. Connect and infer without claiming certainty

Link a text to relevant knowledge or experience. An inference uses a clue plus what you know; it is not a made-up fact. Read surrounding words to check new vocabulary.

A: “The path was slick after rain. It was easy to slip.” B: “Mia smiled when her friend arrived.”

Try it · What does slick mean here? What might Mia feel, and what is the clue?

Slick means slippery here: rain and “easy to slip” support it. Mia may feel pleased to see the friend; the smile is a clue, not certainty about every feeling. A relevant safe connection can help explain the idea without changing the text.

3. Summarise, question and find evidence

A summary keeps important ideas rather than every small detail. Ask questions and check whether the answer is stated, can be inferred or is not given. Build topic vocabulary before and during reading.

A seedling is a young plant. Original text: “Luca planted a seedling. The soil felt dry, so Luca watered it. Later the leaves looked firmer.”

Try it · Give a short summary. Ask a question the words answer. Do they tell the plant’s exact age?

For example, Luca waters a young plant when the soil is dry, and its leaves later look firmer. “Why did Luca water it?” is answered by the dry soil. Exact age is not given. Accept a faithful summary and another answerable question.

Common mistakes

“Every mental picture must look identical.” Keep the named details; leave unstated colours or designs open.

“A prediction is already a fact.” Explain the clue and check it against later information.

“The text answers every question.” Say when information is missing instead of guessing it.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Listen, summarise and ask

Adult reads this original information text without pointing out the answers: “Beetles have six legs. Their hard wing covers protect their wings.” The learner recalls two details, explains what the text is mainly about and asks one answerable question. Reread for access if needed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult reads a short complete text and helps locate a clue. Core: use a strategy and explain evidence. Stretch: revise a prediction, distinguish stated/inferred/missing information or give a concise summary with a relevant question.

Personal disclosure is optional. Record whether the learner listened, viewed or read and what help was given. A text can support several reasonable inferences; keep claims proportionate to its evidence. Do not score artistic skill or assume adult-read responses prove independent reading.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a sensible prediction turn out wrong?

Yes. New information can change it; explain the clue and the revision.

2. Is one small detail always a summary?

No. A summary needs to keep what the whole short text is mainly about or its important connected events.

3. Must an inference be certain?

No. Use may or probably where appropriate and give the clue. Some questions cannot be answered from the supplied text.

FAQ

Can these strategies be used while listening or viewing?

Yes. Use actual spoken text or visible evidence and record that response route.

Does a connection have to reveal a private memory?

No. A safe relevant experience or known example can support understanding without private disclosure.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
1. Visualise the details

Visualising means making a picture in your mind from the words. Keep the objects, actions and places that the text actually names.

Read: A child reads an open book at a table.

Work through it: Picture the child, the open book and the table. A closed book would change a stated detail. Your drawing can be simple; the important part is its meaning.

Try it: A dog curls up on a soft mat. Describe or draw what you picture. Which details came from the words?

Check the answer

Include a dog, curled up, on a soft mat. The words do not give its colour, so more than one drawing is possible. Do not score artistic quality.

2. Predict, then check

A prediction is an idea about what might happen or what a text might be about. Use the title, picture or words as a clue. Read more to keep or change your idea.

Before reading on: What might the children do with the leaves? Say “I think … because …”. Record your first prediction before opening the next part.

Read the next part and check

The children put the leaves in a garden bin.

Did you keep or change your prediction? Explain using this new information. Putting leaves in a bin was a sensible prediction, but the picture alone did not guarantee it.

Try it with words: Dark clouds filled the sky. Mia took an umbrella. What may happen next?

Check the answer

It may rain. The clouds and umbrella support this idea; they do not prove rain will fall.

Try it with a book: Use an adult-supplied book. Look at its title and cover, make a prediction, then pause during reading to check it.

3. Connect and infer

A connection links a text with something you have experienced, learnt or imagined. Explain the shared part, rather than naming an unrelated memory.

Model a connection: “This reminds me of leaving water for birds. Both give birds something they need.”

Try it: What does this picture remind you of? Say how the two situations are connected. A known or imagined example is fine.

Check the answer

Accept any relevant connection with an explanation, such as feeding a pet or helping wildlife. A learner does not need to disclose a personal experience.

An inference uses a clue plus what you know to work out something the words do not state directly.

Read: Max smiled after opening a gift. Max probably felt pleased: his smile is the clue, and we know people often smile when pleased.

Try it: Sam packed a raincoat. Why might Sam have packed it?

Check the answer

Sam may expect rain or want to stay dry. A raincoat is used in wet weather. The text does not prove it will rain; other sensible explanations are possible.

4. Find the main idea and summarise

The main idea tells what the whole text or picture is mostly about. A summary keeps the important ideas or events and leaves out small details.

Read: Mo had a flat tyre. Mum helped fix it. Mo rode again.

Model: “Mo gets help to fix a tyre and rides again.” This keeps the problem, help and result. “Mo has a tyre” leaves out the main events.

Try it: Beetles have six legs. Their hard wing covers protect their wings. Many beetles have spots. What is this text mostly about?

Check the answer

Features of beetles. This covers the legs, wing covers and possible spots. “Spots” alone is just one detail.

Retell a story: Use a familiar adult-supplied book. Briefly say the important beginning, what happens, and how it ends. You do not need to repeat every sentence.

5. Ask questions and find evidence

Ask questions that help you understand. Check whether an answer is stated, can be inferred with a clue, or is not given.

Read: Kim puts a pear in a lunchbox.

  • What does Kim pack? A pear. Point to “pear”.
  • Who eats it? These words do not say.

Try it: Ruby put her bag under the bench. Ask a question that these words answer, then show the evidence.

Check the answer

For example: “Where did Ruby put her bag?” Answer: under the bench. The words “under the bench” are the evidence. Accept another accurate question and answer.

Listen for information

Adult reads aloud: “A garden bird eats seeds. It drinks water from a shallow dish.” Ask the learner what the bird eats and where it drinks. Read twice if needed without pointing out the answers.

Check the answer

Seeds; from a shallow dish. Accept two distinct accurate details. Record listening as the response route.

Use vocabulary and context

Read: A path was slick after rain. It was easy to slip.

“Easy to slip” helps explain that slick means slippery here. Use the sentence around an unfamiliar word, then check that your meaning fits.

Try it: The towel was damp after the splash, but it was not dripping. What does damp mean here? Which words help?

Check the answer

A little wet. The splash and “not dripping” help distinguish this from completely dry or soaking wet. Let the learner use the clues before defining the word.

Quick check: explain your thinking

Use these short checks with listening, viewing or reading. An adult may read the text aloud.

  1. Picture this: “Two kittens sit under a bench.” Name the details you must keep.
  2. “Lee put a finished book in a bag for the library.” What might Lee do next? Give a clue.
  3. “Ava’s smile faded when her ice block fell.” How might Ava feel? Explain.
  4. “Jo spilled some pencils. Jo picked them up and put them in a pot.” Give a short summary.
  5. “A bird built a nest in a tree.” Ask a question the words answer. Can they tell you how much the nest cost?
Check the answer

1. Two kittens, sitting, under a bench. 2. Lee may return the book; it is finished and the bag is for the library. 3. Ava may feel sad or disappointed; the lost treat and fading smile are clues. 4. Jo tidies up the spilled pencils. 5. For example, “Where did the bird build its nest?”—in a tree. The words give no price. Accept equivalent answers supported by the details.

If an answer is uncertain, revisit the matching focus. Use the learner’s explanation to choose the next teaching step.

Model a connection: “This reminds me of leaving water for birds. Both give birds something they need.”

Both give birds something they need.”

Curriculum alignment

AC9E1LY05: use comprehension strategies such as visualising, predicting, connecting, summarising and questioning when listening, viewing and reading to build literal and inferred meaning by drawing on vocabulary and growing knowledge of context and text structures

  • identifying information and details from spoken informative texts
  • building topic knowledge and learning new vocabulary before and during reading
  • making predictions from the cover, from illustrations and at points in the text before reading on, and confirming and adjusting understanding after reading
  • drawing inferences and explaining inferences using clues from the text
  • making connections with existing knowledge and personal experiences
