Learn the skill

Two ways to practise clear word writing

Look at the model, watch the movement, write, then check. These examples use NSW/ACT-style unjoined letter forms; follow the forms and starting points taught at your school.

Get ready: hold, move and look

Unjoined means the letters are separate: there are no joining strokes between them. A word has meaning. It can be one letter or several letters in order. Upper-case letters are also called capitals.

  1. Sit comfortably with your body steady and feet supported. Rest your writing arm on the table.
  2. Steady the paper with your other hand. Turn it a little so your writing hand can move and you can see your work.
  3. Hold your pencil comfortably so you can guide it without squeezing hard. A useful grip lets you form letters with control.

Adult model: Show the start point and movement for one letter using the school's handwriting style. Let the child make the movement in the air, then on paper. Watch their hand as well as the finished letter.

Different functional grasps can work. Look for comfortable, controlled movement and readable marks; do not insist on one identical grip for every child. Pause if the hand becomes sore or tired.

1. Write words with lower-case letters

Look closely before writing. Notice which letters are tall, which stay small, and which have a part below the line.

Worked example: write bag

  1. Read the model: b–a–g. Notice the tall b, small a and tail on g.
  2. Watch an adult write each letter slowly, showing its start point and direction.
  3. Copy bag on the line. Finish each letter and lift the pencil before starting the next letter. Keep the letters unjoined.
  4. Read your word back. Check the shapes and line placement against the model. Try the word again, improving one letter if needed.

Try together: Write sun on lined paper. Then write leg. Which letter in leg is tall? Which has a tail below the line?

Check the writing

Both words need three recognisable, unjoined letters in order. In leg, l is tall, e stays small and g has a tail below the line. Look at the actual writing, not only the spoken answer.

If a letter turns the wrong way: Compare it with the school model. For b and d, notice which side of the tall stroke the rounded part is on. Model the movement again, then practise the letter in a short word.

2. Write capitals and lower-case letters in words

A capital and its lower-case partner can look quite different. Recognising the pair helps you choose the right form; you still need to practise writing it.

Worked example: write Ava

  1. Ava is a person's name, so it begins with capital A.
  2. Watch the adult form A, then lower-case v and a. Keep all three letters unjoined.
  3. Copy Ava. Check the capital's shape, the smaller v and a, and the spaces between the letters.

Watch for this: Writing AVA or aVa does not match the name model. Use the capital where it is needed and lower-case letters for the rest.

Try together: Copy the name Sam. Point to the capital before you write. Then copy Sam can sit on one line, leaving a clear gap between each word.

Check the writing

The name starts with a recognisable capital S followed by lower-case a and m. The phrase has three separate words: Sam | can | sit. The bars here show the word boundaries; do not copy them. Look for unjoined letters and two clear word gaps.

Put it together: space, read and improve

Separate letters still belong together in a word. A word space should be bigger than the small gaps between letters.

Worked example: cat sat

  1. Write the three letters in cat, keeping them separate but close together.
  2. Leave a clear word space. A finger-space reminder can help while you learn.
  3. Write sat, then read both words back. Check that they do not run together as catsat.

Paper check: Write red ball and the name Tom. Ask a partner to read them. Choose one word to write again as clearly as you can.

What to look for
  • Recognisable lower-case letters in red ball, with one clear word space.
  • A recognisable capital T followed by lower-case o and m in Tom.
  • Unjoined letters, sensible line placement and comfortable pencil control.

If word gaps are missing, model one space and repeat the phrase. If a letter is unclear, practise its movement before rewriting the word. A clear first attempt does not need an invented correction.

Handwriting evidence: Watch the child form the letters. Naming letters, pointing to the correct word or selecting an online answer can support learning, but does not demonstrate pencil control or independent handwriting.

For adults: teaching support and curriculum

Support: Use larger lines, a nearby model and one short word at a time. Model, allow tracing if helpful, then ask the child to copy without tracing. Read directions aloud; the child produces the handwriting.

Core: Write short words with lower-case letters and familiar names with initial capitals. Observe comfort, movement and readable forms.

Extend within this skill: Write a short label or phrase using familiar words; check case and word spaces. Prioritise control and readability over speed.
