YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Use sounds and spelling patterns to read and write AC9E1LY11

Learning intention: I can blend and write short words and familiar two-syllable words.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A digraph and a cluster work differently

Ship has /sh/ /i/ /p/: sh represents one sound. Slip has /s/ /l/ /i/ /p/: s and l represent separate sounds. Read through the complete word. For spelling, hear a word with the printed model hidden, say the sounds, choose taught letters or patterns, write it and read it back.

Learn the skill

1. Notice the vowel pattern

Learn short vowels and common long-vowel spellings through familiar words. A long vowel often sounds like its letter name. Read the whole word; no one pattern works in every word.

Cap has a short vowel. Cape has a long a sound in the a–consonant–e pattern. Rain and day show two other common long a spellings.

Try it · Read kit and kite aloud. What written part helps you distinguish them?

Kit has short i; kite has long i and includes final e in the pattern. Hear independent reading before an adult supplies the words. Do not infer a separate syllable for final e.

2. Keep blends and digraphs clear

In a blend, keep each consonant sound. A digraph uses two letters for one sound. Say the whole word to check rather than guess from the first letter.

Wish: /w/ /i/ /sh/. Rest: /r/ /e/ /s/ /t/. Both have short vowels; only rest ends with two separate consonant sounds.

Try it · Read chin and clap. Then cover them and write one, saying its sounds.

Chin begins with the single /ch/ sound; clap begins /k/ /l/. Check the chosen standard spelling after writing. If the adult dictated it first, record spelling rather than independent reading.

3. Join familiar syllables

A two-syllable word has two spoken parts, each with a vowel sound. Blend within each part and join them into the whole word. Use meaning to check, not to guess.

Sunset: sun–set. Bedtime: bed–time, with short e then long i. Read both parts before joining.

Try it · Read napkin aloud. Cover it, write it and read your writing back.

Nap–kin joins to napkin. Check n-a-p-k-i-n, both vowel letters and the order. Copying a visible word is useful practice but does not show spelling without the model.

Common mistakes

“A blend is a single sound.” Keep both sounds in /k/ /l/.

“Every final e makes a long vowel.” Learn common patterns and word-specific exceptions such as have.

“The first letter is enough.” Read all the sounds and syllables.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Read, hide, write and check

Choose a few taught words from wish, rest, rain, kite, sunset and bedtime. Child reads each before an adult says it. Then hide the model, dictate a chosen word, write it and compare with the spelling. Note reading and writing separately.

Adjust the challenge Starter: model one familiar short-vowel word. Core: read and write digraph, blend and common long-vowel words. Stretch: combine familiar patterns in a two-syllable word, such as raincoat, after teaching both parts.

Use the child’s taught phonics sequence and ordinary accent. The printed targets must be visible for reading and hidden during the spelling attempt. Extra reading support is recorded, not treated as independent decoding.

Three tricky questions

1. Can ship be a short-vowel word and a digraph word?

Yes. The vowel and consonant spelling describe different features.

2. Does kite have two syllables because it has i and e?

No. It has one spoken vowel sound and one syllable.

3. Is reading a word after the adult says it independent decoding?

It may be supported practice. Check independent reading before supplying the target.

FAQ

Do we need to learn every spelling pattern at once?

No. Practise taught correspondences, then add useful new ones gradually.

Why read the written word back?

It helps notice missing sounds, wrong order or a spelling that changes the word; compare with the standard spelling too.

  • Blend sounds to read one- and two-syllable words.

Blend a digraph as one sound

Work through the example

  1. The letters sh work together for one sound in ship.
  2. Blend /sh/, /i/, /p/ to read ship. Do not add a separate /s/ and /h/.
  3. A blend is different: the f and r in frog keep two sounds. Learn the particular spelling pattern in each word.

Watch for this: Two letters sometimes represent one sound; they do not always do so.

Try it together

Read ship, then spell it using the sounds and their letters.

Answer and teaching guidance

Hear /sh/, /i/, /p/ and see the spelling s-h-i-p. Reading and spelling are checked separately.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Digraph counted twice — sh/ch/th can make one sound.
  • Guessing from first letter — Look through the whole word.
  • Vowel missing — Every syllable needs a vowel sound.
Try these
  1. Pattern sort — Sort once by vowel sound, then again by consonant spelling. A word may have a short vowel and a digraph, or a long vowel and a blend.
    ship / frog / cake
  2. Blend and write — Blend a word, then write the sounds in order.
    shop
  3. Two-syllable clap — Clap and read a two-syllable word.
    sun-set
Explain and apply

Find a digraph

Read: ship, chip, sip. Which word has sh?

Answer and teaching guidance

ship has sh. The two letters sh work together to make one sound at the start of the word.

Hear both blend sounds

Read: fog, frog, drum. Which word has the blend fr?

Answer and teaching guidance

frog has fr. In a consonant blend, both sounds are heard: /f/ then /r/.

Long vowel pattern

Choose the word with ai: ran, rain, red.

Answer and teaching guidance

rain has ai. The letters ai commonly make the long a sound in one-syllable words.

Blend a two-syllable word

Read sunset by parts: sun-set. How can you blend it?

Answer and teaching guidance

Read each syllable, then join them: sun-set becomes sunset. This builds from sound work into fluent word reading.

Curriculum alignment

  • using knowledge of letters and sounds to write words with short vowels; for example, “man”, and common long vowel sounds; for example, “time”
