YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Hear how patterns connect a poem or chant AC9E1LA04

Learning intention: I can notice repeated words, rhyming endings and spoken rhythm, then explain how they link the lines.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A returning start and a matching ending

Say these original lines: “Tap, tap, by the tree. / Tap, tap, come with me.” Tap, tap returns at the start; tree and me rhyme at the ends. The pattern helps a listener anticipate the next line and join in. It also keeps the two lines sounding connected.

Learn the skill

1. Notice what returns

Listen for repeated words, phrases or lines. Name exactly what returns and what changes. A repeated sentence frame can connect lines even without rhyme.

“Here comes rain. / Here comes wind. / Here comes sunshine.”

Try it · What repeats? Create another meaningful line using that start.

Here comes repeats; the final word changes. “Here comes night” is one possible new line. The returning frame makes the lines feel related.

2. Hear a rhyme in the whole line

Rhyming endings match in sound, not necessarily in spelling. Read or hear the complete lines and check that a new ending keeps a sensible meaning.

“A small frog sat upon a log. / Beside it slept a tired ___.” Choices: dog / hen.

Try it · Which ending keeps the rhyme and makes sense?

Dog rhymes with log and can be described as tired. Hen does not share that ending. Accept locally valid spoken rhymes when creating a fresh line.

3. Keep a beat while the word rhythm varies

A beat is a steady pulse. Rhythm is the timing of the spoken sounds. An adult can model two shorter syllables within one steady beat, so words do not all need one beat each.

Adult: make four evenly spaced taps. Say “tap | tap | lit-tle | tap”, fitting lit-tle inside the third tap. Model slowly, then invite an echo.

Try it · What stays steady, and which beat contains two spoken parts?

The four taps stay steady; little has two syllables within the third beat. Observe the actual echo. A written choice cannot demonstrate timing.

Common mistakes

“A poem is wrong unless it rhymes.” Repetition or rhythm can connect lines without rhyme.

“Rhyming words need matching first letters.” Compare their spoken endings.

“Every word gets one beat.” Hear the model: two parts of little can fit inside one beat.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Keep a returning line

An adult reads “Look, look, at the light. / Look, look, it is bright.” Identify Look, look and light/bright. Make another two-line pattern, then perform it at a manageable pace.

Adjust the challenge Starter: echo a repeated start. Core: identify repetition and rhyme and show the supplied rhythm. Stretch: create two connected lines and explain how returning words or matching sounds help a listener predict.

Adults supply actual speech or singing; no recording is included here. Accept accessible tapping, pointing or movement and judge the sound pattern separately from motor speed or spelling.

Three tricky questions

1. Can repetition link lines without rhyme?

Yes. Returning words or sentence frames can make them connected and predictable.

2. Are beat and syllable count always the same?

No. The timing of spoken syllables can vary against a steady beat.

3. If I choose a rhyming word that makes no sense, is the line finished?

Check both the sound and meaning. Revise the line so the pattern supports what you want to say.

FAQ

What does cohesion mean here?

The parts hold together: returning words and sound patterns help listeners connect the lines. The child can explain this in ordinary language.

Can we compare a poem, chant and song?

Yes. Supply and perform the actual texts. A chant uses spoken patterns; a song has a tune. Compare the patterns heard rather than rely only on the labels.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: Repeat means come back again. Rhyme means matching ending sounds, as in sun and run. Beat means steady taps. Rhythm is how we time the word sounds.

1. Hear repetition

Listen for a word, a phrase or a whole line that comes back.

Example

“Here comes rain. Here comes wind. Here comes sunshine.” Here comes repeats; the last word changes.

Try it: Make another line that starts with “Here comes …”.

See answer and explanation

Accept a meaningful line such as “Here comes night.” Keep the repeated start; discuss the new ending.

2. Find rhyme

Say the words aloud. Listen to the ending sound, not just the letters.

Example

“A bird flew by. It crossed the sky.” By and sky rhyme.

Try it: Which matches the ending sound of day: play or dog?

See answer and explanation

Play. Read both options naturally; accept pronunciation differences and discuss the sounds the child actually uses.

3. Feel beat and rhythm

Keep four steady taps. Fit the spoken sounds into those taps as shown in the model.

Example

Beat cells: tap | tap | lit-tle | tap. Say two quicker parts of little inside the third tap. The taps stay steady while the word sounds vary.

Try it: Echo the model. Are the steady taps the same thing as the word sounds?

See answer and explanation

No. The beat is the steady pulse; rhythm is the timing of the word sounds. Observe the echo, not just a written choice. No rule says every word gets one beat.

4. Hear how the parts connect

Returning words and matching sounds can link the lines and help us join in.

Example

“Clap, clap, in the sun. Clap, clap, now we run.” Clap, clap returns; sun/run rhyme.

Try it: What could a listener predict at the start of another line?

See answer and explanation

Clap, clap. The returning start links the lines and helps a listener join in. Other meaningful patterns are possible.

5. Compare poems, chants and songs

Listen to how each text uses a pattern. Some poems rhyme; some do not. Chants can repeat spoken patterns; songs use a tune.

Example

Compare “Rain on the roof. Rain on my coat.” with “Rain, rain, on the plain.” Both repeat rain; only the second example has a rhyming pair.

Try it: Can a poem use repetition without rhyme?

See answer and explanation

Yes. Returning words can connect a poem even without matching ending sounds. Do not mark a poem wrong just because it does not rhyme.

6. Change and create a pattern

Keep one part of a pattern and change another. Check that the new line still makes sense.

Example

“Tap, tap, by the tree. Tap, tap, come with me.” Keep tap, tap; tree/me match.

Try it: Create two short lines with a repeated start. Read or perform them.

See answer and explanation

Accept meaningful lines with a clearly returning start, with or without rhyme. If rhyme is requested, judge the spoken ending sounds. Observe the actual timing or performance when that is the goal.

Work through the example

  2. Tap, tap repeats. Sun and run rhyme because their ending sounds match.
  3. Repetition, rhyme and rhythm can help the lines sound connected.

Answer and teaching guidance

Fun. Listen to the matching ending sound, not only the spelling.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Beat and rhythm are related but different. Syllable count alone does not tell us exactly how words are timed. Model slowly, then echo. Children can tap, point, move or use another accessible way to show the pattern; motor speed and spelling are separate from understanding.

Check: In “Look, look, at the light. Look, look, it is bright”, identify the repeated phrase and the rhyming endings. Show the pattern aloud or by pointing.

See answer and explanation

Look, look repeats; light and bright rhyme. Ask the child to show or explain how the returning words connect the lines. A written selection alone does not establish performance.
