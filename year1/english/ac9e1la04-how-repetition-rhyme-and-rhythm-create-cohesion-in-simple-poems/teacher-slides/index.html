Explore how repetition, rhyme and rhythm create cohesion in simple poems, chants and songs

Repetition, rhyme and rhythm help simple poems, chants and songs sound connected and easy to join in

Ready to project and teach

Teach it step by step

Work through these six teaching focuses in short sessions. Read, model, try and explain; return to any focus that needs more support.

Words to teach: Repeat means come back again. Rhyme means matching ending sounds, as in sun and run. Beat means steady taps. Rhythm is how we time the word sounds.

1. Hear repetition

Listen for a word, a phrase or a whole line that comes back.

Model together

“Here comes rain. Here comes wind. Here comes sunshine.” Here comes repeats; the last word changes.

Your turn: Make another line that starts with “Here comes …”.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept a meaningful line such as “Here comes night.” Keep the repeated start; discuss the new ending.

2. Find rhyme

Say the words aloud. Listen to the ending sound, not just the letters.

Model together

“A bird flew by. It crossed the sky.” By and sky rhyme.

Your turn: Which matches the ending sound of day: play or dog?

Answer and teaching guidance

Play. Read both options naturally; accept pronunciation differences and discuss the sounds the child actually uses.

3. Feel beat and rhythm

Keep four steady taps. Fit the spoken sounds into those taps as shown in the model.

Model together

taptaplit-tletap

Beat cells: tap | tap | lit-tle | tap. Say two quicker parts of little inside the third tap. The taps stay steady while the word sounds vary.

Your turn: Echo the model. Are the steady taps the same thing as the word sounds?

Answer and teaching guidance

No. The beat is the steady pulse; rhythm is the timing of the word sounds. Observe the echo, not just a written choice. No rule says every word gets one beat.

4. Hear how the parts connect

Returning words and matching sounds can link the lines and help us join in.

Model together

“Clap, clap, in the sun. Clap, clap, now we run.” Clap, clap returns; sun/run rhyme.

Your turn: What could a listener predict at the start of another line?

Answer and teaching guidance

Clap, clap. The returning start links the lines and helps a listener join in. Other meaningful patterns are possible.

5. Compare poems, chants and songs

Listen to how each text uses a pattern. Some poems rhyme; some do not. Chants can repeat spoken patterns; songs use a tune.

Model together

Compare “Rain on the roof. Rain on my coat.” with “Rain, rain, on the plain.” Both repeat rain; only the second example has a rhyming pair.

Your turn: Can a poem use repetition without rhyme?

Answer and teaching guidance

Yes. Returning words can connect a poem even without matching ending sounds. Do not mark a poem wrong just because it does not rhyme.

6. Change and create a pattern

Keep one part of a pattern and change another. Check that the new line still makes sense.

Model together

“Tap, tap, by the tree. Tap, tap, come with me.” Keep tap, tap; tree/me match.

Your turn: Create two short lines with a repeated start. Read or perform them.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept meaningful lines with a clearly returning start, with or without rhyme. If rhyme is requested, judge the spoken ending sounds. Observe the actual timing or performance when that is the goal.

Support, extend and check learning

Watch for this

Beat and rhythm are related but different. Syllable count alone does not tell us exactly how words are timed. Model slowly, then echo. Children can tap, point, move or use another accessible way to show the pattern; motor speed and spelling are separate from understanding.

Support

Read one line at a time. Echo it together. Mark repeated words with matching shapes and practise rhyme using two spoken choices.

Extend within this skill

Compare two original short poems: explain how one uses a returning line and another uses rhyme. Create a new example without losing its meaning.

Quick check

In “Look, look, at the light. Look, look, it is bright”, identify the repeated phrase and the rhyming endings. Show the pattern aloud or by pointing.

Expected evidence and next step

Look, look repeats; light and bright rhyme. Ask the child to show or explain how the returning words connect the lines. A written selection alone does not establish performance.

If the explanation is unclear, model a different example and try again. Record what the child can explain or do, and the support used; a paper score alone does not establish an observed speaking or performance skill.

An adult can read the directions and explain taught terms. Accept spoken, signed, pointed, AAC or scribed responses when these preserve the skill being checked. Short directions do not assume every child can independently decode all the words.

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Rhyme = same first sound: Rhyme usually matches the ending sound.
  • Reading without rhythm: Clap or tap the beat first.
  • Missing repeated words: Listen for words or lines that come back.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh, code-aligned example. The student should show the exact word, sound, sentence, image feature, audience clue or text evidence that supports the answer.

Quick checks
