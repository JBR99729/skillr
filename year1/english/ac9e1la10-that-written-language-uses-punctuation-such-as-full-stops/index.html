YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Use capitals and end marks for a clear message AC9E1LA10

Learning intention: I can choose punctuation for the message, capitalise familiar names and check my writing.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Read the two messages before editing

“mia has a kite where is ben” contains a telling idea and a direct question. Write: “Mia has a kite. Where is Ben?” Mia and Ben are names, each sentence starts with a capital, and the question ends with ?. Read the two sentences aloud and notice their boundaries; a pause alone does not always require a full stop.

Learn the skill

1. Match the end mark to the message

A full stop can end a calm statement or instruction. A question mark ends a direct question. An exclamation mark can show strong feeling, surprise or urgency when the context supports it.

A calm statement: “The gate is shut”. A direct question: “Is the gate shut”. An excited reaction: “What a wonderful surprise”.

Try it · Choose a fitting end mark for each supplied context.

The gate is shut. Is the gate shut? What a wonderful surprise! Purpose and context guide the marks; not every instruction needs an exclamation mark.

2. Capitalise particular names

Use capitals for familiar proper nouns such as a person, pet, place or holiday name, wherever the name appears. Ordinary words do not all need capitals.

“sam visits canberra on australia day.” The supplied names are Sam, Canberra and Australia Day.

Try it · Write the sentence with the supplied names capitalised.

Sam visits Canberra on Australia Day. Sam also starts the sentence. The common words visits and on stay lower case; Australia Day is the supplied two-word holiday name.

3. Edit, write and read back

Check complete ideas and boundaries, then sentence starts, I, names and end marks. Read the result aloud to see whether it communicates the intended meaning.

“i have a book can i read it” is a calm statement followed by a question.

Try it · Edit it, then create a different statement and question of your own.

I have a book. Can I read it? For the fresh pair, accept complete meaningful sentences with a suitable full stop and question mark, correct starts, I and any names. Note support; choosing a correction is not the same as writing independently.

Common mistakes

“Every spoken pause needs a full stop.” Check whole sentence boundaries and meaning.

“Every command needs !.” A calm “Wait here.” can use a full stop; urgency may support “Wait here!”.

“Only names at the start need capitals.” Particular names need capitals inside sentences too.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

A tiny note with two purposes

Write or dictate a calm statement and direct question about a familiar object. Include one familiar supplied name. Check capitals and endings, then read both to a partner, noticing the boundaries and intended expression.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose . or ? for supplied clear contexts. Core: write or dictate a statement and a question with capitals. Stretch: compare Wait here. with Wait here! and explain contexts that suit each, then edit a fresh two-sentence note.

An adult can provide the exact spelling of names. Check punctuation and capitals separately from handwriting. Listen to actual reading when assessing pauses and expression; print does not demonstrate oral performance.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “I asked for a pencil.” a direct question?

No. It reports asking, so a full stop can fit this calm statement.

2. Must ordinary season words have capitals inside a sentence?

No. Names of days, months and holidays take capitals; an ordinary word such as summer usually stays lower case.

3. Can the same words use . or ! in different contexts?

Yes. “Wait here.” can be calm, while “Wait here!” can be urgent. Supply the context before demanding one answer.

FAQ

Does a rising voice always prove a question?

No. Use the words and intended message as well as actual reading. Voice patterns can vary.

What should an adult check in a written answer?

Check the complete ideas, suitable boundaries and end marks, sentence starts, I and familiar names. Record any dictated or supplied support.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: Help children use full stops, question marks, exclamation marks and capital letters to make a short message clear.

Useful words: Punctuation marks help readers follow a message. A full stop ends a telling sentence. A question mark ends a direct question. A capital letter is an upper-case letter.

1. Sentences need end punctuation

A complete sentence tells a whole idea. A capital letter marks its start. An end mark helps show where it finishes.

Example

“The cat rests. It is warm.” has two whole ideas and two full stops.

Try it: Find the two sentence endings: “We pack our bags. We walk to school.”

See answer and explanation

The full stops come after bags and school. Read each whole idea, then pause at its end; do not put a full stop after every spoken pause.

2. Full stops for statements

A statement tells something. It can give information or an opinion. A calm statement usually ends with a full stop.

Example

“The book is open.” gives information. “I like this book.” gives a view. Both are statements.

Try it: Add the usual end mark to this calm statement: “Our rabbit is called Moss”

See answer and explanation

Our rabbit is called Moss. It tells something, so a full stop fits this calm message.

3. Question marks for questions

A direct question asks for an answer. Put a question mark at its end. Say what you need to find out.

Example

“Where is the hat?” asks for a place. “Can I join the game?” asks for permission.

Try it: Which asks a question: “The bag is here” or “Is the bag here”? Add its end mark.

See answer and explanation

Is the bag here? The other sentence tells information. Listen to meaning, rather than deciding only from how a voice rises or falls.

4. Exclamation marks

An exclamation mark can show a strong feeling, surprise or an urgent warning. Meaning and context help us decide between a full stop and an exclamation mark.

Example

“We won!” shows excitement. “Move back!” can show an urgent warning. A calm instruction such as “Open your book.” can end with a full stop.

Try it: A child cheers with great excitement: “I won the race”. Which end mark makes that excitement clear?

See answer and explanation

I won the race! The stated excitement supports the exclamation mark. An instruction is not automatically an exclamation; discuss the intended message.

5. Capital letters for proper nouns

A proper noun names a particular person, pet or place. Names of days, months and familiar holidays also begin with capitals. Use a capital for a name wherever it appears.

Example

“On Friday, Mia visits Canberra.” Friday, Mia and Canberra begin with capitals. The pronoun I is also always a capital.

Try it: Fix the capitals: “on tuesday, i visit sam in july.”

See answer and explanation

On Tuesday, I visit Sam in July. On starts the sentence; Tuesday, Sam and July are names; I is always a capital. Do not put capitals on every ordinary word.

6. Read, choose and edit punctuation

Read the whole message. Decide whether it tells, asks or shows strong feeling. Check the first word, special names and each ending.

Example

“the kite is red where is ben” becomes “The kite is red. Where is Ben?” We separate the telling idea from the question.

Try it: Edit this message: “mia has a book can i read it”

See answer and explanation

Mia has a book. Can I read it? The first idea tells information and the second asks permission. Capitalise Mia, Can and I; keep lower-case letters for ordinary words inside the sentences.

See the idea: Let the message guide the end mark

Let the message guide the end mark

Work through the example

  1. A full stop ends this telling sentence.
  2. A question mark shows that the second sentence asks a question.
  3. An exclamation mark can show a strong reaction. A calm command can still end with a full stop.

Watch for this: An exclamation mark alone does not tell you whether the message is a command or a reaction.

Try it together

Fix this question: “where is sam”

Answer and teaching guidance

“Where is Sam?” Start the sentence and the name with capitals; finish with a question mark.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A command can use a full stop or an exclamation mark. A statement can express an opinion. The last mark must fit the intended message; it does not depend only on a loud voice.

Check: Write or dictate one calm statement and one direct question about a familiar object. Add capitals and end marks, then read both aloud.

Check a whole idea, suitable . and ?, a capital first word and any names. Listen to the actual reading for awareness of the sentence boundaries; a paper score alone does not show reading performance.
