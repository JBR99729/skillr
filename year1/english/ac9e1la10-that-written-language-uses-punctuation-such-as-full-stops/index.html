Key concept

Learning goal: I can use sentence punctuation and capital letters to make written meaning clear.

  • Pause and change your voice when punctuation tells you to.
  • Recognise full stops, question marks and exclamation marks.
  • Choose punctuation that matches a statement or question.
  • Use capital letters for familiar place names and holidays.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: using intonation and pauses in response to punctuation, when reading
  • E2: identifying different sentence boundary punctuation, when reading
  • E3: writing different types of sentences; for example, statements and questions, and discussing appropriate punctuation
  • E4: identifying and using capital letters to name places and holidays
Common misconceptions
  • Question with full stop — If it asks, use a question mark.
  • Capital for every word — Use capitals for sentence starts and proper nouns.
  • Exclamation everywhere — Use exclamation marks for strong feeling or emphasis.
Try these
  1. End mark sort — Sort sentences by punctuation needed.
    Where are you / I am here / Wow
  2. Name hunt — Find proper nouns that need capitals.
    Sam / Friday / Canberra
  3. Fix the sentence — Rewrite a sentence with capitals and punctuation.
    mia ran home
Explain and apply

Choose the end mark

Which mark ends Where is my hat?

Answer and teaching guidance

A question mark ends a direct question. Read the sentence purpose before choosing the mark.

Capitalise proper nouns

Repair We visit the park on tuesday.

Answer and teaching guidance

Tuesday needs a capital letter because it is the name of a day: We visit the park on Tuesday.

Name needs a capital

A dog's name is patch. How should the name be written in a sentence?

Answer and teaching guidance

Patch begins with a capital because it is a proper noun, even though dog does not.

Explain the job

Why does Stop! use an exclamation mark?

Answer and teaching guidance

The exclamation mark shows a strong feeling, warning or urgent command. It is chosen for meaning, not decoration.

Worked example and reasoning checks

Let punctuation show the message

Choose end punctuation and use capitals for familiar names.

Worked example

Write ‘where is ruby’ as a question about a child named Ruby.

  1. The message asks for information, so it needs a question mark.
  2. Start the sentence with a capital W. Ruby is a person's name and needs a capital R.
  3. Write ‘Where is Ruby?’ Read it aloud as a question.

Watch for this mistake: Not every excited voice needs an exclamation mark. Choose punctuation to match the written message and capitalise people's names wherever they appear.

Try it, then explain

  1. Add capitals and end punctuation: ‘mia has a blue bag’.

    Hint

    This is a statement, and Mia is a name.

    Answer and explanation

    Mia has a blue bag.

  2. Which ending fits ‘Can you help me’ — a full stop or a question mark? Explain.

    Hint

    Is the writer asking something?

    Answer and explanation

    A question mark: ‘Can you help me?’ asks a question.
