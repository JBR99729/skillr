YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Build word families from meaningful parts AC9E1LY15

Learning intention: I can find a base word and use endings to change how it works in a sentence.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A shared base carries shared meaning

Play, plays, played and playing retain the meaningful base play. “I played yesterday” uses ed for a past action. “I am playing now” uses am with playing for the ongoing action now. “I was playing yesterday” shows that ing alone does not always mean now. Check the whole sentence.

Learn the skill

1. Find a meaningful base

A base carries the central meaning. Endings can change number or how a verb fits its sentence. Similar rhyme or starting letters do not prove a family.

Help, helps, helped and helping share help. Yelp may sound similar but does not contain that meaningful base.

Try it · Find the base in jumping and jumped. Does bump belong to this same base family?

Jump is the shared base. Bump rhymes but has a different base and meaning. Read both complete family words, not just the shared letters.

2. Let the sentence guide the ending

Noun s can show more than one; verb s can fit a singular subject. Ed often marks a regular past form. Ing works with other words to express an ongoing action.

“The cats run” has plural cats. “The cat runs” uses runs for one cat. “We walked yesterday” uses walked for the past.

Try it · Complete “The dog is ___ now” using bark. Then complete “The dog ___ yesterday.”

Barking fits the first sentence; barked fits the second. Accept the complete forms in their sentences. Do not treat every s as plural or every ing word as present time.

3. Read and build the family

Find the base, notice an ending and blend the whole word. Some endings sound different across words, and some spellings change. Learn useful examples without inventing an always-rule.

Look + ed gives looked. Play + ed gives played. Ed is not always an extra spoken syllable. Playground joins meaningful play and ground; it is a related compound, not a grammatical ending.

Try it · Build three forms from wash and use one in a sentence.

Washes, washed and washing are useful forms. For example, “I washed my hands yesterday.” Accept another grammatical sentence. Explain the base wash and the ending used; teach any unfamiliar ending spelling.

Common mistakes

“Word families are just rhymes.” Look for a shared meaningful base.

“Every s means many.” Compare noun cats with verb runs.

“Ing always means now.” Compare am playing now with was playing yesterday.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build, read and use a family

Write help on a card and s, ed and ing on separate cards. Build helps, helped and helping, read each complete word and use it in a sentence. Compare “She helps each day”, “She helped yesterday” and “She is helping now”. Then try a different familiar base.

Adjust the challenge Starter: identify the shared base with an adult. Core: build endings and use them in complete sentences. Stretch: compare a plural noun with a singular verb ending, or notice a familiar compound such as playground.

Assess meaning and use, not only colour matching or card placement. Observe actual reading when checking reading. Spelling changes such as clapped/clapping need explicit teaching; do not teach “just add” as a universal rule.

Three tricky questions

1. Do look and book form this kind of word family?

No. They rhyme but do not share the same meaningful base.

2. Does runs mean more than one runner?

No. In “The cat runs”, s makes the verb fit one cat.

3. Does walked need a separate ed syllable?

Not in usual pronunciation. Recognise walk and ed, then read the whole word naturally.

FAQ

Can related words use more than an ending?

Yes. Playground combines play and ground; distinguish a compound from grammatical endings.

Must we teach every spelling change at once?

No. Begin with familiar regular families and explicitly teach useful changes as needed.

Key concept

Learning goal: I can use morphemes and base words to build and read word families.

Work through the example

  1. Jump is the shared meaningful base in these words.
  2. In “Sam jumped”, ed helps show the action happened before now.
  3. In “Sam is jumping”, ing is used with is to describe the action happening. Other endings have other jobs.

Watch for this: A word family shares a meaningful base; rhyming words need not belong to the same family.

Try it together

Choose the word for “Yesterday Sam ___”: jump, jumped or jumping.

Answer and teaching guidance

Jumped: “Yesterday Sam jumped.” Explain how yesterday and the ending fit the meaning.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Ending has no meaning — Explain what the morpheme changes.
  • Base word lost — Find the base word first.
  • Word family as rhymes — Word families here are about shared meaning parts.
Try these
  1. Word family build — Start with a base word and add endings.
    jump → jumps/jumped/jumping
  2. Meaning match — Match an ending to its meaning.
    -ed → happened before
  3. Sentence test — Use two words from the same family in sentences.
    I play. I played.
Explain and apply

Add an ending

Add -ing to play. What word do you make?

Answer and teaching guidance

playing is made from the base word play plus the ending -ing.

Show one action now

Complete “I am ___ now”: help, helping or helps.

Answer and teaching guidance

Helping fits “I am helping now.” The whole sentence places the action now. The ending -ing alone does not always mean now; compare “I was helping yesterday.”

Find the base word

What is the base word in reading?

Answer and teaching guidance

read is the base word. The ending -ing has been added to make reading.

Make a word family

List three words in the play family.

Answer and teaching guidance

play, plays and playing belong to the same word family because they share the base word play.
