YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Compare what images add to a text AC9E1LA08

Learning intention: I can point to visible image clues and explain how they help a story, diagram or sequence.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The same subject, different jobs

Both supplied pictures show a duck. The careful drawing shows its shape; the cartoon adds a scarf and smile to create a playful character. The difference is useful for comparing an information picture with a story illustration. Neither style is automatically best for every purpose.

Learn the skill

1. Compare visible details and purpose

Start with what is actually shown, then explain how it helps the text. A drawing is not a photograph. A cartoon’s added features can help create a character.

Use the duck comparison directly below: a careful drawing beside a scarf-wearing cartoon.

Try it · Which suits a playful duck story? Give one visible reason.

The cartoon’s scarf and smile help create a playful character. The careful drawing can help show a real duck’s shape. Explain the purpose and clue rather than choose a favourite style.

2. Read words and pictures together

Words and images can share meaning or contribute different details. A mismatch may be important, so check both rather than ignore one.

Story words: “The room was empty.” The supplied picture shows a kitten under a chair.

Try it · What does the image add or make you question?

An animal is still in the room. The reader should notice the mismatch with empty and consider what the words mean in the story. The image alone does not provide the kitten’s name or the time.

3. Use labels and ordered stages

A label line connects a name with a visible part. A sequence shows selected stages in order. Compare the job of each image.

Use the supplied labelled flower and First–Next–Last plant sequence directly below.

Try it · Which image identifies the leaf? Which shows a change over stages?

The labelled flower links leaf to that part. The sequence shows the seed becoming a small plant and then a taller flowering plant. It does not give an exact number of days.

Common mistakes

“The picture proves every hidden feeling.” Point to the clue and keep uncertain interpretations tentative.

“A larger drawing proves a larger real object.” Printed size may be a design choice; look for actual size information.

“Every image serves the same job.” Compare character, location, named-part, sequence or invitation meanings.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One subject, three image jobs

Use the supplied retained duck story, information picture and story-time poster. Read their headings and point to a detail supporting each job. With an adult, create a simple diagram of a familiar object with two accurate labels.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose between two stated image jobs with the pictures visible. Core: compare two images and cite visible details. Stretch: explain one meaning supplied by words and a different meaning supplied by an image, including a supported mismatch.

All comparison images are supplied in their teaching sections. Cultural-account work needs the actual attributed source and its guidance; do not infer a cultural symbol’s meaning or imitate the artwork. An adult may read captions.

Three tricky questions

1. Does a picture show everything outside its frame?

No. Explain what is visible and keep claims about unseen people, objects or events uncertain.

2. Is the careful duck drawing a photograph?

No. It is a drawing. Compare its useful detail with the cartoon without changing its medium label.

3. Does the plant sequence tell exactly how long growth took?

No. It shows selected stages. Exact elapsed time would need additional information.

FAQ

Can we explore a First Nations Australian artwork?

An adult can supply the National Museum of Australia’s actual Holiday Time by Eunice Yunurupa Porter and its Year 1 digital flip book and teacher guidance. Follow cultural notices, use the artist’s account and distinguish visible detail from meanings you have not been taught.

What makes an answer strong?

It names a visible clue, connects it with the text’s job and avoids unsupported details. Different interpretations may be valid when the evidence supports them.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: Image means picture. A diagram helps explain information. A label names a part. A sequence shows pictures in order.

1. Look closely before guessing

An image can show a place, an action or a detail. Point to the clue you can see. A clue may suggest a feeling, but it does not prove everything about a character.

Example

The two signs in the worked example both say “This way”. Their arrows show different directions.

Try it: Look at the signs here. Which visible clue tells you to go right?

See answer and explanation

The arrow pointing right. The words alone do not name a direction.

2. Compare pictures of the same subject

Ask what is the same and what changes. A cartoon can make a duck into a story character; a careful drawing can help us see a real duck’s shape. Neither kind is always the best: it depends on the job.

Example

Both worksheet pictures show a duck. The careful drawing shows its shape; the cartoon adds a scarf and smile.

Try it: Which picture suits a playful duck story? Give a visible clue.

See answer and explanation

The cartoon. Its scarf and smile help create a playful character. The careful drawing helps readers notice a real duck’s shape.

3. Read the words and picture together

Words can name an event while a picture supplies another detail. Look for what matches, what is added and any mismatch.

Example

A story says, “The room was empty”, but its illustration shows a kitten under a chair. The picture changes what the reader understands.

Try it: What does the kitten picture add to those words?

See answer and explanation

An animal is still there. The words and image do not match completely; a reader should notice and check, rather than ignore either.

4. Explain what a diagram or sign adds

Labels name parts. Arrows can show a direction or connect a label to a part. Explain the information the image contributes, not only its colours.

Example

This worksheet diagram shows where the leaf and stem are. Its label lines connect each name to a visible part.

Try it: In this diagram, what can you learn from the leaf label and line?

See answer and explanation

They identify the leaf and show where it is attached beside the stem. The image helps connect the name to the part.

5. Follow a sequence of pictures

Read the images in order. Notice what is there first, what changes and what happens next. Use first, next and last to explain the sequence.

Example

The pictures below show a seed in a pot, a small plant, then a taller plant with a flower.

Try it: Which picture, First, Next or Last, comes last? What has changed since the first?

See answer and explanation

The picture labelled Last. The seed has grown into a taller plant with a flower. The sequence shows change; it does not tell us exactly how many days passed.

6. Match the image to the text’s job

A story can show a character or event. An information text can name parts. An instruction can show steps. Compare how each helps its reader.

Example

The worksheet shows three duck pictures: a story character, an information picture and a story-time poster. Read each heading as well as its picture.

Try it: Which picture helps you learn what a real duck looks like? Which invites you to story time?

See answer and explanation

The middle picture, ‘Look at a duck’, shows the shape and body parts of a real duck. The poster, ‘Join duck story time!’, invites you to an event. The cartoon fits a story character. The words help establish each picture’s job.

Try a worked example together

An image can show information words leave out

Work through the example

  1. Both signs say “This way”. The arrows point in different directions.
  2. The words alone do not name the direction. The image adds that information.
  3. In other texts, an image may show a setting, an action or a labelled feature. Explain the clue you can actually see.

Watch for this: An image supplies clues; it cannot establish every hidden feeling or cause.

Try it together

Which sign would guide you to the right? What does its image add?

Answer and teaching guidance

The right-arrow sign. Its image shows which direction “this way” means.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Ask “What can you see?” before “What might it mean?” Accept different supported interpretations. A smiling picture does not guarantee a feeling, and a larger printed picture does not prove a larger real object.

Help: Read captions aloud, compare two visible details and accept spoken, pointed or drawn responses.

Explore together: An adult may choose the National Museum of Australia’s published Year 1 resources for Holiday Time by Eunice Yunurupa Porter, with the museum’s teacher guide and digital flip book. Follow the publisher’s cultural guidance and notices. Discuss visible objects and actions using its account; do not guess meanings of cultural symbols or copy the artwork.

Prepare the cultural-account activity

Adult: open the named museum resource, read its cultural notices and attribution, and supply the actual artwork, digital flip book and relevant teacher explanation before discussion. Ask what is visibly shown and what the artist’s published account adds. Do not guess the meaning of a cultural symbol or reproduce the artwork. If the actual resource is unavailable, defer its source-specific questions.
