Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Look, compare and explain before moving on.

Useful words: Image means picture. A diagram helps explain information. A label names a part. A sequence shows pictures in order.

1. Look closely before guessing

An image can show a place, an action or a detail. Point to the clue you can see. A clue may suggest a feeling, but it does not prove everything about a character.

Example

The two signs in the worked example both say “This way”. Their arrows show different directions.

Try it: Look at the signs here. Which visible clue tells you to go right?

See answer and explanation

The arrow pointing right. The words alone do not name a direction.

2. Compare pictures of the same subject

Ask what is the same and what changes. A cartoon can make a duck into a story character; a careful drawing can help us see a real duck’s shape. Neither kind is always the best: it depends on the job.

Example

Both worksheet pictures show a duck. The careful drawing shows its shape; the cartoon adds a scarf and smile.

Try it: Which picture suits a playful duck story? Give a visible clue.

See answer and explanation

The cartoon. Its scarf and smile help create a playful character. The careful drawing helps readers notice a real duck’s shape.

3. Read the words and picture together

Words can name an event while a picture supplies another detail. Look for what matches, what is added and any mismatch.

Example

A story says, “The room was empty”, but its illustration shows a kitten under a chair. The picture changes what the reader understands.

Try it: What does the kitten picture add to those words?

See answer and explanation

An animal is still there. The words and image do not match completely; a reader should notice and check, rather than ignore either.

4. Explain what a diagram or sign adds

Labels name parts. Arrows can show a direction or connect a label to a part. Explain the information the image contributes, not only its colours.

Example

This worksheet diagram shows where the leaf and stem are. Its label lines connect each name to a visible part.

Try it: In this diagram, what can you learn from the leaf label and line?

See answer and explanation

They identify the leaf and show where it is attached beside the stem. The image helps connect the name to the part.

5. Follow a sequence of pictures

Read the images in order. Notice what is there first, what changes and what happens next. Use first, next and last to explain the sequence.

Example

The pictures below show a seed in a pot, a small plant, then a taller plant with a flower.

Try it: Which picture, First, Next or Last, comes last? What has changed since the first?

See answer and explanation

The picture labelled Last. The seed has grown into a taller plant with a flower. The sequence shows change; it does not tell us exactly how many days passed.

6. Match the image to the text’s job

A story can show a character or event. An information text can name parts. An instruction can show steps. Compare how each helps its reader.

Example

The worksheet shows three duck pictures: a story character, an information picture and a story-time poster. Read each heading as well as its picture.

Try it: Which picture helps you learn what a real duck looks like? Which invites you to story time?

See answer and explanation

The middle picture, ‘Look at a duck’, shows the shape and body parts of a real duck. The poster, ‘Join duck story time!’, invites you to an event. The cartoon fits a story character. The words help establish each picture’s job.

Try a worked example together

An image can show information words leave out

Work through the example

  1. Both signs say “This way”. The arrows point in different directions.
  2. The words alone do not name the direction. The image adds that information.
  3. In other texts, an image may show a setting, an action or a labelled feature. Explain the clue you can actually see.

Watch for this: An image supplies clues; it cannot establish every hidden feeling or cause.

Try it together

Which sign would guide you to the right? What does its image add?

Answer and teaching guidance

The right-arrow sign. Its image shows which direction “this way” means.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Ask “What can you see?” before “What might it mean?” Accept different supported interpretations. A smiling picture does not guarantee a feeling, and a larger printed picture does not prove a larger real object.

Help: Read captions aloud, compare two visible details and accept spoken, pointed or drawn responses.

Explore together: An adult may choose the National Museum of Australia’s published Year 1 Holiday Time resources and teacher guide by the National Museum of Australia. Follow the publisher’s cultural guidance and notices. Discuss visible objects and actions using its account; do not guess meanings of cultural symbols or copy the artwork.

Use this guide with your worksheet pack

Look at the actual images: Choose the matching comparison, diagram or sequence focus. Point to visible details before answering the paper activities.

  1. Model one example from the relevant focus.
  2. Try an example together and ask for an explanation.
  3. Use the matching worksheet focus, then revisit the guide where needed.
  4. Try the separate whole-code paper test after the taught focuses.

What to look for: Look for what the image contributes to meaning, not a preferred art style. Use the supplied image; do not expect knowledge of an unseen picture.

Keep the learner’s own responses. Note whether they worked independently or needed help, then choose the next focus or revisit the same skill. Adjust the pace to the learner.
