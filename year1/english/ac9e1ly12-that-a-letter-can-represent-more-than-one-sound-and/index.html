YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Compare letter sounds and hear syllables AC9E1LY12

Learning intention: I can notice different letter–sound links and hear the vowel sound in each syllable.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Count spoken parts, not written vowels

Rain has two vowel letters, ai, representing one vowel sound here: one syllable. Sunset has two spoken parts, sun and set, each with a vowel sound: two syllables. Listen to the word naturally before marking its parts. The model shows these particular words, not a rule that every vowel pair makes one sound.

Learn the skill

1. One letter can represent different sounds

Compare the actual sounds in familiar words. The same letter does not always represent the same sound.

Listen to adult models cut, put and use. The letter u represents different vowel sounds in these words. Also compare a in cat, father and any.

Try it · Hear cat and any. Does their written a represent the same vowel sound?

No in usual Australian pronunciation: cat has short a; any begins with a vowel like short e. Accept local accent variation and model the intended words. Do not change their spellings to match a guessed rule.

2. One sound can use different letters

A sound can be represented by different letters or groups. Look at the complete word and use what you have learned about that word.

Sat and cent both begin with the /s/ sound in usual pronunciation, written s and c. Go and gem use g for different initial sounds.

Try it · Read or hear sat and cent, then point to the letter for their shared beginning sound.

S in sat and c in cent both represent /s/. Identify the actual letter in each word; do not claim c always has that sound.

3. Hear the vowel sound in each syllable

A syllable is a spoken part containing a vowel sound. Say the whole word, then tap its parts. Each part must still fit the word you heard.

Sun: one spoken part. Robot: ro–bot, two. Cake: one, despite two written vowel letters.

Try it · Say raincoat naturally, then tap its parts. What vowel sounds can you hear?

Rain–coat: two parts, with long a in rain and long o in coat. Four written vowel letters do not make four syllables. Use familiar pronunciation rather than adding spoken sounds for silent letters.

Common mistakes

“A letter has only one sound.” Compare its use in different actual words.

“Count every written vowel to count syllables.” Listen for vowel sounds in spoken parts.

“Every vowel pair works alike.” Check the particular word and taught pattern.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Listen, compare and tap

Adult models cat/any, cut/put/use and sat/cent. Discuss which sound–letter link changes. Then child says sun, cake, robot and raincoat, tapping their spoken parts and hearing a vowel sound in each.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare two adult-modelled words. Core: explain a sound–letter difference and tap one- and two-syllable words. Stretch: explain why rain and raincoat need different syllable counts.

Adult models support listening; they are not independent reading evidence. Accept ordinary accent variation. Choose clear familiar words instead of forcing a pronunciation to fit the printed letters.

Three tricky questions

1. Does rain have two syllables?

No. In usual pronunciation it has one vowel sound and one syllable.

2. Does c always represent /s/?

No. Compare cent with cat.

3. Can a spoken syllable have no vowel sound?

For these taught Year 1 words, listen for the vowel sound that gives each syllable its spoken beat. Avoid treating silent written letters as spoken syllables.

FAQ

Must the child use technical sound symbols?

No. Saying and comparing the sounds is enough; an adult can explain the terms.

Is a clap the same as a phoneme counter?

No. A clap here represents a syllable; a sound counter represents one phoneme.

Key concept

Learning goal: I can recognise that letters can represent different sounds and every syllable has a vowel sound.

  • Recognise that one letter can represent different sounds in different words.
  • Recognise that the same sound can be represented by different letters.
  • Remember that every syllable contains a vowel sound.

Work through the example

  1. Say sunset naturally, then clap sun and set: two syllables.
  2. Listen for a vowel sound in each part.
  3. A vowel letter can represent different sounds in different words. Count what you hear, not only the written letters.

Watch for this: Two written vowel letters may represent one vowel sound, as ai does in rain.

Try it together

Say robot. How many syllables do you hear?

Answer and teaching guidance

Two: ro-bot. Accept the usual pronunciation in the child’s accent.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Letter has only one sound — Some letters represent different sounds in different words.
  • Counting written vowels only — Listen for vowel sounds.
  • Syllable without vowel — Every syllable needs a vowel sound.

Explain and apply

Count vowel sounds

Clap cat. How many syllables do you hear?

Answer and teaching guidance

cat has one syllable because it has one vowel sound.

Letter, two sounds

Compare gem and go. What changes about the letter g?

Answer and teaching guidance

The letter g can represent a soft sound in gem and a hard sound in go.

Clap two parts

Clap sunset. How many syllables do you hear?

Answer and teaching guidance

sunset has two syllables: sun-set. Each syllable has a vowel sound.

Sort by vowel sound

Sort these words: cat, cake, bed, he. Which words have a short vowel sound?

Answer and teaching guidance

cat and bed have short vowel sounds. cake and he have long vowel sounds.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E1LY12: understand that a letter can represent more than one sound and that a syllable must contain a vowel sound

  • recognising that letters can have more than one sound; for example, the letter “u” in “cut”, “put”, “use” and the letter “a” in “cat”, “father”, “any”
  • recognising sounds that can be produced by different letters; for example, the “s” sound in “sat” and “cent”
