YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Explain what you prefer and why AC9E1LA02

Learning intention: I can name a like, dislike or preference and give a reason that explains it.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A choice with a connected reason

Nia says, “I prefer building because I enjoy making tall towers.” Building is the choice; enjoying towers explains it. Eli can prefer drawing for a different reason. Neither person has to change a genuine preference to match the other.

Learn the skill

1. Name the choice

Say what you like or dislike, or which option you prefer. A preference belongs to the person expressing it. Another person may choose differently.

Options: drawing a picture / building a model.

Try it · Which do you prefer, or would you choose something else?

Accept a genuine named choice, neither option or another activity. There is no single correct personal favourite. Ask the child to make the choice clear.

2. Connect a relevant reason

Because can connect a choice with its explanation. Check whether the reason says something useful about that choice, rather than merely repeating it.

“I like this story because the ending makes me laugh.” / “I like this story because I like it.”

Try it · Which gives more information about the choice?

The funny ending gives a specific reason. “Because I like it” repeats the preference. Ask neutrally what the person likes about it; do not supply their taste for them.

3. Compare for a stated purpose

A comparison can explain a choice. Better needs a purpose: better for which job? A practical comparison does not prove everyone has the same favourite.

Tray A holds six crayons. Pot B holds two. The job is carrying all six crayons in one container.

Try it · Which is better for this job? Give a reason with because.

Tray A is better for this job because it holds all six crayons. Someone could prefer Pot B for carrying just one or two. Use the supplied capacity facts, not an unseen size guess.

Common mistakes

“My favourite must be everyone’s favourite.” Different connected reasons can support different choices.

“Because I like it explains the feature.” Ask what about the choice the person likes.

“Better means best for every job.” State the job and compare the relevant feature.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Choose, explain, listen

Choose between two familiar activities. Tell a partner your preference and one connected reason. Listen to their choice, repeat what you heard and give your own view respectfully.

Adjust the challenge Starter: a choice and the frame “I prefer ___ because ___”. Core: a clear choice and relevant reason. Stretch: explain why two people can reasonably choose differently or why a different job changes the practical choice.

Accept personal tastes without marking them wrong. Supply actual experiences when a reason depends on sound, taste or comfort; a picture cannot establish those sensations.

Three tricky questions

1. Does because automatically make a reason relevant?

No. “I like the puzzle because the bus is late” does not explain this puzzle preference without another connection.

2. Can someone dislike a food you enjoy?

Yes. Listen to their reason and share yours without requiring agreement.

3. Must a comparison word end in -er?

No. Better compares too. Explain the feature or purpose rather than using a spelling rule.

FAQ

Can the reason be spoken?

Yes. An adult can scribe the learner’s own words. Check the explanation separately from spelling.

What if a reason is unclear?

Ask an open, neutral question such as “What do you like about it?” Then let the learner add a relevant detail.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: Like means enjoy. Dislike means not enjoy. Prefer means choose one thing over another. A reason tells why.

1. Likes and dislikes

Name the thing you like or dislike. Different people can have different views.

Example

“I like drawing.” “I dislike very loud music.” Neither person has to choose the same favourite.

Try it: Choose reading or building. Say which you like.

See answer and explanation

Accept either choice, another genuine preference or not liking either. The child names the choice clearly.

2. Give a reason with because

Use because to connect your choice to a reason. Tell what you like about it.

Example

“I like drawing because I can make my own pictures.” The reason explains the choice.

Try it: Finish: “I like this game because …”

See answer and explanation

Accept a relevant reason, such as “we take turns”. If the child says only “because I like it”, ask neutrally, “What do you like about it?”

3. Prefer one of two choices

Say which you prefer and why. A preference is a personal choice, not a fact everyone must agree with.

Example

“I prefer the quiet corner because I can hear the story.”

Try it: Two books are offered. One has funny animals; one shows how to make a kite. Which would you prefer, and why?

See answer and explanation

Accept either with a connected reason. The supplied descriptions give enough information; no unseen book knowledge is required.

4. Use comparison words

Compare a feature of two things when it helps explain your choice. Better needs a reason: better for what?

Example

“I prefer the bigger box because it holds all the blocks.” Bigger compares the boxes; holding the blocks explains the choice.

Try it: A wide tray holds all six crayons. A small pot holds two. Which is better for carrying all six, and why?

See answer and explanation

The tray is better for this stated job because it holds all six. This practical reason does not prove it is everyone’s favourite.

5. Make the reason clear

Check that the reason matches the choice. Add one useful detail instead of repeating the same words.

Example

“I prefer this hat because its wide brim shades my face.” The detail explains the preference.

Try it: Does “I like the puzzle because the bus is late” explain the puzzle choice? Repair it.

See answer and explanation

The reason is unrelated. For example, “I like the puzzle because I enjoy finding the pieces.” Accept another relevant reason.

6. Listen and respond

Say what you heard before agreeing or giving a different view. Disagree with the choice respectfully.

Example

“You prefer painting. I prefer building because I like making towers.”

Try it: A partner likes running games. You prefer quiet games. What could you say?

See answer and explanation

For example, “You like running games. I prefer quiet games because I can think about my next move.” Accept a relevant respectful response; agreement is not required.

Try a worked example together

Connect a preference to its reason

Work through the example

  1. Say which choice you prefer.
  2. Use because to connect the choice to a reason.
  3. A different person may prefer something else and give a different reason. Listen respectfully.

Watch for this: Repeating “because I like it” does not explain what you like about it.

Try it together

Tell a partner which book you prefer and give a reason.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept a clear preference with a relevant reason, such as liking its funny characters. There is no single correct preference.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Books supplied for the preference try

Use these complete descriptions: Book A has funny animal stories. Book B shows steps for making a kite. Choose either and explain a relevant preference. Alternatively an adult supplies two actual familiar books and lets the learner inspect them before choosing.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A comparison word describes a difference that can help explain a preference; it does not automatically show a stronger feeling. Better is a useful comparison word even though it does not end in -er. Do not judge a personal favourite as wrong or infer sound, taste or comfort from a picture alone.

Check: Choose between two familiar activities. Give your preference and one connected reason. Then listen to someone with a different preference.

See answer and explanation

Look for a named choice and a relevant reason, with because or another clear link. In the exchange, the child acknowledges the other view. Mark the explanation separately from spelling.

Help: Provide two familiar choices and the incomplete frame “I prefer ___ because ___.” Use a small word bank such as quiet, fun, fits or soft only when it suits the choices.

Next challenge: Compare two objects for a stated job, then explain why someone choosing for a different job might prefer the other object.

A short rehearsal before paper practice

Offer drawing and building as two activities. Say your preference and a reason, then listen to your partner’s choice. You may prefer different activities.

Model responses and what to notice

For example: “I prefer drawing because I can make my own pictures.” A partner might say, “I prefer building because I like making towers.” Accept either activity, another genuine preference or not liking either, with a relevant explanation. A respectful reply might be, “You like drawing. I like building.” Agreement is not required.

Support: Use familiar objects or the pack’s picture choices. An adult can read the directions or scribe the learner’s own words. Do not infer taste, sound or comfort from a picture alone; provide the needed experience or context.

Keep a useful record: Save the choice and reason, and note any help given. Check the explanation separately from spelling and handwriting. Different personal preferences are valid; a “better for this job” comparison needs a stated purpose and evidence.
