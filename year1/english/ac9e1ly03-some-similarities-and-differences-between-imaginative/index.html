YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Compare story, information and persuasive texts AC9E1LY03

Learning intention: I can explain a similarity and a difference between texts and choose one for a purpose.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Three messages about the same subject

A: “The moon put a cloud in its pocket.” B: “The moon can be seen in the night sky.” C: “Look at the moon tonight; you may notice its shape.” A invents a human-like action, B gives information, and C encourages an action while also sharing information. Explain clues rather than sorting only by topic.

Learn the skill

1. Read for the main job

An imaginative text can tell an invented story, which may be realistic or fantastic. An informative text helps us learn. A persuasive text tries to influence a choice, belief or action.

A writer invents: “Jo lost a hat at the oval and found it by the gate.” An information card says: “A hat can shade your face.”

Try it · Must A be information because the events could happen? What useful information does B give?

No. A is explicitly an invented story with connected events. B explains a use of a hat. Possible events and real objects can appear in invented stories.

2. Compare what both include and what differs

Say something supported by both messages, then compare what they do. A fact can also support a request, so purposes can overlap.

A: “Plants need water.” B: “Please water our plants so they do not dry out.”

Try it · What do both concern? What does B ask the reader to do that A does not?

Both concern plants and water. B asks for watering and gives a reason. A shares information without that request. The shared topic does not make the whole messages identical.

3. Choose for a stated task

Use the complete text and actual image clues. Headings, photographs and story pictures can occur in different text types; no single feature always proves purpose.

A: “Wombats dig burrows in the ground.” B: “Wally the wombat packed an umbrella and sailed away.” C: “Please protect wombat homes so wombats have shelter.”

Try it · Which helps find out where wombats live? Which gives an invented event? Which asks for an action?

A gives burrow information, B follows a character’s imagined adventure, and C asks for protection with a reason. For the first task, choose A and point to “burrows in the ground”. Discuss the actual messages rather than deciding from the animal alone.

Common mistakes

“Everything possible is an information text.” Realistic events can be invented by a storyteller.

“Only stories can have pictures.” Compare the actual words and image purpose; all these text types can use images.

“A text can do only one job.” Explain its main job and any additional supported purpose.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare three complete cards

Adult writes three cards: “A fox packed a picnic and waved goodbye.” “Foxes have fur.” “Please close the picnic bin so animals cannot reach scraps.” Read them together. Discuss one shared topic connection, a difference in what each does and the best card for learning an animal fact.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult reads two cards and offers purpose words. Core: give a supported similarity and difference. Stretch: explain how an information detail can support persuasion or why a plausible event can still be invented.

Use complete actual texts and supplied pictures. Adult reading supports text comparison but is not independent reading evidence. Avoid long formal genre analysis or judging a family’s choices.

Three tricky questions

1. Does an animal in a text make it factual?

No. Check whether it shares information, follows invented events or encourages a choice.

2. Is a shared title enough to prove the same purpose?

No. Read the messages and compare what they do.

3. Can two different text types share a fact?

Yes. A persuasive request may use a fact as its reason. Explain both the shared content and the difference in purpose.

FAQ

Must imaginative writing include magic?

No. A writer can invent everyday events.

What counts as a useful comparison?

Name a specific shared feature or idea and a difference supported by both actual texts.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learn with short examples

These six teaching focuses help you compare imaginative, informative and persuasive texts. They are teaching sections within one curriculum code. An adult can read the short texts aloud while you concentrate on their meaning.

1. Recognise imaginative texts

An imaginative text can tell an invented story. It can include things that could happen, or fantasy things that could not happen. Look for a character and events, and use what you know about the whole text.

A realistic invented story

The writer made this story up: “Ari lost her hat at the oval. She searched beside the gate and found it.”

Work it through

Ari is a character. Losing, searching and finding are story events. The events could happen, but the writer invented them. A story does not need magic.

Try it together: A writer invents a story about a child catching a bus. Must it be an information report because buses are real?

See answer and teaching guidance

No. A real place or possible event can appear in an invented story. Use the writer’s context and the whole message, rather than calling every possible event a fact.

Watch for this: An impossible event is a useful fantasy clue. A possible event alone does not prove that a text gives factual information.

2. Recognise informative texts

An informative text helps us learn or find something out. A heading can name the topic, labels can name parts, and sentences can give facts or useful details. Check what the words actually explain.

Find the information you need

Card A: “Wombats dig burrows in the ground.” Card B: “Wally the wombat packed a bag and set off on a journey.”

Work it through

A gives information about real wombats. B follows a character’s story events. Both mention wombats, but only A answers where wombats make their homes.

Try it together: Which card would you choose to find out where wombats live? Point to the useful words.

See answer and teaching guidance

Choose A. “Burrows in the ground” answers the question. Accept an oral or supported explanation pointing to that detail.

Watch for this: A photograph or heading can be a clue, but it does not prove the text’s purpose. Stories and persuasive messages can also have photographs.

3. Recognise persuasive texts

A persuasive text tries to influence what someone chooses, thinks or does. Find the request or opinion, then a reason supporting it. Ask what the writer wants from the reader.

A request and its reason

“Choose the shady seat. It will keep our lunch out of the sun.”

Work it through

The request is to choose the shady seat. Keeping lunch out of the sun is the reason. The message encourages a choice; it does not tell a character’s adventure.

Try it together: What is the request? Which words explain why the reader might agree?

See answer and teaching guidance

Request: choose the shady seat. Reason: keep our lunch out of the sun. A suitable paraphrase is accepted.

Watch for this: “Please” alone is not proof of persuasion. Read the full message. Facts can support a persuasive request.

4. Find similarities

A similarity is something that is the same. Texts can share a topic, a word, a fact, an action or a picture while doing different jobs. Say what both texts actually include.

A shared fact, different jobs

A: “Birds have feathers.” B: “Please keep the bird’s feathers clean so it can stay healthy.”

Work it through

Both mention a bird’s feathers. A gives information; B asks for an action. Their shared topic does not make their purposes the same.

Try it together: A story says “Jo watered the garden.” A message says “Water the garden so the plants can grow.” What do both mention?

See answer and teaching guidance

Both mention watering a garden. One tells Jo’s event; the other encourages the reader to water. Do not claim both are instructions or both are stories.

Watch for this: Name a specific similarity supported by both texts. Do not guess a shared detail that appears in only one.

5. Explain differences

A difference is something that is not the same. Compare what the messages do, what their words tell us and how they organise ideas. Use “Both …, but …” to connect a similarity with a difference.

Same picture, different messages

Two cards show the same garden picture. A says “Plants need water.” B says “Please water our plants so they do not dry out.”

Work it through

The picture and topic are the same. A shares information. B uses information to support a request. The words reveal a purpose difference that the picture alone cannot show.

Try it together: What is the same? What does B ask the reader to do that A does not?

See answer and teaching guidance

Both concern plants and water. B asks the reader to water the plants. A gives information without that request. Accept a reasoned explanation of overlapping information and persuasion.

Watch for this: Do not use “It has a picture” as the difference when both cards have pictures. Check both messages.

6. Use evidence and choose a text

Evidence means a clue that supports your answer. Read the words, look at the pictures and check the whole message. Choose the text that helps with your task. A text can give information and encourage a choice.

Choose for your task

A: “The moon tucked a cloud into its pocket.” B: “The moon can be seen in the night sky.” C: “Look at the moon tonight; you may notice its shape.”

Work it through

For an invented story, choose A: the moon acts like a person. For a fact about the moon, B gives information. C encourages looking and also shares information. Explain the clue instead of forcing every text into one exclusive box.

Try it together: A text is titled “Our Garden”. What else must you read to decide whether it tells a story, explains facts or encourages a choice?

See answer and teaching guidance

Read the sentences and inspect the picture in context. The title names the topic but does not settle the purpose. Identify an event, useful information, a request or a reason as evidence.

Watch for this: A title, word or image alone can fit several purposes. Revise an initial guess when the full text gives stronger evidence.

Teaching help and a quick check

Teach, compare, try, check

  1. Explicit teaching and modelling: read three short texts on one topic. Name each main job and point to the words or image clues.
  2. Guided analysis: ask what both texts share, then how their purposes differ. Model “Both …, but …”.
  3. Collaborative or independent practice: compare a fresh pair; choose one for a stated task and explain the clue.
  4. Formative assessment: use the new pair below without giving the answer. Note the child’s comparison and the support used.

Quick check

A: “Spiders make silk.” B: “Leave this spider’s web in place; it helps catch insects.” Tell one similarity, one difference and which text encourages an action.

Expected evidence and next step

Both concern spiders. A gives information; B asks the reader to leave the web and supports the request with a reason. Accept other relevant shared clues. If the child sorts by topic alone, underline the shared topic first, then compare what each sentence does. Try another pair after modelling.

Support: read aloud, use two short texts, point to clues and accept oral, signed, AAC, drawn or scribed responses. Core: name a similarity and a purpose difference. Extend: explain how a factual detail supports a request, within familiar Year 1 contexts.

Keep evidence clear: compare understanding separately from decoding, spelling, handwriting and drawing. Record adult support; do not call a read-aloud response independent reading. Formal rhetoric and long written text analysis are outside this lesson’s Year 1 target.

Common misconceptions
  • All texts tell stories — Some texts inform or persuade instead.
  • Opinion confused with fact — Opinion can be agreed or disagreed with.
  • No comparison language — Use same, different, both, but.

Explain and apply

Real or imagined

Read: A dragon carried a lunchbox to school. Could this happen in real life?

Answer and teaching guidance

No. Dragons and the event make it imaginative.

Information or persuasion

Read: Bees make honey. Read: You should protect bees. How are the texts different?

Answer and teaching guidance

The first gives information. The second tries to persuade the reader to do something.

Same topic, different purpose

Two texts are about dogs. One tells dog facts and one says dogs make the best pets. Which one gives an opinion?

Answer and teaching guidance

Dogs make the best pets is an opinion and has a persuasive purpose.

Compare features

Name one feature of a story and one feature of an information text.

Answer and teaching guidance

A story often has characters and events. An information text often gives facts, labels or topic words.
