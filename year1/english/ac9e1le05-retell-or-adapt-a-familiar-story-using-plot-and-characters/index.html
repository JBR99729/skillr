YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Retell a story and make a new version AC9E1LE05

Learning intention: I can retell important events and deliberately adapt a familiar story while keeping it clear.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the links that explain the ending

Read this original story together until familiar: “Sam took a ball to the park. It rolled under a bench. Jo used a stick to roll it out. Sam thanked Jo, and they played.” A short retelling keeps the stuck ball and Jo’s help because they explain how play resumes. The exact wording can change.

Learn the skill

1. Keep the important sequence

Hear the whole story more than once, then choose events a listener needs. A retelling keeps its main meaning; it is more than a list of objects.

Original story: “Nell planted a seed. She watered the soil. A shoot appeared.” Read or hear it twice before retelling.

Try it · Tell the important events in order. Can the shoot appear first in an accurate retelling of this version?

Nell plants the seed, waters the soil, and a shoot appears. An accurate retelling keeps this supplied order. The learner tells it; recognising a correct order on paper is only support.

2. Make characters and language clear

Name who acts, keep pronouns clear and use story words or a little dialogue. A listener can follow the meaning without a memorised script.

Original story: “Sam could not reach his ball. Jo rolled it out with a stick. ‘Thank you, Jo,’ said Sam.”

Try it · Describe Jo using an action clue. Say Sam’s thanks in a way that fits the event.

Jo is helpful because Jo retrieves the ball. Accept a clear character description and a spoken, signed or accessible expression of thanks. Observe the actual retelling or role-play; spelling and accent are separate from the story meaning.

3. Adapt deliberately and check the links

Say what you will change. Keep enough plot, characters, language or structure for the familiar story to be recognisable, and adjust linked details so the new version makes sense.

Familiar version: a ball rolls under a park bench and Jo uses a stick to retrieve it. Planned adaptation: move the event to a classroom.

Try it · Tell an adapted version. Which objects or actions might need changing?

For example, the ball rolls under a desk and Jo uses a ruler to roll it out, then Sam gives thanks. Accept another coherent version. One change is a manageable starting point, not a rule limiting every adaptation.

Common mistakes

“Retelling means repeating every word.” Use your own clear words while preserving the important events.

“Any forgotten event is an adaptation.” Ask what changed on purpose and whether the new links work.

“A plan or selected answer proves oral retelling.” Listen to the learner tell or communicate the actual story.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Three pictures, then a fresh telling

Read the complete Sam and Jo story in the worked example together twice. The child draws three simple planning pictures, retells it to a listener, then announces one change and tells an adapted version. Compare what stayed and what changed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult models, then offers three event prompts. Core: retell connected events and a clear finish. Stretch: adapt a character or setting and explain the linked changes; add fitting dialogue or a short character description.

Drawing, role-play, signing, AAC, a scribe or an optional recording can support communication. Observe the actual targeted performance and note help. Use actual familiar texts; no special device or private experience is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Can you leave out the colour of the ball?

Usually, if that detail does not explain the important events in the chosen version.

2. Can you leave out the helper when the helper retrieves it?

That may break the explanation of the ending. Keep the event that connects the problem and result.

3. Must every object stay unchanged in an adaptation?

No. Deliberate changes can work when the version stays recognisable and the linked events make sense.

FAQ

Must it be written?

No. Oral telling is central; drawing, writing or digital tools can support it.

How familiar should the story be?

Read or hear it several times and discuss its meaning before expecting a retelling from memory or a plan.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: I can orally retell or adapt a familiar story using its characters, important events, story language and structure.

Start together: Read or hear a whole story several times. Make it familiar before asking the child to retell it. Read the short Sam and Jo story below together first, then revisit it across the focuses.

Useful words: Retell: tell a story again. Adapt: change part of a story. Character: who is in a story. Setting: where and when it happens. First, next, then, last help tell the order.

1. Choose key events

Listen to the whole story more than once until it is familiar. Choose the events someone needs to understand what happened. You can use your own words; you do not need to repeat every sentence.

Read together

Sam took a red ball to the park. The ball rolled under a bench. Sam could not reach it. His friend Jo used a stick to roll it out. Sam thanked Jo, and they played together.

Work it through

Sam took the ball to the park → the ball rolled under a bench → Jo helped get it back → they played together. The red colour is a detail; the lost ball and Jo’s help explain the story.

Try it together: Can we leave out Jo’s help and still explain how Sam got the ball back?

See answer and teaching guidance

No. Jo’s help connects the problem to the ending. The ball’s colour can be left out in a short retelling.

Watch for this: A list of objects is not a retelling. Ask “What happened?”

On paper: Practice A has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

2. Use story structure

A beginning introduces who and where. The middle tells what happens, including a problem if there is one. The ending tells how the story finishes. Not every familiar text has the same pattern.

Beginning, middle, end

Beginning: Sam and his ball are at the park. Middle: the ball rolls away, and Jo helps. End: Sam thanks Jo, and they play.

Work it through

Draw one picture for each part. Point to each picture while you tell the story aloud. The pictures help you remember; your telling gives the listener meaning.

Try it together: Which part tells us how the problem is solved?

See answer and teaching guidance

The middle includes Jo using a stick to get the ball back. The ending shows what happens afterwards: Sam thanks Jo, and they play.

Watch for this: Do not stop at the problem. Give the listener a clear finish.

On paper: Practice B has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

3. Keep events in order

Tell the events in the order they happen. First, next, then and last can help. Use the story’s meaning as well as these words; adding “first” does not make a mixed-up event correct.

Put the events together

Jo got the ball back. Sam took the ball to the park. The ball rolled under a bench.

Work it through

Start with Sam taking the ball to the park. Next the ball rolls under the bench. Then Jo gets it back. The help comes after the problem.

Try it together: Tell the three events in order. Why does Jo’s help come later?

See answer and teaching guidance

Sam takes the ball to the park; it rolls under a bench; Jo gets it back. Jo helps because the ball is stuck. A drawing can support the telling.

Watch for this: Order the events, not just the cards. Ask why one event follows another.

On paper: Practice C has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

4. Keep characters and setting clear

Name who is involved and where the important events happen. Use actions and speech from the story to show what a character does or feels. Make it clear who “he”, “she” or “they” means.

Who did what?

Sam could not reach the ball. Jo used a stick to roll it out. “Thank you, Jo,” said Sam.

Work it through

Jo is the friend who helps. Sam thanks Jo. Their actions and speech show help and gratitude. The story happens at the park; we are not told an exact time.

Try it together: Who uses the stick? Tell it clearly without saying only “he did it”.

See answer and teaching guidance

Jo uses the stick to get the ball. Use Jo’s name when the listener might not know who a pronoun means. Accept an oral character description such as “Jo helps Sam.”

Watch for this: Do not invent character actions or feelings for an accurate retelling. Use clues, and distinguish a reasonable inference from a stated fact.

On paper: Practice D has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

5. Use story language

Use familiar story words, clear action words and a little character speech. You can act, draw or use a recording to support your telling. A grown-up may write your words for you.

Tell and act

“I cannot reach it,” said Sam. Jo rolled the ball out. “Thank you,” said Sam.

Work it through

Practise Sam’s worried voice, then his thankful voice. Keep the main meaning even if your exact words change. A description like “Jo is helpful” needs an action clue: Jo gets the ball back.

Try it together: Say Sam’s words in your own way. What does Jo do that shows help?

See answer and teaching guidance

For example: “It is too far away!” Jo uses a stick to get the ball. Accept different clear wording, gesture and character expression; do not demand memorised lines or a particular accent.

Watch for this: Using your own words is still a retelling. Writing accuracy is not the speaking goal.

On paper: Practice E has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

6. Adapt without losing sense

An adaptation changes part of a familiar story on purpose. Say what you will change. Keep enough familiar characters, events or structure for someone to recognise the story, and make the changed parts fit.

Make one deliberate change

Change the place from the park to the classroom. Sam’s ball rolls under a desk. Jo uses a ruler to roll it out. Sam thanks Jo.

Work it through

The place changes, so the bench becomes a desk and the tool becomes a ruler. The familiar problem, help and thankful ending stay connected. You can also change a character or ending; one change is a manageable starting point, not a rule for every adaptation.

Try it together: Could the ball roll under a classroom desk and then be rescued from a park bench with no explanation? Tell a version that fits.

See answer and teaching guidance

That version mixes places without explaining the move. Keep it in the classroom: the ball rolls under a desk, Jo helps get it back, and Sam thanks Jo. Accept other coherent adaptations with an explained link.

Watch for this: A forgotten event is not automatically a deliberate adaptation. Ask what was changed and whether the new story makes sense.

On paper: Practice F has 10 questions. Use the separate test after teaching and practice; observe an oral retelling or adaptation as well.

Teaching help: listen to the child tell the story

Model, support, then listen

  1. Read a familiar story together. Model a short retelling and explain which events matter.
  2. Let the child plan with three drawings or story cards, role-play or a story map. Ask for a retelling in their own words.
  3. Ask the child to announce a change and tell an adapted version. Listen for connected events and a clear ending.
  4. Record which prompts or adult help were needed. Reduce help when the child is ready.

Observe: Does the listener know the main characters and events? Is the order clear? Does the child use relevant story words or character speech? Does the changed version fit together?

Accessible ways to participate: Drawing, gesture, role-play, signed communication, AAC, a scribe or recorded telling can support the child’s communication needs. A device is optional. Assess meaning, story structure and the support required; avoid treating handwriting, accent or fluent decoding as this code’s target.

Keep the evidence clear: Multiple-choice and written activities check supporting understanding. They do not by themselves establish that the child can orally retell or adapt a familiar story. Observe the actual communication performance and note the support used.

Key concept

  • Describe a character using details from a familiar story.
  • Imitate a character's way of speaking or expressing an attitude.
  • Retell key events in order using speaking, drawing, performance or digital tools.

  2. To adapt the story, choose a part to change, such as the character or place.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept a clear adaptation with the changed character and a connected ending.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Random changes — Keep enough familiar structure for the story to make sense.
  • Missing ending — A retell needs a clear ending.
  • Unexplained changes — Start with one change, then check that linked details fit.

Explain and apply

Retell in order

Retell: Mia found a seed. Mia planted it. A flower grew. What happened first, second and last?

Answer and teaching guidance

First Mia found a seed. Next Mia planted it. Last a flower grew. Keep this event order so listeners can follow the story.

Use characters and plot

If you act as the fox in a story, what words or actions help the audience know who you are?

Answer and teaching guidance

A strong answer uses character behaviour, voice, gesture or a simple prop tied to the plot.

Adapt one part

Change the setting of a familiar story while keeping the main character and problem.

Answer and teaching guidance

The adaptation should change one clear feature while preserving enough structure to remain recognisable.

Compare versions

Complete: In the original story ____. In my adapted story ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The comparison shows what stayed the same and what changed in the retell or adaptation.
