YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Build one complete sentence idea AC9E1LA06

Learning intention: I can recognise a complete idea, find its parts and add useful details.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A longer sentence can still be simple

“The little bird sang softly in the tree.” names what is involved and tells a happening with useful details. It is one independent clause: a complete idea that can stand alone. “In the tree” alone only gives a place in this context. Length is not the test of a simple sentence.

Learn the skill

1. Check the complete idea

Read the whole statement. Does it make sense by itself, or are you waiting for what happens or what is said about the named thing? Questions and commands can be complete too.

“The heavy bag” / “The bag is heavy.”

Try it · Which is a complete statement, and what does it tell?

The bag is heavy tells a state. The heavy bag only names something in this context. A sentence need not describe visible movement.

2. Keep the whole parts together

Ask who or what is involved and what is being said about it. A naming part can contain several words. Useful details can answer where, when or how.

“Our small class planted seeds in the garden.”

Try it · Which whole group names who planted? What happened, and where?

Our small class names who; planted seeds tells what happened; in the garden tells where. Seeds names something else involved. Do not reduce every meaningful group to one word.

3. Repair and expand without losing the idea

Say the intended whole idea before writing it. Supply a missing part or arrange the words sensibly. Add details without introducing another independent clause.

Words: the / puppy / rested. Then add a where detail.

Try it · Build a complete statement and add a sensible place.

The puppy rested. For example, The puppy rested beside the chair. The place detail keeps the one main idea. Capitals and end marks show a written boundary but cannot create missing meaning.

Common mistakes

“Short means complete.” Check whether the idea stands alone.

“A verb must show movement.” A state such as is heavy also completes an idea.

“Simple means exactly one verb word.” The teacher criterion is one independent clause; length or a surface word count is not enough.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Say it, build it, expand it

An adult says “The cat slept.” The learner makes a different complete statement, adds one where or when detail and reads it back. Compare it with a place phrase such as “under the bench”.

Adjust the challenge Starter: supplied who/what and happening cards. Core: create a complete statement and add one relevant detail. Stretch: explain why a longer version still has one independent clause and compare a complete calm command.

Read and scribe when grammar is the target. Observe the learner’s own sentence production separately from choosing a supplied complete sentence. Formal clause diagrams are not required.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “Close the gate.” complete even without a printed you?

Yes. In this command the person being addressed is understood.

2. Does a full stop make “Under the bench.” a complete statement?

In this standalone statement task it is still only a place phrase. Punctuation does not supply who or what and a happening or state.

3. Can “The bird is small.” be a simple sentence?

Yes. It is one complete clause describing a state rather than an action.

FAQ

Should we count words to find a simple sentence?

No. Check its complete clause and meaning. A sentence can include several useful details.

How can an adult help without giving the whole answer?

Ask who or what and what happens or is, then let the child supply and arrange the parts. Record any provided words.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: A sentence gives a complete idea. In a statement, a naming part tells who or what; a happening part tells what happens or what something is like.

1. One complete idea

Read the whole group of words. Check that it makes sense as a statement by itself.

Example

“The cup is full.” is complete. “The full cup” only names something; it does not give the whole statement.

Try it: Which gives a complete statement: “The little duck” or “The little duck swims”?

See answer and explanation

The little duck swims. It names the duck and tells what it does. A statement can also describe a state, as in The cup is full.

2. Who or what is involved

Keep the whole naming part, including any useful describing words.

Example

In “The small frog jumped”, The small frog tells who or what the statement is about.

Try it: Find the naming part in “Our class planted seeds.”

See answer and explanation

Our class. Planted seeds tells what happened. Do not confuse the name of the naming part with a demand to identify every grammatical label.

3. What happens or what is

A sentence can tell an action, a happening or a state. A state does not need visible movement.

Example

“The dog runs.” tells an action. “The bag is heavy.” tells a state.

Try it: Is “The gate is shut” complete even though nothing is moving?

See answer and explanation

Yes. The gate is the naming part; is shut tells its state. Not every sentence describes movement.

4. Repair and order words

Say the intended idea before writing. Add a missing part or put supplied words in a meaningful order.

Example

Recount fragment: “sat beside the fence” → “The child sat beside the fence.”

Try it: Order these words into a statement: “the / bird / sings”.

See answer and explanation

The bird sings. Read it aloud to check the meaning. Capitals and end marks help the written boundary but do not supply missing meaning.

5. Add details without adding another clause

A sentence can be longer and still have one main event or idea. Add useful where, when or how details.

Example

“The duck swam.” → “The small duck swam slowly beside the reeds.” It still tells one event.

Try it: Add a where detail to “The puppy rested.”

See answer and explanation

For example, The puppy rested on the mat. Accept another relevant place. Do not count words to decide whether a sentence is simple.

6. Check meaning and the written boundary

Read what you wrote. Check the complete idea first, then the capital and suitable end mark.

Example

“Is the gate shut?” is a complete question. “Close the gate.” is a complete command; the person being addressed is understood.

Try it: Repair “the child smiled” as a written statement.

See answer and explanation

The child smiled. Start with a capital and end with a full stop. A question uses a question mark; some complete commands do not show the word you.

Try a worked example together

Build a complete idea

Work through the example

  1. Join the parts: “The bird sang in the tree.” It makes sense by itself.
  2. “In the tree” alone gives a place but not the whole idea.
  3. A simple sentence can include useful details. It can also describe a state, such as “The bird is small.”

Watch for this: Sentence length alone does not tell you whether the idea is complete.

Try it together

Make “sat on the mat” into a complete sentence.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, “The cat sat on the mat.” Accept another suitable naming part.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Single independent clause is the teacher term: one clause that can stand alone. Length is not the test. Do not teach that every sentence must contain a visible action, one written noun, exactly one verb word or a full stop. Model complete statements first, then acknowledge simple questions and commands.

Check: Compare “Under the bench” and “The puppy rested under the bench.” Explain which gives a complete statement, then add one useful detail to a new statement.

See answer and explanation

The puppy rested under the bench is complete: it names who or what and tells what happened. Under the bench supplies only a place in this context. Accept a relevant detail that preserves one main clause.

Help: Use two labelled card groups: who/what and what happens or is. Rehearse aloud, point to the parts, then write or dictate the child’s sentence.

Next challenge: Expand a short statement with two relevant details. Explain why the longer version still gives one main event or idea.
