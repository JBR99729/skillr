YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Hear, echo and invent sound patterns AC9E1LE04

Learning intention: I can listen to a literary sound pattern, imitate it and invent a meaningful new version.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Listen to beginnings and endings

Adult reads the original lines aloud: “Small snails slide. We see a bee beside a tree.” Small/snails/slide repeat the beginning /s/ sound. Bee/tree have matching spoken endings. Say the whole lines for meaning before comparing their different sound patterns.

Learn the skill

1. Hear the part that matches

Alliteration repeats a beginning sound in nearby words. Rhyme matches spoken endings in different words. Listen; spelling alone cannot decide either pattern.

Adult says: “Big bears bounce.” Then says cat, hat and cup.

Try it · Which beginning sound repeats in the line? Which two words rhyme?

Big/bears/bounce begin with /b/. Cat/hat rhyme; cat/cup share a beginning but have different endings. Adult models the actual sounds and accepts pronunciation differences with appropriate guidance.

2. Echo and choose an effect

Listen first, then echo a poem, chant or song. Pace, volume and pauses can change an effect. Keep any movement comfortable and optional.

Original chant: “Tap, tap, rest. Tap, tap, rest.” Adult says it gently, then with a livelier steady pulse. For a song, adult sings “We can play, come this way” to do–re–mi, mi–re–do, one word per beat at a comfortable pitch.

Try it · Echo one model. Which delivery do you choose, and how does it fit?

Accept a deliberate gentle or lively choice with an explanation or indication. Observe actual listening and echoing. The song has play/way rhyme; the chant repeats its words. Written recognition does not demonstrate singing or imitation.

3. Invent and discuss a new line

Change a heard pattern while keeping a sensible meaning. A new line can use a different word pair or action. Tell a listener what you kept and changed.

Original model: “A goat floats in a boat.” Goat/boat rhyme. Another model is “Little lizards leap,” with a returning /l/ beginning.

Try it · Invent a different rhyming or alliterative line, say or communicate it, and explain its pattern.

For example, “A cat sits on a mat” rhymes, or “Little lambs listen” repeats /l/. Accept another meaningful creation. A copied model is imitation; a genuine new choice shows invention. Do not require one exact line or a technical definition.

Common mistakes

“Same letter always means same sound.” Listen to the actual beginning; an adult can model spelling contrasts.

“The same word repeated supplies a new rhyming word.” Name repetition separately from a different word with a matching ending.

“Louder is always better.” Choose a voice that fits the text and listener.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Hear it, change it, share it

Adult reads this original model: “Sleepy seals slide by the sea.” Echo it, then create a different meaningful line with a repeated beginning sound. Share it with a partner and discuss what repeats. Use the actual adult-sung six-word model in focus 2 for an optional new song version.

Adjust the challenge Starter: echo a short adult model and choose between two heard words. Core: imitate, make a meaningful change and share it. Stretch: compare delivery effects or combine rhyme with repetition, within familiar language.

Actual listening and response are needed. Use authorised First Nations Australian performances, Asian poetry/chants/songs and familiar-topic haiku in wider reading with their creator, language and context identified. Original neutral verse does not replace these cultural selections. The retained guide gives preparation guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Are cat and cup a rhyming pair?

No. They begin alike, but their spoken endings differ.

2. Must syllables always get one beat each?

No. A steady beat and the word syllables can differ. The short teaching tune assigns one word per beat only for that model.

3. Does saying a copied line prove invention?

It shows imitation. A meaningful new word or line is needed to show an original change.

FAQ

Does the child need formal music notation?

No. The tune guide supports the adult, who models the actual song first.

Do we require a perfect performance?

No. Notice the intended sound pattern, meaningful change, discussion and access support; do not demand perfect pitch or a particular accent.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: Listen to and discuss poems, chants, rhymes and songs, then imitate and invent their sound patterns.

How to use this guide: Listen to a whole text, echo a model, make an original change and discuss its sound. These six learning focuses match Practice A–F in the worksheet pack. Sound words, repetition and performance support the central work of hearing, imitating and inventing alliteration and rhyme.

I can: hear matching beginning and ending sounds; echo a pattern; invent a meaningful new version; perform or communicate it and discuss what I notice.

Useful words: Alliteration: nearby words repeating a beginning sound. Rhyme: words with matching ending sounds. Repetition: a word or line heard again. Sound word: a word that suggests a sound. Performance: how we say, sing or communicate a text.

1. Hear alliteration

Alliteration repeats a beginning sound in nearby words. Say the words aloud before looking at their spelling. Read the whole short line for meaning, then listen to its pattern.

Listen together

Busy bees buzz beside the bucket.

Work it through

  1. Read the line naturally and talk about the bees.
  2. Say busy, bees, buzz, beside and bucket slowly. Hear /b/ at the beginning of each.
  3. Echo the line and gently emphasise /b/. The repeated beginning sound makes the words sound connected.

Try it together: Which keeps the /b/ pattern: “bouncy bear” or “bouncy fox”? Say your choice, then add another familiar /b/ word.

See answer and explanation

“Bouncy bear” repeats /b/. “Bouncy fox” changes to /f/ in fox. A new phrase such as “bouncy brown bear” is one possible creation; accept another meaningful phrase with the heard /b/ pattern.

Adult-led contrast: Say cat and kite. Both begin with /k/ although the letters differ. This is a listening example; the child need not spell either word.

Watch for this: A matching first letter is a useful clue, but the sound decides. Introduce spelling exceptions only with adult modelling, not as an independent reading trap.

Listen and look for: The child hears the shared sound, echoes the phrase and creates a meaningful new phrase.

On paper: Continue with Practice A in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

2. Hear rhyme

Rhyming words have matching spoken endings, such as cat and hat. Listen from the vowel sound to the end. Naming a picture clearly matters: a picture could have more than one sensible name. Adults should agree on the intended word before asking for a rhyme.

Listen together

The cat sits on a mat.
It watches a little hat.

Work it through

  1. Listen to the whole two-line rhyme. Name what the cat does.
  2. Say mat and hat slowly. Their /at/ endings match, while the beginning sounds differ.
  3. Say cat and cup. They start alike, but their endings do not rhyme.

Try it together: Say boat, goat and book. Which word does not rhyme? Now listen: “A goat climbs into a boat on the pond.” Complete “The goat is in the ___” using boat or coat so it matches that scene, then reread for meaning.

See answer and explanation

Book does not rhyme with boat/goat. Boat gives the sensible line “The goat is in the boat.” Coat rhymes too, but it does not match the stated scene of a goat in a boat on the pond. In a different imagined scene a goat could wear a coat; a rhyme must fit the context we are completing.

Watch for this: Rhyme is heard, not decided by the last letter. Repeating the exact same word is repetition; for this rhyme task, find a different word with the matching ending.

Listen and look for: The child names the words, compares endings, rejects the odd ending and checks the completed line’s meaning.

On paper: Continue with Practice B in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

3. Hear sound words

A sound word helps us imagine a noise. Use familiar words such as drip, buzz or bang in a short poem or chant. How we say the word can change the effect; explain why a voice choice fits.

Listen together

Drip, drip, rain on the tin.
Soft little drops are tapping in.

Work it through

  1. Listen once and picture the rain.
  2. Find drip: it suggests the sound of drops. Rain names what is falling; it is not itself the sound word in this line.
  3. Say drip softly, then more loudly. Compare how the two deliveries make the rain seem.

Try it together: Which delivery fits the “soft little drops”: a gentle drip or a shouted DRIP? Echo your choice and explain.

See answer and explanation

A gentle drip usually fits soft little drops. A loud version could suggest heavier rain or a deliberately different effect. Accept a choice when the child explains it using the text; loud does not automatically mean better.

Watch for this: A sound word and an object name ask different things. Model the noise, then return to the whole line so this stays a literary listening activity.

Listen and look for: The child imitates the sound word and connects a delivery choice to a detail in the poem.

On paper: Continue with Practice C in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

4. Hear repetition

Repetition uses a word or line again. A repeated refrain can help listeners predict and join in. Rhyme matches endings in different words; repetition brings back the same word or phrase. A text can use both.

Listen together

Tap with me, tap with me.
Tap your knees, tap with me.

Work it through

  1. An adult says both lines once; the child listens for what returns.
  2. Point out “tap with me” in both lines. Echo that phrase together.
  3. Read again and pause before the final phrase so the child can join in. Keep a comfortable pace.

Try it together: Keep the repeated phrase and invent a new line about tapping toes. Is “me” repeated, or is it a new rhyming word?

See answer and explanation

One possible line is “Tap your toes, tap with me.” The repeated phrase is kept while the action changes. Me is the same word again, so that example is repetition; it does not supply a new rhyming word.

Watch for this: A repeated starting sound, a repeated whole word and a rhyme are different patterns. Say exactly which part returns rather than calling every repetition a rhyme.

Listen and look for: The child joins the refrain after listening and changes one action while preserving the repeated phrase.

On paper: Continue with Practice D in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

5. Perform for effect

Listen, echo and then perform. We can change pace, volume, clear word endings and optional actions to suit a poem, chant or song. A beat is a steady pulse; word syllables do not always match one beat each. Keep movement comfortable and optional.

Listen, echo and perform

Original chant: Hop, hop, stop!
Original song words: We can play, come this way.

Work it through

  1. Say the chant first. Echo it and tap a steady pulse together. Compare a gentle version and an excited version.
  2. For the song, an adult sings the six words to the short original tune below. Let the child listen first, then echo each phrase.
  3. Discuss how the sung version differs from the spoken chant. Sing or say the words again so play/way endings can be heard.

Try it together: Echo the chant and the song. Choose a calm or lively delivery. What pattern do you hear, and how does your delivery fit?

See answer and explanation

Hop repeats in the chant; hop/stop rhyme. Play/way rhyme in the song. A calm delivery might use a gentler voice and slower pace; a lively delivery might use a brighter voice and brisker pace. There is no single best performance: listen for a deliberate choice and an explanation.

Adult tune guide: Use a comfortable pitch. Sing We–can–play to do–re–mi, then come–this–way to mi–re–do. Give each word one steady beat. This is an original short teaching tune; notation is adult support, not a Year 1 assessment. Model it before expecting an echo. Children may join through voice, signing, AAC, tapping or another accessible mode.

Watch for this: Talking about a performance is not the same as doing it. Hear a real adult song model and observe the child’s response. Do not require perfect pitch, movement or formal musical notation.

Listen and look for: Actual listening, echoing, joining or an accessible response; clear audible patterns and a reason for one performance choice.

On paper: Continue with Practice E in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

6. Invent sound patterns

Start with a heard model, change it and check your new version. Choose rhyme, alliteration or a repeated refrain. Say your creation aloud: it should keep the chosen pattern and make the meaning you intend. Adult scribing can record the child’s words.

Invent and share

Rhyme model: A dog sits on a log.
Alliteration model: Small snails slide.

Work it through

  1. Hear dog/log, then collect another familiar rhyming pair such as boat/goat. Make a new meaningful line from that pair.
  2. Hear /s/ in small/snails/slide. Change or add a word that keeps the shared beginning sound.
  3. Say the new version to a partner. Point out or explain the part that matches, then listen to the partner’s response.

Try it together: Invent a short rhyme or alliterative line. Then change one word in the chant from focus 4, or invent new words to the tune from focus 5. Share what you kept and what you changed.

See answer and explanation

Examples include “A goat floats in a boat” (goat/boat rhyme) and “Small snails sleep” (the /s/ sound returns). “Tap your toes, tap with me” keeps the refrain; “We can go, watch us grow” can use the six-note tune and go/grow rhyme. These are models, not required answers. Accept a different meaningful creation with an audible pattern.

Watch for this: A copied line shows imitation; an original change provides evidence of invention. A rhyming word must also fit the intended meaning. Support planning without supplying the whole response.

Listen and look for: The child produces a genuine change or new line, performs or communicates it, names or indicates the pattern, and discusses it with someone.

On paper: Continue with Practice F in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

The refrain and tune for this new version

Original refrain from focus 4: “Tap with me, tap with me. Tap your knees, tap with me.” Original song from focus 5: “We can play, come this way.” Adult sings We–can–play to do–re–mi, then come–this–way to mi–re–do, one word per steady beat at a comfortable pitch. Let the child actually hear the model before inventing new words. These are supplied original teaching models, not traditional cultural works.

Teaching help: listen, echo, change and discuss

Start: Read or perform the whole short text for meaning. Reread and draw attention to one audible pattern. Name picture words before comparing them. An adult can say all written choices so this is not a spelling test.

Support: Offer two familiar word choices and a short echo. Accept speech, signing, AAC, pointing, tapping or adult-scribed creation as appropriate. Match the response to the child’s communication and hearing needs and record the support given.

Core: The child hears a pattern, imitates it, makes a meaningful original change, then shares and explains or indicates the pattern.

Extend within Year 1: Compare two delivery choices or combine a refrain with rhyme. Change one line and explain what stays the same. Formal rhyme schemes, metre and technical literary terminology are not required.

Observe: Keep a brief note of what the child heard, echoed, invented and discussed. A correct printed choice supports recognition; it cannot by itself establish listening, imitation, singing or invention.

Quick check: hear it, make it, share it

Original two-line model:
Little lizards lie in the sun.
Run to the shade when the day is done.

  1. Listen to an adult read both lines. Echo “little lizards lie” and name the shared beginning sound.
  2. Say sun/done. What matches? Choose a new familiar pair of rhyming words.
  3. Invent a short line using alliteration or rhyme, then share it with a partner.
  4. Tell your partner what pattern you used and respond to a question or comment.
See answer and explanation

Little/lizards/lie share /l/. Sun/done rhyme in usual Australian English despite different spelling; model pronunciation and respect the child’s accent. An original /l/ phrase such as “Little lambs leap” or a meaningful line with another rhyming pair can meet the creation task. There is no single correct invented line. Observe the actual echo, original change and exchange; do not infer them from a written answer.

Continue or revisit: If beginnings and endings are mixed, contrast cat/cup with cat/hat aloud. If a copied line is offered for invention, help change one meaningful word. If the pattern works but the meaning is unclear, ask the child to explain their imagined scene or revise the line. Use a fresh example after support.

See the idea: Make a pattern with sounds

Make a pattern with sounds

Work through the example

  1. Big, blue and bag begin with the same /b/ sound: this is alliteration.
  2. Bee and tree have matching ending sounds: they rhyme.
  3. Say a short pattern aloud, then choose new words that keep its sound pattern and meaning.

Watch for this: Alliteration uses beginning sounds; rhyme uses ending sounds. They are different patterns.

Try it together

Add a word that begins with /s/ to “silly, sunny, ___”.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, sandy or sleepy. Accept an appropriate word starting with /s/.

Alongside the original sound-play examples, listen to suitable authorised performances by First Nations Australian creators and poetry, chants or songs from Asian cultures. Common misconceptions
  • Alliteration = same letter only — Listen for same sound, not just same spelling.
  • Rhyme = same first sound — Rhyme is usually ending sound.
  • No performance — Say it aloud to hear the pattern.

Which line repeats a beginning sound: Big bears bounce or The frog jumps?

Answer and teaching guidance

Answer and teaching guidance
