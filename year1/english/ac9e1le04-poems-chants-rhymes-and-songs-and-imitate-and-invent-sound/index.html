Learn the skill

Six small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

Learning goal: Listen to and discuss poems, chants, rhymes and songs, then imitate and invent their sound patterns.

Useful words: Alliteration: nearby words repeating a beginning sound. Rhyme: words with matching ending sounds. Repetition: a word or line heard again. Sound word: a word that suggests a sound. Performance: how we say, sing or communicate a text.

1. Hear alliteration

Listen for the same beginning sound in nearby words. Spelling alone cannot decide the pattern.

Example

Original line: Busy bees buzz beside the bucket. The /b/ sound repeats.

Try it: Do cat and kite share a beginning sound even though their first letters differ?

See answer and explanation

Yes: both begin with /k/. Children can listen to an adult’s model; spelling knowledge is not the target. Accept sound-pattern responses appropriate to the child’s speech or communication mode.

2. Hear rhyme

Listen for matching ending sounds. Words need not begin with the same sound or use the same spelling.

Example

Original rhyme: A bee by the tree / is buzzing at me. Bee, tree and me rhyme in the modelled pronunciation.

Try it: Which rhymes with night: light or nest? Then suggest another rhyme.

See answer and explanation

Light. A possible additional rhyme is bright. Nest begins like night but its ending does not rhyme. Listen to the child’s pronunciation and allow accent variation rather than marking an accent wrong.

3. Hear sound words

Notice words that suggest a noise and discuss what they make you hear or imagine. A sound word is not automatically rhyme or alliteration.

Example

Original line: Plop! The pebble drops. Drip, drip goes the tap.

Try it: Which words suggest noises? How could you perform one?

See answer and explanation

Plop and drip suggest noises. A child could give plop a quick dropping sound, or pause between the repeated drips. Accept a different performance with an explanation.

4. Hear repetition

Find a word or line repeated in a poem, chant or song. Discuss how the repeating part helps listeners join in or notice an idea.

Example

Original chant: Tap the tin, tap the tin. / Let the gentle beat begin.

Try it: Which phrase repeats? Copy the pattern with a different suitable object.

See answer and explanation

Tap the tin repeats. For example, “Tap the box, tap the box.” Repetition means words or a pattern return; the replacement does not also have to rhyme unless that is the chosen task.

5. Perform for effect

Try pace, volume, rhythm and pauses, then discuss what changed. Perform a chant or sing a short repeated line on an adult-modelled tune.

Example

Original lines: Softly, softly, leaves drift down. / Suddenly — CRACK! — a branch hits the ground.

Try it: How could you change your voice or timing between these lines? Explain the effect.

See answer and explanation

A quieter, slower first line and a sharper crack may suggest the contrast. Other choices are valid if explained. Observe listening and discussion; a written selection alone does not show performance.

6. Invent sound patterns

Imitate a feature you heard, then create your own meaningful words or lines. Discuss which sound choice you kept and perform it for a partner.

Example

Original model: Small snails slide. / At night, they hide. The first line repeats /s/; night and hide are not a rhyming pair.

Try it: Invent a new /s/ line, then a pair of rhyming lines about a familiar topic.

See answer and explanation

For example, “Sleepy snakes snooze.” A rhyme pair could be “I see a kite / high in the light.” Accept other sound-appropriate, meaningful examples. Do not call all repeated sounds rhyme.

See the idea: Make a pattern with sounds

Make a pattern with sounds

Work through the example

  1. Big, blue and bag begin with the same /b/ sound: this is alliteration.
  2. Bee and tree have matching ending sounds: they rhyme.
  3. Say a short pattern aloud, then choose new words that keep its sound pattern and meaning.

Watch for this: Alliteration uses beginning sounds; rhyme uses ending sounds. They are different patterns.

Try it together

Add a word that begins with /s/ to “silly, sunny, ___”.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, sandy or sleepy. Accept an appropriate word starting with /s/.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Read directions and texts aloud as needed. Accept oral, signed, AAC, drawn or adult-scribed responses. Record the learner’s own work and the help given; observe the actual discussion or performance when that is the skill.

Explore poems and songs from different traditions

Alongside the original sound-play examples, listen to suitable authorised performances by First Nations Australian creators and poetry, chants or songs from Asian cultures. Identify the creator, language and context; follow permissions and cultural guidance. The pack’s original texts are not presented as First Nations or traditional cultural works.

Listen to a suitable haiku about nature or a season. Discuss what children notice and how the poem sounds; do not require a formal syllable-count formula. These are wider teaching contexts, not extra scored test questions.

Key concept

Learning goal: I can listen to, discuss and create sound patterns in poems, chants, rhymes and songs.

  • Listen and respond to performance poetry, chants and songs from First Nations Australians.
  • Explore poetry, chants and songs from Asian cultures.
  • Listen to short poems such as haiku about familiar topics.
  • Imitate and invent alliteration, rhyme and other sound patterns.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Common misconceptions
  • Alliteration = same letter only — Listen for same sound, not just same spelling.
  • Rhyme = same first sound — Rhyme is usually ending sound.
  • No performance — Say it aloud to hear the pattern.
Try these
  1. Sound hunt — Find repeated first sounds in a line.
    big blue bird
  2. Rhyme chain — Make a chain of words that rhyme.
    light / night / bright
  3. Invent a chant — Create a two-line chant with one repeated sound.
    Tap the table, tap the tin.
Explain and apply

Complete the rhyme

Finish: I see a bee in a ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

A rhyming answer such as tree fits because it shares the spoken ending sound. This is the closest IXL match for the code.

Hear alliteration

Which line repeats a beginning sound: Big bears bounce or The frog jumps?

Answer and teaching guidance

Big bears bounce repeats the beginning /b/ sound. The decision is made by listening.

Clap rhythm

Clap the beat while saying Run to the beach. Which word comes last?

Answer and teaching guidance

Students track spoken rhythm and word order, then identify the final word.

Invent a sound pattern

Make a new chant line that uses rhyme, repetition or alliteration.

Answer and teaching guidance

The new line should imitate a sound feature from poems, chants, rhymes or songs.

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • Which uses alliteration: lazy lion or cat hat?
  • Give a rhyme for day.
  • What is a chant?
  • Invent two words starting with /s/.
