Short oral and multimodal presentations

Learning goal: Plan a short talk, share useful details and help your listener follow. Use words, a picture or object, helpful gestures and a clear voice.

Get ready to speak

A small plan. A clear message.

  1. Choose something you know or have learnt.
  2. Plan a start, useful details and an ending.
  3. Show, speak, listen to feedback and try again.

You need a listener, a familiar object or drawing, and paper for a few picture or word cues. Work through one focus at a time.

Learn the skill

Five small steps to a clear talk

A presentation shares ideas with listeners. A multimodal presentation uses more than one way to share meaning, such as speaking while showing a picture. An adult can read the prompts aloud.

1. Plan a clear talk

Your opening tells the topic. The middle gives useful details in a sensible order. Your conclusion is a final thought that finishes the topic.

1 · Opening

“This is my pencil case.”

Name the topic so listeners know what the talk is about.
2 · Middle

“It holds my pencils. The zip keeps them inside.”

Add two different details that belong to the topic.
3 · Conclusion

“My pencil case keeps my pencils together.”

Finish with a connected thought.

Watch for this: “Thank you” is polite, but first give a final thought about your topic. “And then…” sounds as though more is coming.

Try together: “We made a reading corner. We put books on a shelf and cushions on the floor.” Say a final sentence that finishes this talk.

See an example and why it works

“Now our reading corner is ready to use.” This finishes the same topic. A different connected ending is also welcome.

Your turn: Choose a familiar activity. Draw or write three reminders: topic, two details, final thought. Use your reminders to give a short talk.

2. Choose useful details and topic words

A detail tells more about the topic. Choose details that help your listener. Topic words name its parts, actions or ideas clearly.

Worked example: tell about the brush

“This paintbrush has a long handle. Its bristles spread paint.”

The two topic words name useful parts. “It is nice” gives the listener less information. The picture does not tell us who owns the brush, so we do not invent that detail.

Try together: This class record says: “We sorted buttons into two bowls. One bowl held small buttons. The other held large buttons.” Which detail belongs in the report: “We sorted by size” or “We ate apples”? Explain your choice.

See answer and explanation

“We sorted by size” tells how the buttons were grouped. The record says nothing about eating apples. Report what the record actually tells you.

Your turn: Show a familiar object. Say two different useful details and use a word that names one of its parts. Explain that word if your listener does not know it.

3. Report, explain how, or give a reason

Choose your message to fit what you want your listener to learn.

Report: what happened?

“Our group folded a card. We drew a star on the front.”

This tells what the group did.
Explain how: what do I do?

“First, fold the paper in half. Next, draw a star on the front.”

Action words and order help someone make it.
Give a reason: why try it?

“Let's learn the clap game because everyone gets a turn to lead.”

The reason explains a benefit.

Worked example: In the clap game, one person claps a pattern, the group copies it, then a new person leads. “Everyone gets a turn to lead” matches those rules. “Because I like it” says you enjoy it, but does not explain what you like about it.

Try together: In a colour-spotting game, one player names a colour. Everyone points to something that colour. Players take turns naming colours. Recommend the game and give one reason from these rules.

See an example and why it works

“We should learn this game because we can all join in by finding a colour.” The reason fits what players do. Other relevant reasons are welcome; a child may also explain why they would choose a different game.

Your turn: Make a small model or drawing. First report what you made. Then explain one making step to a partner. Notice how your words change when your purpose changes.

4. Make pictures and gestures helpful

A gesture is a movement that helps show meaning. Point to the part you are naming. Keep your picture or object where the listener can see it.

These are possible meanings. Your words and the situation help a listener understand the movement.

Worked example: show the part you mean

Say, “This fan has folds,” while pointing to the folds in the picture. Move your hand away so the listener can see them. Pointing at an unrelated part would make your words harder to follow.

Try together: Say, “The balloon went up,” with a movement that shows what happened. Ask your partner how the movement helped.

What to look for

An upward movement timed with “went up” can show the direction. Other clear, accessible movements or pointing are valid. The movement should add meaning, not just keep the hands busy.

Your turn: Draw a favourite place. Give a short talk and point to one feature as you name it. Use three small picture or word cues if they help you remember.

5. Use a clear voice, pace and pauses

Volume is how loud or quiet your voice is. Pace is how fast or slowly you speak. Use a comfortable voice your listener can hear and a pace they can follow. Louder or slower is not always better.

Hear the difference: adult model

  1. Say, “Our class made a flag. We hung it beside the door.” once too quietly for the listener, then at a comfortable, audible volume. Ask which was easier to hear. There is no need to shout.
  2. Say the same message once rushed, then at a followable pace with a short pause between the ideas. Keep the volume similar. Ask the listener to tell you what happened and where the flag went.

Listen to both versions aloud. Looking at the words cannot show whether the voice or pace worked.

Our class made a flag.

Pause between ideas

We hung it beside the door.

Try together: Give your partner these two instructions: “Draw a circle. Put a dot inside it.” Pause after each instruction so they can act. If your partner gets stuck, check the words and the pace.

What to look and listen for

The partner has time to draw the circle before adding the dot. Keep each idea together; do not break it into awkward single-word chunks. If the words were unclear, repair them rather than only speaking more slowly.

Try a different purpose: Say this little rhyme: “Up goes my hand. Down to the sand.” Try a flowing rhythm, then a useful pause between the lines. Show the movements if they help. Ask which version was easy to follow. An information talk and a rhyme need not sound exactly the same.

Your turn: Rehearse a short talk. Ask, “Could you hear each part? Was any part hard to follow?” Use specific feedback such as “I missed the last words.” Practise that part, then say the whole message again. Keep what already works.

Put it together: plan, speak and review

Choose a familiar interest, report a real group activity, explain how to do something, or recommend a game. Plan a brief talk with a picture, object or a few cues.

  1. Introduce the topic.
  2. Give two relevant details, steps or a reason that fits your purpose. Use useful topic words.
  3. Finish with a connected final thought.
  4. Show a helpful feature or use a meaningful gesture while speaking.
  5. Ask your listener about volume and pace. Rehearse a useful change if needed.
Adult observation and support

Listen to the actual talk. Note the opening, connected middle and conclusion; accurate details and topic words; purpose; meaningful gesture or visual reference; audibility; and followable pace. Note any prompting. If a feature was not observed, leave it unjudged and provide another opportunity.

Offer oral planning, drawing, a familiar listener, seated delivery and suitable communication supports. Model first, then gradually remove sentence starters. Do not grade handwriting, drawing skill, confidence, accent, standing or eye contact. Extend by changing the audience or purpose, not by demanding a longer speech.

Online selections can check some ideas about speaking. They cannot establish successful live delivery.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • A new topic at the end: return to the main idea and finish it.
  • Interesting but unrelated details: ask, “Does this help explain my topic?”
  • A reason that just repeats liking: explain a useful feature or benefit.
  • A picture hidden by the speaker's hand: point briefly, then uncover the feature.
  • Rushing or shouting: ask what the listener could hear and follow; adjust only what needs changing.
For adults: curriculum focus

Australian Curriculum v9 · Year 1 English · AC9E1LY07. Children create and deliver short presentations on personal and learnt topics. Teaching covers an opening, middle and conclusion, topic vocabulary, meaningful gesture, volume and pace.

Contexts include reporting group work, explaining a process, sharing interests or learning, adapting voice for information, stories or verse, and giving reasons for learning a game. Observe speaking as well as planning.
