Learn the skill

Five ways to practise writing

Plan → Say your idea → Write → Reread → Edit. Choose a purpose and reader first. Open one focus, work through its model, then try your own words.

1. Report facts about a topic

A report tells a reader facts about a topic. A fact can be checked. Name the topic, then give related details in simple sentences.

Use these facts: A watering can holds water. It has a handle and a spout.

Model: “A watering can holds water. Water comes out of its spout.” Both sentences stay on the topic. Spout is a precise topic word.

Say who or what the sentence is about and what it does or is like. “Has a spout” leaves out what has the spout. “A watering can has a spout” makes the idea complete.

Try it: A book has pages. Its cover protects the pages. Write a two-sentence report for someone learning about books. Use the words pages and cover.

Check the answer

For example: “A book has pages. The cover protects the pages.” Accept other accurate sentences using the supplied facts. “Books are the best” is an opinion, not a report fact.

2. Give an opinion and a reason

An opinion tells what you think or prefer. A reason explains why. People can have different opinions.

Model: “I like making models because I can turn boxes into new things.” The choice is making models. Turning boxes into new things explains why the writer likes it.

“Boxes can be brown” is a related fact, but it does not explain this preference. Check that the reason answers “Why do you think that?”

Try it: Would you choose drawing or singing? Write your choice and a reason for a friend. Say your idea aloud first.

Check the answer

For example: “I like singing because I can sing with my friends.” Accept either choice with a relevant reason. Do not require the model answer or the same preference as the adult.

3. Put a recount in order

A recount tells about something that happened, or something you imagine happened. Tell who was there and what happened in order. Words such as first, then and last can help.

Model: “This morning I made a badge. First, I drew a star. Then I wore my badge.” The introduction gives the time and event. The next sentences keep the actions in order.

Keep the same time when it makes sense: “I drew” and “I wore” tell about the past. A recount does not need a made-up problem or a long story.

Try it: Imagine that you put on a coat and then went outside. Write two sentences to recount those actions in order.

Check the answer

For example: “First, I put on my coat. Then I went outside.” Accept equivalent sentences with the same order. For a longer try, add when it happened or one relevant detail.

4. Make pictures and words work together

A caption is a short piece of writing that explains a picture. Match its subject and action. Do not add a detail that the picture cannot show unless you clearly supply it as extra information.

Make the evidence: Draw a person tying a shoelace. Show the hands near the lace.

Model caption for that drawing: “The child ties a shoelace.” Check your drawing: does it show the action named in the sentence? “The child eats lunch” would not match.

Try it: Draw someone doing a familiar action. Write a caption that helps another person understand it. Ask the reader to point to the part that matches your words.

Check the answer

Accept a caption with a subject and an action that match the learner’s own drawing. The drawing is created before the caption; no unseen picture is needed. Do not score artistic quality.

Try a short digital sequence with an adult

Create two drawings on a device: planting a seed, then watering it. Put them in order and type a caption under each. For example: “First, I plant a seed.” “Then I water it.” Read the captions and check each against its image. Save the sequence with adult help. Paper work prepares for this activity but does not show digital creation by itself.

5. Reread and edit for meaning

To edit is to improve your writing. Reread aloud or quietly, pointing to each word. Check meaning first, then grammar, capitals, end marks and spelling. Change one thing at a time.

Draft: “my dog run fast”

Edited: “My dog runs fast.” Use a capital at the start, runs to agree with my dog, and a full stop to finish the statement.

Add, remove or change words: “I saw a little little bug.” repeats little. Remove one. Add a useful detail if you know it: “I saw a little bug on a leaf.” Do not add words only to make a sentence longer.

Choose an end mark that fits

  • “The bug is on a leaf.” tells something: full stop.
  • “Where is the bug?” asks something: question mark.
  • “Watch out!” gives an urgent warning: exclamation mark.

Notice and check spelling

Draft: “The bird has a bek.” If bek looks uncertain, check a class word wall, picture dictionary or supplied word list. Compare it with beak, then rewrite: “The bird has a beak.” Check familiar one- and two-syllable words, such as bird and rabbit. Asking for a spelling resource is part of editing.

Try it: Edit “i saw a rabit rabit”. Use this reference: rabbit. Explain each change, then read your finished sentence.

Check the answer

“I saw a rabbit.” Capitalise I, correct rabit using rabbit, remove the repeated word, and finish the statement with a full stop. Also accept a relevant added detail that keeps the meaning.

Quick check: create, then improve
  1. Write two report sentences using these facts: a rabbit has fur; it has long ears.
  2. Choose an activity you like. Write an opinion and a reason that explains your choice.
  3. Recount two real or imagined actions in order.
  4. Draw one of your actions and add a matching caption.
  5. Reread your writing. Show one change that improves it, or explain why a checked sentence is already clear.
Check the answer

Look for accurate related report facts; a stated preference with a relevant reason; a clear event order; agreement between the learner’s drawing and caption; and a meaningful edit or accurate explanation. Check simple sentence grammar, topic vocabulary, capitals, suitable end marks and familiar spelling. Accept varied original responses. Revisit the relevant focus when evidence is missing.

Adult support: Read directions aloud, discuss ideas and offer a short word bank. Model one sentence, then let the child compose. Record any scribing or spelling help. Build from one sentence to a few linked sentences; extend by adding a useful detail and explaining an edit. Independent writing needs the learner’s own composition, not copying a model.

For teachers: curriculum links

Australian Curriculum v9, Year 1 — AC9E1LY06: Create and re-read to edit short written and/or multimodal texts to report on a topic, express an opinion or recount a real or imagined event, using grammatically correct simple sentences, some topic-specific vocabulary, sentence boundary punctuation and correct spelling of some one- and two-syllable words.

The five teaching focuses include text structure and grammar, learning-area vocabulary, image-caption sequences, adding or deleting words for meaning, noticing uncertain spelling and using spelling resources. The supported digital sequence requires a device; the printable pack alone does not assess digital production.

  • Victoria: Level 1, selected written components of VC2E1LY13 and VC2E1LY14. Broader spoken-text and text-structure learning is also needed.
  • NSW: Stage 1, selected skills within EN1-CWT-01. The broader outcome also includes paragraph-level composition.
  • Queensland and South Australia: AC9E1LY06 within local planning.

These links support planning; they do not claim complete coverage of broader state outcomes. Keep the main writing demand at Year 1: short texts, simple sentences and supported editing.
