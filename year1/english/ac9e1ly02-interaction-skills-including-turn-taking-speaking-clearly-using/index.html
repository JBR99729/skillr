YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Listen, take turns and build a conversation AC9E1LY02

Learning intention: I can share an understandable message, listen for meaning and add a connected idea or question.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A pause can be thinking time

Mia says, “My tower fell because…” and pauses. Jo waits. Mia adds, “…the base was too small.” Jo asks, “Could we make a wider base?” Jo listens to the complete idea and builds on it instead of filling the pause with an unrelated topic.

Learn the skill

1. Share a fair turn

Agree on a useful signal and leave thinking time. If two people start together, pause and repair the overlap. A relevant response shows more than waiting silently.

Partner A: “I want a quiet game.” Partner B: “We could try a puzzle. Would that suit you?”

Try it · Practise a short exchange about a game. How will you repair speaking together?

Observe an actual exchange with a connected reply. “Please finish; I will wait” is one useful repair. Accept access supports and a choice to pass; do not supply the child’s contribution then call it independent.

2. Make meaning clear and check it

Use precise words, a suitable pace and a comfortable volume. Repeat, reword or use a communication support if needed. A listener can restate or ask for clarification.

Materials: paper and pencil. Adult says, “Draw a small circle beside a large square.” Listener checks, “Small circle beside large square—is that right?”

Try it · Give and follow a different simple drawing direction with a partner. Check what was understood.

Look for the stated size and position in the response and drawing. Clarification can be good listening. Observe actual speech where required; signing or AAC can communicate meaning while a speech-specific criterion remains unobserved.

3. Add a useful question or response

Stay connected to the last contribution. Ask for the detail you need, offer a reason or build a plan that considers different ideas. Open questions can invite explanation; closed questions can check a detail.

Visitor role-play: “I grow vegetables.” Ask “What do you grow?” Adult replies, “Beans.”

Try it · Ask a follow-up based on that reply. Then try a yes/no question that checks a detail.

For example, “How do you look after the beans?” and “Do you water them each day?” are connected. Listen to the actual answer before the next turn. Accept other relevant questions; do not judge a question only by its first word.

Common mistakes

“Eye contact proves listening.” Check understanding through a relevant reply, restatement or question.

“Everyone must speak on command.” Allow thinking time, different communication modes and a choice to pass.

“Louder fixes every unclear message.” Check wording, pace, noise and the listener’s needs; avoid shouting.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Agree on a reading place

With a partner, choose a place to read. One wants quiet and the other wants room to sit together. Take turns, respond to both needs, ask a useful question and check the agreed plan. At home, the partner can be an adult.

Adjust the challenge Starter: offer a topic and one talk stem. Core: exchange turns, make a clear message and give a related reply. Stretch: combine two suggestions or respectfully disagree with a reason and a follow-up.

Observe the real exchange and note context, communication mode and prompts. Accent, dialect, eye contact and stillness are not behaviour scores. Written choices alone cannot establish conversation performance.

Three tricky questions

1. Must you agree to respond well?

No. A different view can be helpful when it is connected, respectful and explained.

2. Is every pause an invitation to interrupt?

No. The speaker may be thinking. Wait or check whether the turn is finished.

3. Can a short yes/no question be useful?

Yes. It can check a missing detail; open questions can invite a fuller explanation.

FAQ

Can signing or AAC support participation?

Yes. Record how meaning was communicated and any speech-specific skill that was not observed.

How do we tell whether someone listened?

Look for a response that uses the partner’s meaning, including a relevant clarification question.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: I can take turns, communicate clearly, show understanding and build a conversation with ideas and questions.

Start together: Use one focus at a time. Model a short exchange, practise with a classmate or adult, then reduce help. Try both everyday talk and a planned group discussion.

Useful words: Topic: what we talk about. Turn: time to share a message. Listen: try to understand. Reply: respond to a message. Question: ask for information.

1. Take turns

A conversation is an exchange: one person shares, another listens and responds. A short pause may mean someone is thinking, not that they have finished. Agree on a useful turn signal and leave time for a reply.

Wait, check, then reply

Mia says, “My tower fell because…” and pauses. Jo waits. Mia adds, “…the bottom was too small.” Jo asks, “Can we make a wider bottom?”

Work it through

Jo waits for Mia’s complete idea, then adds a connected question. If both begin together, one can pause and say “You go first” or “Sorry, please finish.”

Try it together: Practise a three-turn exchange about a game with a partner. What will you do if you start at the same time?

See answer and teaching guidance

Observe an actual exchange: the child gives a turn, responds to the partner and repairs overlap when needed. A suitable repair is “Please finish; I will wait.” A partner can be a classmate or an adult.

Watch for this: Waiting silently is not the whole skill. Listen for meaning and respond to what was shared.

2. Speak clearly

Help the listener understand your message. Use words that name what you mean, a pace they can follow and a volume suited to the place. Pause between ideas. If the message is missed, repeat, reword or use an agreed communication support.

Repair an unclear message

“Put it there” does not tell Jo which object or place. Try: “Put the blue pencil beside the book.”

Work it through

Naming the blue pencil and the book gives Jo enough information. Ask “Was that clear?” If Jo did not hear, reduce nearby noise or move closer with permission, then repeat at a comfortable pace.

Try it together: Give a partner one clear drawing direction, such as “Draw a circle above a square”. Ask what they understood.

See answer and teaching guidance

Observe whether the partner can follow the message. Accept a clear spoken, signed or AAC message. Note any speech-specific criterion that was not observed; a different accent is not an error.

Watch for this: Louder is not always clearer. Check words, pace, background noise and the listener’s needs; do not demand shouting.

On paper: Practice B has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

3. Listen actively

Listening actively means trying to understand. Show this through a relevant response, a restatement or a question about something missing. Looking a particular way or sitting still is not proof of understanding.

Check what you heard

A partner says, “Put the red block below the blue block.” You reply, “Red below blue. Is that right?”

Work it through

The reply keeps both the colour and position details, and checks the meaning. If “below” is unclear, ask “Do you mean underneath?” before acting.

Try it together: Tell a partner “Draw a small circle beside a large square”. Ask them to repeat the direction, clarify if needed, then draw.

See answer and teaching guidance

Look for the size and position details in the restatement and drawing. A clarification question can be useful evidence of listening. Repeat for access needs; record prompts that supply an answer separately.

Watch for this: Do not require eye contact, a motionless body or copying someone’s posture. Check understanding through the response.

On paper: Practice C has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

4. Respond to an idea

A reply connects to what someone has just contributed. You can add a detail, ask for an explanation, give useful feedback or respectfully offer a different view. You do not have to agree.

Add meaning to a reply

Sam says, “Let’s read outside.” Jo says, “The grass is wet. Could we sit on the covered seats?”

Work it through

Jo responds to Sam’s plan, names a practical problem and offers a connected alternative. “I like dinosaurs” would change the topic without building on the plan.

Try it together: Your partner wants a quiet place to read, and you want room to sit together. Suggest a plan that considers both ideas.

See answer and teaching guidance

For example, “We could sit together in the quiet corner.” Accept a feasible plan that connects both contributions. Observe the child listening and responding, not just choosing a written answer.

Watch for this: A different opinion can still be a helpful reply. Listen to the idea and explain your reason respectfully.

On paper: Practice D has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

5. Add a relevant idea or question

The topic is what the conversation is about. Add an idea that fits it, or ask a question that helps you understand or find out more. Some questions have a short answer; others invite a longer explanation.

Ask for what is missing

A visitor says, “I help at the library.” “When do you help?” asks about time. “How do you help?” asks about the job. “Why do you enjoy it?” asks for a reason. “Do you help on Mondays?” can have a short yes/no answer.

Work it through

Choose the question for the information you need. After the answer, use its meaning to choose your next question rather than reciting an unrelated list.

Try it together: Role-play a visitor who grows vegetables. Ask one question, listen to the answer, then ask a connected follow-up.

See answer and teaching guidance

For example: “What do you grow?” Answer: “Beans.” Follow-up: “How do you look after the beans?” Accept relevant alternatives. Open questions invite explanation; closed questions can help check a detail.

Watch for this: An on-topic question is useful even when it is short. Do not judge it only by its first word; check what information it asks for.

On paper: Practice E has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

6. Make discussion fair

Help everyone access the discussion. Offer a turn, allow thinking time and respect a choice to pass. People may speak, sign, point or use AAC (a communication tool). Give useful support without answering for them.

Choose together

Three children are choosing a game. Jo says, “Mia, would you like a turn, or would you like to pass?” They wait while Mia uses her communication board. Then they check which game each person prefers.

Work it through

The invitation makes space without forcing speech. Waiting allows Mia’s contribution. The group listens to different views and checks the decision rather than letting one person choose for everyone.

Try it together: In a small group, agree on a game or book. Invite a waiting partner and check the plan with everyone. At home, use a child and adult pair.

See answer and teaching guidance

Observe a fair invitation, time for a response, attention to different views and a checked decision. Record the communication mode and adult help. Do not count passing as a wrong answer.

Watch for this: Fair does not mean every person must use the same words or take the same amount of time. Support participation and choice.

On paper: Practice F has 10 questions. Teach and practise first, then use the separate test.

Teaching help: observe the child in conversation

Model, practise, then observe

  1. Model an exchange with a clear message, a thinking pause and a connected reply.
  2. Practise with an adult or peer. Give a stem or visual prompt if needed, then reduce help.
  3. Try everyday talk and a structured task such as choosing a book or explaining a drawing. Include a familiar teacher or visitor role-play.
  4. Observe a fresh exchange. Record what the child did, the partner/context, communication mode and prompts used.

Support: Give a short topic, thinking time and one stem such as “You said…” or “How…?” Allow signing, gesture, AAC and access adjustments without supplying the answer.

Core: Exchange turns, communicate a clear idea, show understanding and contribute a connected reply or question.

Extend within Year 1: Combine two suggestions, offer respectful disagreement with a reason, or ask a follow-up based on the partner’s answer.

Exit ticket

With a partner, choose a book to share. Listen to their choice, give a connected reply, add your idea or question and check the plan.

Expected evidence and next step

Look for a fair exchange, a response showing understanding, an understandable message and a relevant contribution. Accept different choices and communication modes. If the reply is unrelated, restate the partner’s idea and model one connected response, then try a new exchange. Record the prompt rather than calling the performance independent.

Keep evidence clear: The paid pack includes 18 observed Practice activities and five observed Test activities. Its other items check supporting knowledge and application. Written answers alone do not establish conversation performance. Do not score forced eye contact, stillness, accent, dialect or willingness to speak on command.

Boundary: Teach familiar Year 1 interaction, not formal debate, a speech diagnosis or a behaviour rating. Where speech is not observed, record the alternative communication and the unobserved speech criterion.

Key concept

Learning goal: I can take turns, listen actively and build a conversation with ideas and questions.

  • Take turns in pair and group talk.
  • Stay on topic, listen carefully and respond to what another person says.
  • Contribute ideas in class, group and pair discussions.
  • Interact appropriately with peers, teachers and visitors.
  • Ask useful open, closed, when, why and how questions.

Common misconceptions
  • Waiting only to talk — Listen for the idea before responding.
  • Too quiet/fast — Use a pace and volume suited to the listener and context, or an agreed communication support.
  • New idea ignores partner — Connect to what was already said.

Explain and apply

Take a fair turn

A partner is explaining an idea. What should you do before you add your idea?

Answer and teaching guidance

Listen to the idea and wait for a suitable turn before responding. Eye contact is not required; show understanding through a connected reply or question.

Build on an idea

Sam says, I think the map is useful. Give a reply that builds on Sam's idea.

Answer and teaching guidance

A strong reply links to Sam's idea, such as I agree because it helps us find the library.

Ask a helpful question

A speaker says, I chose this animal for my report. What question would help the discussion continue?

Answer and teaching guidance

A helpful question asks for more detail, such as Why did you choose that animal?

Speak clearly

What can you change if classmates cannot hear your answer?

Answer and teaching guidance

Check the words, pace, volume and background noise. Repeat or reword the message, or use an agreed communication support. Judge whether the listener can understand; record the support used.
