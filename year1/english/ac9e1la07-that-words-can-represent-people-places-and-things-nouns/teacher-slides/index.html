Ready to project and teach

Teach it step by step

Work through these six teaching focuses in short sessions. Read, model, try and explain; return to any focus that needs more support.

Words to teach: Nouns name people, places and things, including animals. Pronouns can refer to someone or something without repeating a name. Verbs tell happenings or states. Adjectives describe qualities. Adverbs can add when, where or how details.

1. Nouns: name what is involved

Find the naming word in its sentence. Animals are included among the things nouns can name.

Model together

“The koala sleeps.” Koala names the animal. “We visit the library.” Library names a place.

Your turn: Find the two nouns in “The child carries a bag.”

Answer and teaching guidance

Child and bag. Read the sentence rather than guessing from the word’s position.

2. Pronouns: keep the meaning clear

Use a clear person or thing for the pronoun to refer to. Do not guess someone’s pronouns from a name or picture.

Model together

“Sam uses they. Sam waved. They smiled.” They refers to Sam. “The books fell. They landed on the mat.” They refers to the books.

Your turn: You and a friend are speaking together. Replace the repeated group name: “Mia and I are ready.”

Answer and teaching guidance

We are ready. We includes the speaker and another person. Other pronouns depend on the supplied speaker, group or referent.

3. Verbs: happenings and states

Ask what happens or what something is like. Include doing, saying, thinking and being.

Model together

“The bird hops.” Hops tells an action. “The bird is small.” Is is a being verb; small describes the bird.

Your turn: Find the verb in “The child thinks about lunch.”

Answer and teaching guidance

Thinks. Thinking is a process even though the picture may not show movement. In “The box is empty”, is is the verb.

4. Adjectives: qualities

Ask which word describes the person or thing. Use visible or supplied information when describing a picture.

Model together

“A soft blanket.” Soft tells a quality of the blanket. “The empty basket.” Empty describes the basket.

Your turn: In “The wide gate opened”, what does wide describe?

Answer and teaching guidance

Gate. Wide is the adjective. A monochrome picture alone cannot prove a colour or that something feels soft or is heavy.

5. Adverbs and other details

Ask when, where or how. Some details are one adverb; others are groups of words. Not every adverb ends in -ly.

Model together

“The ducks swim slowly outside today.” Slowly tells how, outside tells where, today tells when. “Beside the reeds” is a group of words giving a place detail.

Your turn: Which word tells when in “The class walks quietly outside today”?

Answer and teaching guidance

Today. Quietly tells how and outside tells where. Do not label every detail phrase as one adverb word.

6. Make a sentence more specific

Choose useful qualities, details and a more exact verb. Keep the meaning sensible.

Model together

“The child moved.” → “The child tiptoed quietly beside the cot.” Tiptoed is more exact; quietly tells how; beside the cot tells where.

Your turn: Improve “The dog went” with a more exact verb and one useful detail.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, The dog trotted outside. Accept another sensible precise verb and detail. Ask what the change helps the reader understand, not only which label it has.

Support, extend and check learning

Watch for this

A word’s job depends on its sentence. Compare “We water the garden” with “The water is cold”: water is a verb in the first and a noun in the second. Teach the familiar meanings first. Keep pronoun references clear, include being verbs, and judge added details by what they mean.

Support

Read the whole sentence aloud. Ask one job question at a time. Use a short incomplete frame and a small word bank; accept pointing, oral answers or scribed sentence creation.

Extend within this skill

Compare a sentence before and after adding a useful quality or exact verb. Explain the new meaning, then check a word that changes its job between two sentences.

Quick check

In “The small bird sang softly outside”, identify the naming word, verb, describing word and two details. Then use a clear pronoun in a follow-up sentence.

Expected evidence and next step

Bird is the noun; sang the verb; small the adjective; softly tells how and outside where. For example, It flew away clearly refers to the bird. Accept another suitable follow-up and explain its reference.

If the explanation is unclear, model a different example and try again. Record what the child can explain or do, and the support used; a paper score alone does not establish an observed speaking or performance skill.

An adult can read the directions and explain taught terms. Accept spoken, signed, pointed, AAC or scribed responses when these preserve the skill being checked. Short directions do not assume every child can independently decode all the words.

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Words have different jobs: nouns name people, places or things; verbs show happenings or states; adjectives describe; adverbs add detail such as when, where or how.

Learning target: understand that words can represent people, places and things (nouns, including pronouns), happenings and states (verbs), qualities (adjectives) and details such as when, where and how (adverbs)

Big idea: Notice what jobs words do in sentences

Learning routine: Read sentence → Find word → Ask its job → Name word class

See the idea: Use the job of a word in its sentence

Use the job of a word in its sentence

Work through the example

  1. Read “The small bird sang.” Bird names the thing; small describes it; sang tells what happened.
  2. Words can do different jobs in different sentences. Read the whole sentence first.
  3. A verb may describe a state, not only movement: “The bird is small.”

Watch for this: Do not decide a word’s job from its position alone.

Try it together

In “The green frog jumps”, which word describes the frog?

Answer and teaching guidance

Green. Frog names it; jumps tells what it does.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: understanding that words or groups of words can represent the participants (nouns; for example, people, places, things) that are involved in various activities or processes (verbs of doing, saying, thinking, being) and the details or circumstances surrounding the activity (adjectives and adverbs that answer “when?”, “where?”, “how?”)
  • E2: recognising how a sentence can be made more specific by adding adjectives, adverbs and precise verbs

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Word meaning only: Ask what job the word does in the sentence.
  • Adjective as noun: A describing word needs something to describe.
  • Missing verbs: Find what happens or what is.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment
