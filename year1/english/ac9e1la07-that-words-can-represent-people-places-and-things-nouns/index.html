YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Explore the jobs words do in a sentence AC9E1LA07

Learning intention: I can explain naming words, verbs, qualities and details using the whole sentence.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One sentence, several word jobs

“The small bird sang softly outside.” Bird names the animal, sang tells the happening, small describes the bird, softly tells how and outside tells where. Read the whole sentence before naming a word’s job. A word can do a different job in a different sentence.

Learn the skill

1. Name participants and keep pronouns clear

Nouns name people, places, animals and things. Pronouns can refer to a noun or group. Make the reference clear; do not guess a person’s pronouns from a name or picture.

“The books fell. They landed on the mat.” / “Jo and I are ready.”

Try it · What does They refer to? Replace Jo and I with one pronoun.

They refers to the books. We can replace Jo and I because it includes the speaker and another person. The sentence supplies the reference.

2. Find doing, thinking and being verbs

A verb can tell a happening or state, including saying, thinking and being. It is not always an action you can see in a picture.

“The puppy runs.” / “The puppy is tired.” / “The child thinks.”

Try it · Find the verb in each sentence.

Runs, is and thinks are the verbs. Tired describes the puppy’s state; it is not the being verb in that sentence.

3. Add useful qualities and details

Adjectives describe qualities of nouns. Adverbs can add when, where or how; some details use groups of words instead. A more exact verb can also improve a sentence.

“The ducks swim slowly outside today.” / “The small ducks paddle beside the reeds.”

Try it · Which words tell how, where and when in the first sentence? What does small describe?

Slowly tells how, outside where and today when. Small describes the ducks. Beside the reeds is a group giving a place detail, not one adverb word. Paddle makes the movement more specific than swim in this context.

Common mistakes

“A word’s position always tells its job.” Read its meaning in that particular sentence.

“All adverbs end in -ly.” Today and outside can add details too.

“Every detail group is one adverb.” Keep single words and groups distinct.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make the meaning more specific

Begin with “The child moved.” Choose a more exact verb such as tiptoed and a relevant detail. Explain what the change tells a reader. Then compare “We water the garden” with “The water is cold”.

Adjust the challenge Starter: find one named word job in a supplied sentence. Core: identify naming, verb, quality and detail meanings in context. Stretch: explain how water changes its job across the two sentences and create a clearer sentence.

An adult can read or scribe. Use actual or supplied facts for descriptions; a monochrome picture cannot establish colour, weight or texture. Accept clear pronoun references without guessing gender.

Three tricky questions

1. Is water always a noun?

No. It is a verb in “We water the garden” and a noun in “The water is cold”.

2. Is softly a quality of the bird in “The bird sang softly”?

It tells how the bird sang. Small in “The small bird” describes the bird.

3. Can a thinking word be a verb?

Yes. Thinks and hopes can tell processes without visible movement.

FAQ

Do we need advanced word-class labels?

Use the familiar noun, pronoun, verb, adjective and adverb labels with meaningful examples. Avoid sorting isolated ambiguous words by guesswork.

What makes a sentence improvement useful?

It adds accurate meaning: a precise verb, quality or relevant when, where or how detail. More words alone do not guarantee clarity.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: Nouns name people, places and things, including animals. Pronouns can refer to someone or something without repeating a name. Verbs tell happenings or states. Adjectives describe qualities. Adverbs can add when, where or how details.

1. Nouns: name what is involved

Find the naming word in its sentence. Animals are included among the things nouns can name.

Example

“The koala sleeps.” Koala names the animal. “We visit the library.” Library names a place.

Try it: Find the two nouns in “The child carries a bag.”

See answer and explanation

Child and bag. Read the sentence rather than guessing from the word’s position.

2. Pronouns: keep the meaning clear

Use a clear person or thing for the pronoun to refer to. Do not guess someone’s pronouns from a name or picture.

Example

“Sam uses they. Sam waved. They smiled.” They refers to Sam. “The books fell. They landed on the mat.” They refers to the books.

Try it: You and a friend are speaking together. Replace the repeated group name: “Mia and I are ready.”

See answer and explanation

We are ready. We includes the speaker and another person. Other pronouns depend on the supplied speaker, group or referent.

3. Verbs: happenings and states

Ask what happens or what something is like. Include doing, saying, thinking and being.

Example

“The bird hops.” Hops tells an action. “The bird is small.” Is is a being verb; small describes the bird.

Try it: Find the verb in “The child thinks about lunch.”

See answer and explanation

Thinks. Thinking is a process even though the picture may not show movement. In “The box is empty”, is is the verb.

4. Adjectives: qualities

Ask which word describes the person or thing. Use visible or supplied information when describing a picture.

Example

“A soft blanket.” Soft tells a quality of the blanket. “The empty basket.” Empty describes the basket.

Try it: In “The wide gate opened”, what does wide describe?

See answer and explanation

Gate. Wide is the adjective. A monochrome picture alone cannot prove a colour or that something feels soft or is heavy.

5. Adverbs and other details

Ask when, where or how. Some details are one adverb; others are groups of words. Not every adverb ends in -ly.

Example

“The ducks swim slowly outside today.” Slowly tells how, outside tells where, today tells when. “Beside the reeds” is a group of words giving a place detail.

Try it: Which word tells when in “The class walks quietly outside today”?

See answer and explanation

Today. Quietly tells how and outside tells where. Do not label every detail phrase as one adverb word.

6. Make a sentence more specific

Choose useful qualities, details and a more exact verb. Keep the meaning sensible.

Example

“The child moved.” → “The child tiptoed quietly beside the cot.” Tiptoed is more exact; quietly tells how; beside the cot tells where.

Try it: Improve “The dog went” with a more exact verb and one useful detail.

See answer and explanation

For example, The dog trotted outside. Accept another sensible precise verb and detail. Ask what the change helps the reader understand, not only which label it has.

Try a worked example together

Use the job of a word in its sentence

Work through the example

  1. Read “The small bird sang.” Bird names the thing; small describes it; sang tells what happened.
  2. Words can do different jobs in different sentences. Read the whole sentence first.
  3. A verb may describe a state, not only movement: “The bird is small.”

Watch for this: Do not decide a word’s job from its position alone.

Try it together

In “The green frog jumps”, which word describes the frog?

Answer and teaching guidance

Green. Frog names it; jumps tells what it does.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A word’s job depends on its sentence. Compare “We water the garden” with “The water is cold”: water is a verb in the first and a noun in the second. Teach the familiar meanings first. Keep pronoun references clear, include being verbs, and judge added details by what they mean.

Check: In “The small bird sang softly outside”, identify the naming word, verb, describing word and two details. Then use a clear pronoun in a follow-up sentence.

See answer and explanation

Bird is the noun; sang the verb; small the adjective; softly tells how and outside where. For example, It flew away clearly refers to the bird. Accept another suitable follow-up and explain its reference.

Help: Read the whole sentence aloud. Ask one job question at a time. Use a short incomplete frame and a small word bank; accept pointing, oral answers or scribed sentence creation.

Next challenge: Compare a sentence before and after adding a useful quality or exact verb. Explain the new meaning, then check a word that changes its job between two sentences.
