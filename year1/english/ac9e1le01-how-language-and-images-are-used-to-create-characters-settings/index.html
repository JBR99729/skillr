YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Notice how words and pictures build a story AC9E1LE01

Learning intention: I can explain a word choice and a picture clue that help create a character, setting or event.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Words tell an action; the picture adds a place

The words “Mae read” name the action. In the supplied illustration, the open book fits that action and shelves of books suggest a library setting. The plant adds a detail the sentence does not name. We can explain those visible choices without inventing the book’s title or Mae’s thoughts.

Learn the skill

1. Link words with visible clues

Read the words and inspect the actual picture. Say what each contributes. Authors choose language; illustrators choose images, and sometimes one creator does both jobs.

Words: “Mae read.” Use the illustration directly below.

Try it · What does the picture add that these two words leave out?

The shelves of books suggest a library; the plant is another visible detail. Explain the contribution rather than only naming the illustrator’s job.

2. Explain a character or setting choice

Actions and speech can suggest a quality. Place and time words create a setting. Keep a supported interpretation separate from a fact.

Original story: “At dusk, Pip the duck gave a scarf to a shivering rabbit. ‘You can use mine,’ said Pip.” Dusk means the light is fading near the end of the day.

Try it · Which choice suggests that Pip is helpful? What human-like action has the writer given the duck?

Giving the scarf and offering its use suggest help. Speaking and lending clothing are human-like story actions, rather than evidence about how real ducks behave. At dusk gives a time clue; no place is named.

3. Compare real creators’ choices

Use actual picture books across First Nations Australian, other Australian and world creators. Compare a specific word or picture choice and explain its effect. One invented example does not replace this reading.

Adult supplies lawful illustrated copies of Little Bird’s Day (Sally Morgan and Johnny Warrkatja Malibirr), Time for Bed (Mem Fox and Jane Dyer), and Dear Zoo (Rod Campbell), or verified equivalent selections. Read and show the actual pages first.

Try it · Choose two actual pages. What does a word choice help you imagine, and what does the picture add?

Accept an explanation tied to visible details and real wording in each selected book. Identify title, author and illustrator. Different supported responses are welcome; do not infer cultural symbolism, reproduce artwork or treat one book as representing a whole culture.

Common mistakes

“The picture proves every thought.” Describe the visible clue and say what it may suggest.

“A real animal always acts like its story character.” Talking or wearing clothes may be an author’s imaginative choice.

“Knowing the creator’s name completes the discussion.” Explain what the actual language and image choices do.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare one moment, then another

Use two actual pages from the adult-supplied books named above. Name a character, place or event. Point to one language choice and one image detail on each page, then discuss a similarity or difference. Revisit the third creator context in another short session.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult reads a page and offers two visible clues. Core: explain what a word and image each add. Stretch: compare how two creators build a similar moment, using evidence from both.

The published books and their artwork are not included. Follow publisher and community guidance. Record the actual discussion and support; a correct written choice cannot establish this literature experience.

Three tricky questions

1. Can one person be author and illustrator?

Yes. Explain the two jobs without assuming two different people must do them.

2. Does a moon prove an exact clock time?

No. It may suggest night in context, but an exact time needs more information.

3. Must all readers describe a character in the same way?

No. Different interpretations can be reasonable when the actual words and pictures support them.

FAQ

Can an adult read the book aloud?

Yes. The child can discuss the language and pictures without decoding every word independently.

Are the original mini-stories enough for this whole code?

They support the skill. Actual illustrated literature across the required creator contexts is also needed.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: Explore how authors and illustrators build characters, places and events.

Useful words: A character is a person, animal or other being in a story. Setting means where and when. An event is something that happens. An author writes; an illustrator makes the pictures.

1. Words and pictures introduce story meaning

Read the words and look at the illustration together. Each can give information the other does not. Name the clue before explaining what it suggests.

Example

The worksheet words say, “Mae read.” The picture shows an open book and shelves of books. Together, the words and picture help us imagine the moment.

Try it: What place do the shelves of books suggest? What do the words tell Mae is doing?

See answer and explanation

The shelves suggest a library. The words tell us Mae is reading. Point to the visible book and shelves; the picture does not establish every detail about her feelings.

2. Character clues and story animals

Notice what a character does and says. These choices can help us understand the character. Pictures may add expression, posture or action clues.

Example

Two children share crayons in the worksheet picture. A story could say, “Lea passed a crayon to her friend.” The action can suggest kindness.

Try it: Which action in the picture could suggest kindness? Explain.

See answer and explanation

Passing or sharing a crayon can suggest kindness because it helps another child join in. Point to the action. Accept a supported interpretation; one smile does not prove every feeling.

3. Setting: where and when

Find words and image details that show where or when events happen. Explain how they help you picture the place.

Example

“At night, Jo stood on the beach.” Beach names the place and at night tells when. The worksheet illustration adds a shoreline and a moon.

Try it: Which word names the place? Which word helps tell when?

See answer and explanation

Beach names the place; at night tells when. The moon and shoreline are matching picture clues. The picture alone does not tell Jo’s name or an exact clock time.

4. Events and story sequence

Follow what happens first, next and last. Words such as then and pictures of changes can help us connect events.

Example

“A gust blew the hat away. Then Sam caught it. Sam put it back on.” Gust means a short burst of wind.

Try it: What happens between the hat blowing away and Sam putting it on?

See answer and explanation

Sam catches it. Explain the change using the supplied sequence. An event is something that happens; the setting names where or when it happens.

5. Picture clues add to the words

Ask what an illustration adds: a place, a movement, an expression or a detail. A picture can also create a playful mismatch with the words.

Example

The words say, “Mae read.” The picture also shows shelves of books and a plant. These visible details are not named in the short sentence.

Try it: Name one detail the picture adds that the words “Mae read” leave out.

See answer and explanation

The bookshelves suggest the place is a library; a plant is another added detail. Point to the visible clue. The picture alone does not tell the book’s title or what Mae is thinking.

6. Compare stories and illustrators respectfully

Read books by First Nations Australian creators, other Australian creators and world creators. Compare a specific choice in their words or pictures. Stories and artistic styles can differ within each group.

Example

In two picture books shared by an adult, find a page where a character helps someone. Compare the action words and one visible picture clue.

Try it: Tell a partner one way the two pages build a helpful character.

See answer and explanation

The child points to actual words and picture details in each book and discusses what they suggest. Record the title, author and illustrator. Do not label a generic animal picture as First Nations literature or invent cultural meanings.

Prepare three actual illustrated books

Adult supplies lawful illustrated copies of Little Bird’s Day by Sally Morgan and Johnny Warrkatja Malibirr, Time for Bed by Mem Fox and Jane Dyer, and Dear Zoo by Rod Campbell, or verified equivalent selections across First Nations Australian, other Australian and world creators. The published text and artwork are not included. Identify author and illustrator; read the whole books and show the actual spreads. Follow publisher/community guidance; do not guess cultural symbolism or imitate artwork.

Discuss the actual choices, then compare

Little Bird’s Day: compare an opening daytime spread with a later night spread; explain how real words and visible details create setting and connected events. Time for Bed: hear a returning word pattern and discuss what an actual image adds about the animals or care. Dear Zoo: inspect a real closed and opened flap, listen to the actual describing word and explain the reveal; check any event-order link in the copy rather than testing an unverified animal sequence. Compare one genuine word or image choice from two books and explain its effect. Use all three creator contexts over time; a creator-name quiz is not a substitute. Accept different supported responses and note the help used.

Use story details to explain a character

Work through the example

  1. The story tells an action: Sam shared the apple.
  2. That detail supports the idea that Sam may be kind.
  3. An illustration can add more clues about the character, place and event. Check the actual picture rather than imagining missing details.

Watch for this: Describe a character using evidence from the story, rather than appearance alone.

Try it together

Which story detail supports “Sam may be kind”?

Answer and teaching guidance

Sharing the last apple with a friend. Link the character idea to the action.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A character’s feeling is an interpretation supported by clues. A setting is not the same as an event. An illustration can add meaning rather than simply repeat the words.

Check: With an adult, discuss a picture-book page. Name a character, the setting and an event. Point to one word choice and one image detail and explain what each adds.
