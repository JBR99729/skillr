Learn the skill

Six small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

Learning goal: Explore how authors and illustrators build characters, places and events.

Useful words: A character is a person, animal or other being in a story. Setting means where and when. An event is something that happens. An author writes; an illustrator makes the pictures.

1. Words and pictures introduce story meaning

Read the words and look at the illustration together. Each can give information the other does not. Name the clue before explaining what it suggests.

Example

The worksheet words say, “Mae read.” The picture shows an open book and shelves of books. Together, the words and picture help us imagine the moment.

Try it: What place do the shelves of books suggest? What do the words tell Mae is doing?

See answer and explanation

The shelves suggest a library. The words tell us Mae is reading. Point to the visible book and shelves; the picture does not establish every detail about her feelings.

2. Character clues and story animals

Notice what a character does and says. These choices can help us understand the character. Pictures may add expression, posture or action clues.

Example

Two children share crayons in the worksheet picture. A story could say, “Lea passed a crayon to her friend.” The action can suggest kindness.

Try it: Which action in the picture could suggest kindness? Explain.

See answer and explanation

Passing or sharing a crayon can suggest kindness because it helps another child join in. Point to the action. Accept a supported interpretation; one smile does not prove every feeling.

3. Setting: where and when

Find words and image details that show where or when events happen. Explain how they help you picture the place.

Example

“At night, Jo stood on the beach.” Beach names the place and at night tells when. The worksheet illustration adds a shoreline and a moon.

Try it: Which word names the place? Which word helps tell when?

See answer and explanation

Beach names the place; at night tells when. The moon and shoreline are matching picture clues. The picture alone does not tell Jo’s name or an exact clock time.

4. Events and story sequence

Follow what happens first, next and last. Words such as then and pictures of changes can help us connect events.

Example

“A gust blew the hat away. Then Sam caught it. Sam put it back on.” Gust means a short burst of wind.

Try it: What happens between the hat blowing away and Sam putting it on?

See answer and explanation

Sam catches it. Explain the change using the supplied sequence. An event is something that happens; the setting names where or when it happens.

5. Picture clues add to the words

Ask what an illustration adds: a place, a movement, an expression or a detail. A picture can also create a playful mismatch with the words.

Example

The words say, “Mae read.” The picture also shows shelves of books and a plant. These visible details are not named in the short sentence.

Try it: Name one detail the picture adds that the words “Mae read” leave out.

See answer and explanation

The bookshelves suggest the place is a library; a plant is another added detail. Point to the visible clue. The picture alone does not tell the book’s title or what Mae is thinking.

6. Compare stories and illustrators respectfully

Read books by First Nations Australian creators, other Australian creators and world creators. Compare a specific choice in their words or pictures. Stories and artistic styles can differ within each group.

Example

In two picture books shared by an adult, find a page where a character helps someone. Compare the action words and one visible picture clue.

Try it: Tell a partner one way the two pages build a helpful character.

See answer and explanation

The child points to actual words and picture details in each book and discusses what they suggest. Record the title, author and illustrator. Do not label a generic animal picture as First Nations literature or invent cultural meanings.

See the idea: Use story details to explain a character

Use story details to explain a character

Work through the example

  1. The story tells an action: Sam shared the apple.
  2. That detail supports the idea that Sam may be kind.
  3. An illustration can add more clues about the character, place and event. Check the actual picture rather than imagining missing details.

Watch for this: Describe a character using evidence from the story, rather than appearance alone.

Try it together

Which story detail supports “Sam may be kind”?

Answer and teaching guidance

Sharing the last apple with a friend. Link the character idea to the action.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A character’s feeling is an interpretation supported by clues. A setting is not the same as an event. An illustration can add meaning rather than simply repeat the words.

Check: With an adult, discuss a picture-book page. Name a character, the setting and an event. Point to one word choice and one image detail and explain what each adds.

See answer and explanation

Listen to the actual discussion and check the cited words and image details. Use several books over time to cover the required range of creators; one original mini-example does not establish the whole code.

Help: Read the page aloud, point to two possible clues and model “The words say … The picture shows …”. Accept oral, signed, AAC or scribed responses.

Next challenge: Compare how two illustrators show a similar place or character action. Explain what the different choices help the reader notice.

Bring picture books for the full lesson
