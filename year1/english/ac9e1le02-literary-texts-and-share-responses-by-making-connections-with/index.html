YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Connect a story with your own experience AC9E1LE02

Learning intention: I can share a response to a literary text and explain a safe connection to my own experience.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A connection explains a response

Original story: “Nia waited for the swing. At last it was her turn. She grinned.” A reader might connect this with waiting for a game: both involve finally getting a turn. That memory can help explain a response to Nia; it does not tell us new facts about her.

Learn the skill

1. Start with a real text detail

Name what happens before adding your memory. Ask a question about the character, setting or event and check what the words answer.

Original story: “Tom dropped his pencil. Ada lent him one. Tom smiled.”

Try it · Who helps Tom, and which words tell you? What could you ask about the event?

Ada helps: “Ada lent him one.” A question such as “Why might Tom smile?” invites a supported inference. His exact thoughts and the pencil’s colour are not stated.

2. Explain the shared experience

A useful connection links an event, feeling or idea with a safe experience you choose to share. Explain the shared part; the details need not match exactly.

Story: “Lena tried again when her tower fell.” Model connection: “I kept trying a puzzle when it was hard.”

Try it · What do the two experiences share? Make a safe connection of your own if you wish.

Both involve continuing after difficulty. Accept a relevant voluntary own-experience connection and explanation. An imagined example supports participation when needed, but does not demonstrate a personal memory.

3. Share and listen to different responses

A reader may enjoy a moment that worries another reader. Point back to the text and explain your response, then listen and ask a related question.

Original poem: “Rain taps the path. I splash and spin. My socks get wet. I hurry in.”

Try it · Which part interests you? Draw or act it, then explain a safe connection and ask your partner about their response.

Accept a response linked to a stated line and a relevant voluntary experience. A child may like splashing or dislike wet socks. Observe the actual exchange; a drawing alone does not show discussion. The voice saying I is the poem’s speaker, not necessarily its author.

Common mistakes

“My memory changes what happened.” Keep the story’s details and your own experience separate.

“We must all feel the same.” Explain different responses with relevant details.

“I must share something private.” Choose an ordinary safe memory or decline; an adult can support a later comfortable connection.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Think, connect, share

Read the complete rain poem above with a partner. Choose one line, explain your response and connect it with a safe experience. Listen to a different response and ask one related question. A drawing or role-play may support the explanation.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult rereads one line and offers a connection stem. Core: link a text detail with a voluntary experience and explain. Stretch: compare two responses or discuss how the same memory connects differently with two actual texts.

Never require private disclosure or agreement. An adult may read, sign or scribe. Record actual discussion and the assistance used; supported participation is not independent reading.

Three tricky questions

1. Is owning a pencil always a useful connection to a helping story?

It is only a surface match unless you explain how it helps you understand the event or your response.

2. Can similar experiences have different details?

Yes. Waiting for a swing and waiting for a game can share the experience of waiting for a turn.

3. Does I in a poem prove the author experienced it?

No. The speaker can be imagined. Use what the text actually gives.

FAQ

Does there have to be one correct personal answer?

No. Accept different relevant connections with an explanation.

What if the child has no matching memory?

Use a safe known or imagined example for participation, and revisit an own-experience connection when appropriate. Record that boundary honestly.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: Help children share a thoughtful response to a story or poem.

Useful words: A response is what we think, feel or say about a text. A connection links a text detail with our own experience. A memory is something we remember.

1. Make a relevant connection

First name a detail from the text. Link it with a safe experience of your own. A useful connection helps explain your response, rather than just matching a colour or object.

Example

Original mini-story: “Nia waited for the swing. At last it was her turn. She grinned.” A reader might say, “I waited for a turn at a game. I felt glad when my turn came.”

Try it: Which link helps us understand Nia: waiting for a turn at a game, or owning a green pencil? Why?

See answer and explanation

Waiting for a turn connects the experience. The pencil does not explain this event. A child’s own relevant experience may be different from the model; no one memory is required.

2. Notice story details first

Ask a question about the character, place or event. Check the answer in the text before making a connection. Keep the story detail and your own memory separate.

Example

In Nia’s mini-story, “At last it was her turn” tells us she gets a turn. It does not say how many children were waiting.

Try it: Did Nia get a turn? What words tell us? What could you ask about the event?

See answer and explanation

Yes: “At last it was her turn.” A relevant question might be “What did Nia do when her turn came?” If the answer is not supplied, say we do not know. A memory cannot prove an unstated story fact.

Complete story for this discussion

Original mini-story: “Nia waited for the swing. At last it was her turn. She grinned.” For the comparison, the reader waits for a turn at a game. Keep the shared experience and differing activities clear.

3. Compare with care

Your experience can be similar without being identical. Explain both the shared part and a difference. Your feelings do not replace the character’s feelings.

Example

Nia waits for a swing. The reader waits for a game. Both wait for a turn, but the activities differ.

Try it: Name one thing that is alike and one thing that differs in those experiences.

See answer and explanation

Alike: waiting for a turn. Different: a swing versus a game. Accept another accurate comparison. Do not require identical places, objects or feelings.

Complete story for this discussion

Original mini-story: “Nia waited for the swing. At last it was her turn. She grinned.” For the comparison, the reader waits for a turn at a game. Keep the shared experience and differing activities clear.

4. Use a connection to understand

Explain how the memory helps you respond to the character or event. Use because to make the link clear.

Example

“I understand Nia’s glad feeling because I remember finally getting a turn.” That adds an explanation to the connection.

Try it: Finish using a safe memory of your own: “I understand waiting because …”.

See answer and explanation

Accept a relevant, voluntary memory and an explanation of its link. If the child has no matching experience or prefers not to share, discuss an imagined example for participation and revisit a safe own-experience connection later; the imagined example is not proof of personal experience.

5. Keep the response relevant

A response can be different from someone else’s. Point to what in the poem or story prompted it. A drawing or role-play can help you explain.

Example

Original mini-poem: “Rain on the roof. Rain on my shoe. I hop in a puddle. What will you do?” One reader remembers enjoying puddles; another remembers keeping their socks dry.

Try it: Which line could you draw or act out? Then explain your own response to that part.

See answer and explanation

For example, draw hopping in a puddle and name the matching line. Accept a different feeling or preference with a relevant connection. The speaker saying I is the voice in the poem; it is not automatically the author. Drawing without an explanation does not show the full discussion skill.

6. Share responses respectfully

Think first, then share with a partner. Listen to their response, ask a related question or add a connected idea. You can disagree respectfully.

Example

“You liked the puddle part. I felt worried about wet socks because mine got wet once. What did you like about the puddle?”

Try it: Discuss the rain poem with a partner. Add one related response or question after listening.

See answer and explanation

Observe the actual exchange. Look for a text detail, a safe own-experience link and a response to the partner. Accept speech, signing, AAC or supported communication. Do not score agreement or private disclosure.

See the idea: Explain your connection to a story

Explain your connection to a story

Work through the example

  1. Name the story event you are responding to.
  2. Connect it to something you have experienced or know about.
  3. Explain how the connection helps you understand the story. You may respond differently from a partner.

Watch for this: Your experience helps interpretation; it does not change what happened in the story.

Try it together

Share a connection to a familiar story, or explain why an event interests you.

Answer and teaching guidance

Accept a relevant connection or thoughtful response, with a link to the text. Children need not disclose private experiences.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: A retell alone is not a personal response. Your memory does not change the text. Similar does not mean identical, and readers do not have to agree.

Check: Choose a part of a short story or poem. Share a safe connection to your own experience and explain your response. Listen to a partner and ask or answer a related question.

See answer and explanation

Observe an accurate text detail, a relevant voluntary own-experience link and actual discussion. Different feelings can be valid. A worksheet selection alone cannot establish discussion; an imagined example supports participation but does not confirm an own-experience connection.

Help: Read a short complete text aloud and offer “In the story … I remember … This helps me understand …”. Let the child choose a safe everyday memory or decline to share.

Next challenge: Connect to two different texts and explain how the same experience helps you respond differently. Keep each connection tied to the text.

Who is telling the story?

In the Nia mini-story, a narrator tells us about Nia. In the rain poem, a speaker says “I”. That voice may be a character or an imagined speaker; it does not prove who the author is. Ask what the voice helps us notice, without expecting an untaught narrator label.
