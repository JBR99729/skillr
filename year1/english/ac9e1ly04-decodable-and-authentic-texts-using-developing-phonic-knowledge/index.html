Read the words, check and reread AC9E1LY04

Learning intention: I can use taught letter–sound knowledge, check the printed meaning and reread with clear phrasing.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The letters identify the word

Printed sentence: “The frog hops.” If a reader says “The flag hops”, return to the letters in frog: /f/ /r/ /o/ /g/. Blend the sounds, then reread the printed sentence and check its meaning. A picture can explain what a frog is; it cannot replace reading the word’s spelling.

Learn the skill

1. Blend taught patterns in order

Use the letter–sound patterns already taught to the child. A digraph can use two letters for one sound. Blend through the whole word, then connect it with the sentence meaning.

Adult model: shop → /sh/ /o/ /p/. Fresh printed text: “The fish swims.” Adult teaches any unfamiliar word or spelling first.

Try it · Read fish. Which letters work together? Then read the whole short sentence.

The letters sh represent one sound in fish: /f/ /i/ /sh/. Listen to actual word and sentence reading. Supply help for untaught patterns, then try a suitable fresh example rather than judging unsupported decoding.

2. Read meaningful phrases and monitor

First read accurately, then group words that belong together. Punctuation helps show boundaries and expression. Use grammar and meaning to notice a mismatch, then return to the actual spelling.

Adult model: “The little dog / ran home.” The slash marks a practice phrase group, not punctuation. Fresh printed text: “Can the dog jump?”

Try it · Read the fresh question aloud. If you say bump instead of jump, how will you check it?

Read the question with natural phrasing. Return to jump and check j and the remaining letters, then reread. A plausible word is not enough: read the word that is printed. Do not insist on one exaggerated voice.

3. Correct, seek help and reread

When reading breaks down, check the letters and blend again. If needed, ask for help. Reread for accurate words, a suitable pace and meaning rather than racing. Authentic texts may contain untaught words needing adult support.

Original classroom notice with a real purpose: “Please close the gate. The dog is in the yard.” Adult supplies untaught words such as please before supported reading.

Try it · Read the notice with the support you need. What does it ask you to do? Then reread without changing the printed words.

It asks the reader to close the gate. Observe the actual supported reading, corrections, phrasing and meaning. Note supplied words and other help. Adult read-aloud comprehension or one word choice cannot establish independent fluency.

Common mistakes

“A picture tells me which word to guess.” Look through the letters and blend; use the picture to support meaning.

“Fastest reading is best.” Keep accurate words, meaningful phrases, a suitable pace and understanding.

“A supported notice is independently decoded.” Record help and assess taught-pattern reading separately.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

First accurate, then smooth

Use the printed original text “The fish swims. The dog can jump.” Confirm or teach the needed correspondences and words first. Listen to a first reading, help with a specific difficulty, then invite a reread and a brief explanation of what each animal does.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult models a word, blends with the child and supports a short phrase. Core: read suitable taught-pattern text, correct a mismatch and reread for meaning. Stretch: read a slightly longer familiar text or supported authentic notice with clear phrasing.

Match texts to the child’s taught phonics and explain new vocabulary. Familiar high-frequency words still need their known spelling parts and any unusual parts taught. Observe reading itself, using appropriate access support and recording what was supplied.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a correct picture choice prove reading?

No. It may show recognition or meaning. Hear or otherwise observe the child reading the actual printed text.

2. What if a guessed word makes sense?

Check all the letters anyway. More than one word can be plausible, but the spelling identifies the printed word.

3. Is asking for help a failure?

No. It is a useful strategy when checking and rereading do not resolve a difficulty. Record the support and practise the needed pattern.

FAQ

Is every Year 1 text independently decodable for every child?

No. Match the text with what that child has been taught; support untaught patterns and words.

Do grammar and context replace phonics?

No. Use them to monitor and check meaning while reading and correcting the actual printed words.

Answer and teaching guidance

Hear the child read the word and sentence. Supply help with the taught letter–sound correspondences when needed.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Teach each strand: model, practise and check

Choose a short text with letter–sound patterns the child has been taught. Explain unfamiliar vocabulary before reading. A decodable text practises those patterns; an authentic text has a real purpose, such as a classroom notice or a book. An adult may support unfamiliar words in an authentic text.

1. Blend sounds in order

Point to shop. Say /sh/ /o/ /p/, then blend the sounds into the whole word. The two letters sh work together to represent one sound.

Try: Read fish. Which two letters work together?

Answer and teaching guidance

sh represents one sound. Blend /f/ /i/ /sh/. If the child names letters without blending, model the sounds and slide them together.

2. Read meaningful phrases

Read The little dog / ran home. The slash shows a useful phrase group for practice; it is not printed punctuation. First solve unfamiliar words, then reread the words together so the sentence sounds like speech.

Check: Who ran home? Listen for the child keeping The little dog together. Answer: the little dog.

3. Let punctuation guide your voice

Read Can the dog jump? as a question. Read Stop! with an urgent voice, without shouting. A full stop marks the end of a sentence; take a natural pause before the next sentence.

Try: Read The dog can jump. and Can the dog jump? aloud. Listen for the child distinguishing a statement from a question.

4. Check all the letters

The printed word is frog. If you say flag, compare the middle letters: r and o. Read /f/ /r/ /o/ /g/, then blend. Check the beginning, middle and end; a first letter alone does not identify a word.

5. Check grammar and meaning after decoding

The printed sentence is The dog runs. A reader says The dog run. Return to runs, notice the final s, and reread. The spelling tells you the word; the sentence helps you check that it sounds right and makes sense.

Try: You read The dog jumps. as The dog bumps. Both words can be words, but the printed word starts with j. Return to the letters instead of choosing a word that merely seems plausible.

6. Reread for fluency

Read The fish swims. First read accurately, then reread in a meaningful phrase. Explain what the fish does. Fluency includes accuracy, suitable pace and expression; racing through a sentence is not the goal.

Adult check: Listen to both readings. Notice whether the child keeps the words correct, groups them smoothly and explains that the fish swims. Give brief help, then invite another reading.

High-frequency words and asking for help

Practise familiar words such as the and is in short sentences. Use the known letter–sound parts and explicitly teach any unusual part. If blending and rereading still do not resolve a word, the child can ask an adult for help, then read the sentence again.

Support, core and extend

  • Support: model one word, blend together and echo-read a short phrase.
  • Core: read a short taught-pattern text, correct a mismatch and reread with meaning.
  • Extend: reread a slightly longer familiar text or an adult-supported classroom notice, keeping accurate words, phrasing and meaning.

Exit check

Listen to the child read The fish swims. Ask them to explain what the fish does, check the letters after an error, and reread the sentence smoothly. Expect /f/ /i/ /sh/, the correct printed words and an explanation of swimming. Reteach the particular sound or word that caused difficulty before trying again.

Common misconceptions
  • Only guessing from context — Look at the letters and sounds.
  • Sounding without meaning — After decoding, check the sentence makes sense.
  • Robot reading — Practise phrase groups, not one word at a time forever.

Explain and apply

Blend and check meaning

Read: The frog sat on a log. Which two words rhyme? Read their letters first, then explain how the sentence helps you check the meaning.

Answer and teaching guidance

Frog and log rhyme. Read their printed letters and blend first; the sentence then helps you check the meaning. A rhyming guess alone does not identify a printed word.

Use phonics first

You see the word shop. Which sounds help you read it?

Answer and teaching guidance

The digraph sh and the sounds /o/ and /p/ help the reader blend the word.

Read in phrases

Where is a good pause: The little dog / ran home or The / little dog ran home?

Answer and teaching guidance

The little dog / ran home keeps words in meaningful phrases and supports fluency.

Self-correct

The printed sentence is “The frog hops.” You accidentally read “The flag hops.” What should you do?

Answer and teaching guidance

Stop and return to the printed letters. Blend the sounds to check the word, then reread and check meaning; do not guess the word from the picture.
