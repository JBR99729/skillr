Key concept

Learning goal: I can use a text's purpose and organisation to work out how it is structured.

  • Compare what familiar texts are trying to do.
  • Recognise common stages in texts such as recounts and procedures.
  • Notice that words, pictures and diagrams can work together to organise information.

Steps to use

Identify purpose → Notice structure → Find key features → Explain

Teach it step by step

Work through these six teaching focuses in short sessions. Read, model, try and explain; return to any focus that needs more support.

Words to teach: A text has a job. Recount: tell what happened. Story: tell connected events. Opinion: say what you think. Facts: information we can check.

1. Recount: what happened

A recount often starts with who, when and where, then puts events in order.

Model together

“On Monday our class went to the school garden. First we looked at the beds. Next we watered them. Last we went back inside.”

Your turn: Which happened before watering: looking at the beds or going back inside?

Answer and teaching guidance

Looking at the beds. First and next help show the order. A recount can retell a real or imagined experience; do not use real-versus-made-up as the only test.

2. Narrative: a story

Follow who is in the story and how the events connect. Beginning, middle and end help us organise a short story.

Model together

“A puppy found an empty bowl. It carried the bowl to Jo. Jo filled it with water.” Each event leads to the next.

Your turn: What could happen if the last event came first?

Answer and teaching guidance

The reader would hear the bowl was filled before knowing why Jo filled it. Discuss the sequence; not every story needs the same problem-and-solution pattern.

3. Opinion: a view and a reason

State the view, then give a reason that explains it.

Model together

“I think our garden needs a shady seat because we could sit there after watering.”

Your turn: Point to the view and the reason.

Answer and teaching guidance

View: the garden needs a shady seat. Reason: people could sit there after watering. Because connects them.

4. Inform: share facts

A heading names the topic. Words, pictures and labels can work together to give information.

Model together

An information card headed “Our garden” says: “The gate is beside the tap.” A labelled plan can show the same places.

Your turn: Would a labelled plan or a list of everyone’s favourite games help readers find the tap? Why?

Answer and teaching guidance

The labelled plan helps show where the tap is. The game list does a different job.

5. Report: group related facts

A report is an information text. It often groups related facts under headings instead of telling one event in time order.

Model together

“Tools: We use a trowel and a watering can. Jobs: We dig small holes and water the beds.”

Your turn: Under which heading would “We sweep the path” fit: Tools or Jobs?

Answer and teaching guidance

Jobs. Sweeping is something people do. Headings help readers find a group of related information.

6. Explain and compare with instructions

An explanation tells how or why something happens. A procedure tells the reader how to do something. Both may use a sequence, but their jobs differ.

Model together

Explanation: “Water freezes when it becomes cold enough. It changes into ice.” Procedure: “1. Put water in a tray. 2. Place it in the freezer.”

Your turn: Which text tells you what to do? Which tells you about the change?

Answer and teaching guidance

The numbered procedure tells the reader what to do; the explanation describes the change. Steps, arrows and pictures can organise either, so check the message as well as the feature.

Support, extend and check learning

Watch for this

Topic and purpose differ: two texts about the same garden can have different jobs. A heading, a time word or a picture is a clue, not proof of one text type. Informing is a broad purpose; a report is one way to organise information. Use short texts and supplied pictures rather than relying on a child’s knowledge of an unfamiliar book.

Support

Read the text aloud, then offer two purpose choices. Use separate event cards to rehearse the order. Keep recount, report and explanation terms supported by the examples.

Extend within this skill

Keep one topic and change its job: write a two-sentence recount and an opinion with a reason. Explain what you changed.

Quick check

Compare the garden recount with the grouped Tools/Jobs report. Say one difference in their organisation and why it helps the reader.

Expected evidence and next step

The recount follows events in time order to tell what happened. The report groups related facts so readers can find information. Accept equivalent child language and a pointed example from each text.

If the explanation is unclear, model a different example and try again. Record what the child can explain or do, and the support used; a paper score alone does not establish an observed speaking or performance skill.

An adult can read the directions and explain taught terms. Accept spoken, signed, pointed, AAC or scribed responses when these preserve the skill being checked. Short directions do not assume every child can independently decode all the words.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: discussing and comparing the purposes and organisation of familiar texts
  • E2: becoming familiar with the typical stages of types of texts; for example, recount and procedure
  • E3: recognising that the structure of a text may include words and pictures; for example, an informative text may include words, illustrations and diagrams
See the idea: Notice how the purpose shapes the text

Notice how the purpose shapes the text

Work through the example

  1. This text tells someone how to make a card.
  2. Its numbered actions show the order to follow.
  3. A recount would instead tell what already happened. A story may introduce characters and a problem.

Watch for this: Purpose and organisation work together; a title alone is not always enough to identify the purpose.

Try it together

Why would changing the order of a procedure sometimes cause a problem?

Answer and teaching guidance

Some actions depend on earlier steps. Explain a concrete example, such as folding before drawing the front of a card.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common misconceptions
  • Topic = purpose — Ask what the text is doing, not only what it is about.
  • One structure for all texts — Match structure to purpose.
  • No evidence — Point to a heading, time word, fact or opinion word.
Try these
  1. Purpose detective — Look at three short text examples and name the purpose.
    recount / story / information
  2. Structure sort — Match text features to purpose.
    heading → information text
  3. Change the purpose — Turn one event into a recount sentence and an opinion sentence.
    We went to the park. / I liked the park because...
Explain and apply

Purpose decides organisation

Compare a recount, report and instruction. What order would each one usually use?

Answer and teaching guidance

A recount often follows time order, a report groups information, and instructions use steps in order.

Find the organising clue

A page has First, Next and Finally. What purpose might those words support?

Answer and teaching guidance

Those words support a sequence, often recounting an event or explaining instructions.

Match text to job

Which text would use facts under headings: a report about frogs or a personal recount of recess?

Answer and teaching guidance

A report about frogs is more likely to group facts under headings. The organisation fits the purpose to inform.

Explain the choice

Complete: This text is organised with ____ because it is made to ____.

Answer and teaching guidance

The learner should connect a feature such as headings, time words or steps to a purpose such as inform, recount, explain or instruct.

Check your understanding

Quick checks

  • How can time words help organise a recount or instructions?
  • What purpose gives facts?
  • Name one feature of an opinion text.
  • How is a story organised?

  • I can notice that words, pictures and diagrams can work together to organise information.
