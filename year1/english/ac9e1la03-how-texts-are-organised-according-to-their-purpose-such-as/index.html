YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Notice how a text’s job shapes its order AC9E1LA03

Learning intention: I can compare short texts and explain how their order or grouping helps the reader.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

One garden, two different organisations

Recount: “Yesterday we visited the garden. First we watered the beds. Then we picked beans.” Report: “Tools: a trowel and watering can. Jobs: watering and weeding.” The recount follows an event in time. The report groups information under headings. The topic is the same, but the organisation serves different purposes.

Learn the skill

1. Follow connected events

A recount retells events, often using time order. A narrative tells a story with connected characters and events. Look at the whole text rather than deciding from one time word.

“A puppy found an empty bowl. It carried the bowl to Jo. Jo filled it with water.”

Try it · Why does the last event make sense after the first two?

The empty bowl and the puppy carrying it help us understand why Jo fills it. Moving the last event first would change how the story is revealed. Not every narrative needs the same problem-and-solution pattern.

2. Find information grouped for a job

Headings group related details so a reader can find information. A report is one kind of information text. Pictures, labels and diagrams may contribute to its organisation.

Garden report: Tools — trowel, watering can. Jobs — dig holes, water plants. New detail: sweep the path.

Try it · Where does the new detail belong, and why?

Under Jobs, because sweeping is an activity. The heading helps the reader find that kind of information without reading an event sequence.

3. Compare a view, an explanation and instructions

An opinion gives a view and may support it with a reason. An explanation tells how or why something happens. A procedure tells a reader what to do. Sequence alone does not decide the purpose.

Opinion: “I think we need shade because it helps us rest comfortably.” Explanation: “Water can freeze when it becomes cold enough.” Procedure: “Pour water into a tray. Put it in a freezer.”

Try it · Which directs actions, and which explains a change?

The procedure directs the actions. The explanation describes how water can change into ice. The opinion gives a view with a reason. Read the words and their organisation together.

Common mistakes

“Texts about the same topic have the same job.” Compare what each text helps its reader do.

“A heading proves it is a report.” Read the whole short text and how its information is grouped.

“Every sequence is a procedure.” A recount or explanation can also use an order.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

One topic, two jobs

Use a familiar class event. Tell a short recount with first and next, then state an opinion about the event with a reason. An adult scribes both texts. Compare what came first and what each text helps a listener understand.

Adjust the challenge Starter: sort two complete texts into their stated jobs. Core: explain the time order or grouping in a text. Stretch: keep a topic and change the text’s purpose, explaining the organisation you changed.

Supply complete texts and read them aloud when decoding is not the target. Do not require knowledge of an unseen book or treat another curriculum’s year label as an Australian equivalence.

Three tricky questions

1. Is a report the opposite of an information text?

No. A report is one way to organise information, often grouping facts about a topic.

2. Can a recount describe an imagined experience?

Yes. Real versus invented is not the only way to identify the organisation and purpose.

3. Do pictures only decorate a text’s structure?

No. Labels, diagrams or ordered images can help organise and communicate its information.

FAQ

Does every narrative need a problem?

No. Teach the connected events in the supplied story rather than impose one universal template.

What should an adult ask?

Ask “What is this text helping its reader do?” and “How does its order or grouping help?” Look for evidence in the complete text.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Useful words: A text has a job. Recount: tell what happened. Story: tell connected events. Opinion: say what you think. Facts: information we can check.

1. Recount: what happened

A recount often starts with who, when and where, then puts events in order.

Example

“On Monday our class went to the school garden. First we looked at the beds. Next we watered them. Last we went back inside.”

Try it: Which happened before watering: looking at the beds or going back inside?

See answer and explanation

Looking at the beds. First and next help show the order. A recount can retell a real or imagined experience; do not use real-versus-made-up as the only test.

2. Narrative: a story

Follow who is in the story and how the events connect. Beginning, middle and end help us organise a short story.

Example

“A puppy found an empty bowl. It carried the bowl to Jo. Jo filled it with water.” Each event leads to the next.

Try it: What could happen if the last event came first?

See answer and explanation

The reader would hear the bowl was filled before knowing why Jo filled it. Discuss the sequence; not every story needs the same problem-and-solution pattern.

3. Opinion: a view and a reason

State the view, then give a reason that explains it.

Example

“I think our garden needs a shady seat because we could sit there after watering.”

Try it: Point to the view and the reason.

See answer and explanation

View: the garden needs a shady seat. Reason: people could sit there after watering. Because connects them.

4. Inform: share facts

A heading names the topic. Words, pictures and labels can work together to give information.

Example

An information card headed “Our garden” says: “The gate is beside the tap.” A labelled plan can show the same places.

Try it: Would a labelled plan or a list of everyone’s favourite games help readers find the tap? Why?

See answer and explanation

The labelled plan helps show where the tap is. The game list does a different job.

5. Report: group related facts

A report is an information text. It often groups related facts under headings instead of telling one event in time order.

Example

“Tools: We use a trowel and a watering can. Jobs: We dig small holes and water the beds.”

Try it: Under which heading would “We sweep the path” fit: Tools or Jobs?

See answer and explanation

Jobs. Sweeping is something people do. Headings help readers find a group of related information.

6. Explain and compare with instructions

An explanation tells how or why something happens. A procedure tells the reader how to do something. Both may use a sequence, but their jobs differ.

Example

Explanation: “Water freezes when it becomes cold enough. It changes into ice.” Procedure: “1. Put water in a tray. 2. Place it in the freezer.”

Try it: Which text tells you what to do? Which tells you about the change?

See answer and explanation

The numbered procedure tells the reader what to do; the explanation describes the change. Steps, arrows and pictures can organise either, so check the message as well as the feature.

Work through the example

  1. This text tells someone how to make a card.
  2. Its numbered actions show the order to follow.
  3. A recount would instead tell what already happened. A story may introduce characters and a problem.

Watch for this: Purpose and organisation work together; a title alone is not always enough to identify the purpose.

Try it together

Why would changing the order of a procedure sometimes cause a problem?

Answer and teaching guidance

Some actions depend on earlier steps. Explain a concrete example, such as folding before drawing the front of a card.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Check learning and offer support

Watch for this: Topic and purpose differ: two texts about the same garden can have different jobs. A heading, a time word or a picture is a clue, not proof of one text type. Informing is a broad purpose; a report is one way to organise information. Use short texts and supplied pictures rather than relying on a child’s knowledge of an unfamiliar book.

Check: Compare the garden recount with the grouped Tools/Jobs report. Say one difference in their organisation and why it helps the reader.

See answer and explanation

The recount follows events in time order to tell what happened. The report groups related facts so readers can find information. Accept equivalent child language and a pointed example from each text.

Help: Read the text aloud, then offer two purpose choices. Use separate event cards to rehearse the order. Keep recount, report and explanation terms supported by the examples.

Next challenge: Keep one topic and change its job: write a two-sentence recount and an opinion with a reason. Explain what you changed.
