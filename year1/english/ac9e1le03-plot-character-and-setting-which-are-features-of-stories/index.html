YEAR 1 ENGLISH

Talk about who, where and what happens AC9E1LE03

Learning intention: I can discuss characters, settings and connected events using clues from a story.

An adult can read and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A solution needs to address the problem

Original story: “After lunch, Tia took seedlings into the yard. Her watering can leaked. Dad brought a jug, and Tia watered the seedlings.” Tia and Dad are characters; the yard after lunch is the setting. The leak creates a problem, and the jug helps solve it by holding water.

Learn the skill

1. Separate the story features

Ask who is involved, where and when it happens, and what happens. A character can be an animal or imagined being that takes part. Do not turn every named object into a character.

Original story: “On Saturday morning, Bo returned a book to the library. The librarian thanked Bo.”

Try it · Name a character, the place, the time and an event.

Bo and the librarian are characters. The place is the library and the time is Saturday morning. Returning the book or thanking Bo is an event. The book is an object in this story.

2. Connect the problem, action and result

A problem gets in the way of a goal. Explain why an action helps. Some stories give a sequence without a stated problem; do not invent one.

A: “Ari could not reach the ball under a bench. Jo rolled it out with a stick. Ari picked it up.” B: “Mia picked a flower, placed it in a jar and put the jar on a table.”

Try it · What solves A’s problem? Is a problem stated in B?

Rolling the ball out makes it reachable. B tells connected events without a stated problem. Keep each retelling in its own order rather than forcing both into one pattern.

3. Compare places and character choices

Use actual clues when comparing stories, narrative poems or fables. A familiar place can contain imagined events. A character’s action may affect what happens next.

A: “In a city garden, a mouse thanked a talking tree for shelter.” B: “On a country path, a child thanked a neighbour for an umbrella.”

Try it · What is similar? What differs? Which detail is imagined?

Both show thanks for help with shelter. The characters, places and helping objects differ. A talking tree is an imagined feature; the garden is a familiar kind of place. No season or exact year is given. Later compare an actual adult-supplied traditional tale or fable with another familiar story.

Common mistakes

“A setting is something that happens.” A setting tells where or when; an event tells what happens.

“Every ending solves every problem.” Check whether the action addresses the obstacle.

“Every story must state a problem.” Discuss the connected events the actual text gives.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make a story map and discuss it

Adult reads this original story: “Before breakfast, Lee took a kite into the yard. Its ribbon caught on a chair. Lee loosened the ribbon and flew the kite.” Use three headings: Who? Where/when? What happens? Retell the events and explain how Lee’s action helps.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult rereads and offers two feature choices. Core: name features and show their clues. Stretch: explain a cause or compare features in two actual familiar literary texts.

Accept spoken, signed, AAC, drawn or scribed explanations. Use actual complete texts for comparisons. Assess discussion of features separately from handwriting and independent decoding.

Three tricky questions

1. Does Saturday morning tell where?

No. It tells when. A place clue answers where.

2. Can a realistic setting appear in a fantasy?

Yes. A talking tree can appear in a city garden; discuss each actual clue.

3. Is an object always a character?

No. Check its role. An imagined speaking book could be a character, while a book being returned can simply be an object.

FAQ

Must a child use formal plot terms?

No. Who, where, when and what happens are useful Year 1 language.

Can there be more than one useful retelling?

Yes. Accept faithful main events and relevant extra details when their order and connections remain clear.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Useful words and learning goals

Learning goal: Discuss who is in a story, where and when it happens, and how its events fit together.

How to use this guide: Read a whole story, model one focus, try it together and ask for the clue. These six learning focuses match Practice A–F in the worksheet pack; choose the section the child needs rather than completing everything at once.

I can: name characters and the place/time; follow connected events; explain an idea with a story clue; compare story features and discuss them with someone.

Useful words: Character: a person, animal or other story participant. Setting: where and when events happen. Plot: the connected events of a story. Problem: what goes wrong. Solution: how it is resolved.

1. Find the setting

The setting tells us where and when a story happens. Read or hear the whole short story first. Then find the place and time clues. Sometimes a story gives only one of them; say what you know without filling in missing details.

Read together

After lunch, Ruby waited beside the school gate. A tiny dragon landed on her bag. Ruby held out a biscuit, and the dragon nibbled it.

Work it through

  1. Ask “Where?” The words beside the school gate tell the place.
  2. Ask “When?” After lunch tells the time; an exact clock time is not needed.
  3. Check realistic or imagined details. A school gate is familiar, but a tiny dragon makes this an imagined story.

Try it together: Name the setting. Does “Ruby” tell us who or where? What makes the story imagined?

See answer and explanation

The setting is beside the school gate, after lunch. Ruby is a character: her name answers who. The tiny dragon is the imagined detail. The text gives no season or exact clock time.

Watch for this: A character’s name is not a place. “Ruby waited” is an event, not the whole setting. Ask where and when separately.

Listen and look for: The child points to or repeats each relevant clue and can distinguish a familiar place from the imagined creature.

On paper: Continue with Practice A in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

2. Know story features

Character asks who is involved. Setting asks where and when. Plot asks what happens and how the events connect. A story map helps keep these questions apart; it is a way to talk about a story, not a requirement to write a new one.

Read together

At the library on Saturday morning, Finn found a dropped book. He gave it to the librarian. She thanked him.

Work it through

  1. Read the complete story. Name the participants: Finn and the librarian.
  2. Find the place and time: the library, Saturday morning.
  3. Follow the events: Finn finds the book, gives it to the librarian, then receives thanks.

Try it together: Put “the library”, “Finn” and “gave the book to the librarian” under who, where or what happened.

See answer and explanation

Who: Finn. Where: the library. What happened: Finn gave the book to the librarian. “Saturday morning” adds when. The book is an object in this story, not a speaking or acting character.

3. Find the problem

In many stories a character wants something, but an obstacle gets in the way. That is a problem. A solution is an action that addresses it. Some stories describe connected events without a problem; do not force one into every text.

Read together

Lila wanted to water the seedlings. The watering can had a hole, so the water leaked out. Dad brought a jug. Lila used it to water the seedlings.

Work it through

  1. Find the goal: Lila wants to water the seedlings.
  2. Find what stops her: water leaks from the can.
  3. Connect the solution to the problem: a jug holds the water so she can pour it onto the seedlings.

Try it together: Would moving the leaky can to another shelf solve the problem? Why does the jug help?

See answer and explanation

Moving the can does not stop the leak or water the seedlings. The jug can hold the water and lets Lila finish the job. Name how the action helps rather than choosing any event near the ending.

Another kind of story: “Zoe picked a flower, put it in a jar and placed the jar on the table.” These events form a short sequence. No problem is stated.

Watch for this: The problem is the leak, not the garden setting. A different action is a solution only if it addresses what went wrong.

Listen and look for: The child links the goal, obstacle and helping action, using “because” or an equivalent explanation.

On paper: Continue with Practice C in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

4. Follow plot order

Track the main events in the order they happen. Words such as first, next and finally can help, but actions also give clues: a result usually follows the action that causes it. Keep a retell faithful to the story.

Read together

Milo put soil in a pot. He planted a seed in the soil. Then he watered it.

Work it through

  1. Identify the starting action: Milo puts soil in the pot.
  2. Find what depends on it: the seed is planted in that soil.
  3. Place watering after planting, as the story states. Tell the sequence as first, next and finally.

Try it together: A retell says, “First he watered the planted seed. Next he put soil in the pot. Finally he planted the seed.” Repair it. Which action belongs in the middle?

See answer and explanation

First he puts soil in the pot; next he plants the seed; finally he waters it. Planting is the middle action. The faulty retell puts watering a planted seed before that seed is planted. Use this story’s clues; do not claim plants can only ever be watered in one order.

Watch for this: The order of mixed cards is not the story order. Reread the whole text and connect each action to what must already have happened.

Listen and look for: The child orders three events, spots the reversed retell and explains a dependency rather than only naming a sequence word.

On paper: Continue with Practice D in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

5. Notice character choices

What a character does and says can suggest a feeling or quality. Say “may feel” when the text leaves room for more than one interpretation. Give the clue. One action does not tell us everything about a person.

Read together

When the class game began, Hana noticed a child standing alone. “You can join our team,” she said, and moved over to make space.

Work it through

  1. Find the action: Hana makes space.
  2. Find the speech: she invites the child to join.
  3. Connect them to an idea: this choice is welcoming because it includes someone standing alone.

Try it together: What might Hana’s choice suggest? Which words or action support your idea? Do we know for certain how the other child feels?

See answer and explanation

Hana’s choice may suggest kindness or friendliness: she invites the child and makes space. Other supported descriptions are welcome. We do not know the other child’s feeling for certain; the story does not state a reaction.

Watch for this: Do not use a face, name or favourite colour as proof of a trait. Return to the stated action or speech. Separate a possible feeling from a fact.

Listen and look for: The child offers an interpretation and a matching clue, and accepts another sensible interpretation when its evidence fits.

On paper: Continue with Practice E in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

6. Link story features

Talk about characters, setting and plot together. Ask how a place, time or character choice affects the next event. Compare what is alike and different in two complete short texts before extending to a shared tale or fable.

Read together

Original story: Rain began at the playground. Ari put the chalk under the shelter so the drawings could continue there.
Original narrative poem:
Rain on the street,
Wet little feet.
Bea finds a doorway,
A dry place to stay.

Work it through

  1. Name who and where in each text: Ari at a playground; Bea on a street. No time of day is stated.
  2. Find the shared situation: rain affects what each character does.
  3. Compare the actions and outcomes: Ari moves chalk under shelter; Bea finds a dry doorway. The poem uses short lines and rhyme, but both texts tell connected events.

Try it together: How does the rain affect the plot in each text? What is similar, and what is different?

See answer and explanation

In both, rain leads to seeking shelter. Ari acts to keep a drawing activity going; Bea acts to find a dry place. The characters, places and exact actions differ. The poem’s street/feet rhyme is a form difference; it does not replace discussing the events.

Shared-reading follow-up: Choose an age-appropriate complete traditional tale or fable from a lawful, attributed source. Compare its characters, place/time and event pattern with another familiar story. Ask whether a problem is stated and how its ending follows the earlier events.

Watch for this: A comparison needs both texts. “They are the same” is too broad; name a shared feature and a difference, with clues.

Listen and look for: The child discusses a connection between setting and action, refers to each text and responds to a partner’s question.

On paper: Continue with Practice F in the matching worksheet pack. Model first, reduce help as the child is ready, and revisit this focus before the separate test.

Teaching help: model, support and check

Start: Read a complete short story aloud. Ask the child to tell what happened before separating its features. Use three cards headed who, where/when and what happened; no special materials are needed.

Support: Offer two choices, reread the clue and accept pointing, speech, signing, AAC, drawing or adult scribing. Note the help given. Reading and spelling difficulty should not hide an understanding of story features.

Core: Ask the child to name a feature and show the clue. Invite a short explanation of how an action affects the next event.

Extend within Year 1: Compare two familiar texts, repair a retell or discuss two possible feelings using different clues. Do not require formal plot terminology or a full written story.

Partner talk: Think first, share one idea, then ask or answer “Which part tells you that?” Listen for a relevant reply. A paper answer alone cannot show participation in a real discussion.

Quick check: discuss a fresh story

Original story: Before breakfast, Mae took her puppy into the yard. The puppy’s ball rolled behind a box. Mae moved the box and the puppy picked up its ball.

  1. Who is involved? Where and when?
  2. What goes wrong, and which action addresses it?
  3. Tell the three main events in order. Explain how Mae’s choice affects the ending.
  4. Ask a partner a question about one story feature and respond to their reply.
See answer and explanation

Mae and her puppy are the characters. The setting is the yard, before breakfast. The ball rolls behind a box; Mae moves the box so the puppy can retrieve it. A useful three-event retell is: the ball rolls behind the box, Mae moves the box, the puppy picks up the ball. Taking the puppy into the yard introduces the scene and can also be included. Accept a faithful longer retell. Observe the actual partner exchange and the evidence used.

Continue or revisit: Continue when the child distinguishes the features, gives relevant clues and discusses how events connect. If who/where are mixed, return to focus 2. If the retell is reversed, model focus 4 again. If a feeling is guessed without evidence, return to focus 5. Use another fresh story after support; this short check is not whole-year certification.

See the idea: Separate who, where and what happens

Separate who, where and what happens

Work through the example

  1. The character is who the story is about.
  2. The setting gives the place and may also tell when the story happens.
  3. The plot follows what happens. Losing the ball could introduce a problem to be solved.

Watch for this: The setting is not the event; “the park” and “losing the ball” tell different parts.

Try it together

Tell a possible next event that follows this problem.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, Sam looks beside the slide or asks a friend to help. Explain how the event follows losing the ball.

Common misconceptions
  • Character = any object — Characters usually act or feel in the story.
  • Setting forgotten — Ask where and when the story happens.
  • Plot as one event only — Track events across beginning, middle and end.
Try these
  1. Feature sort — Sort cards into character, setting and plot.
    dragon / cave / found treasure
  2. Plot chain — Put three events in order.
    first / next / finally
  3. Setting swap — Change the setting and discuss how the story might change.
    forest → city

Complete event cards for the plot chain

Mixed cards: “The fox found its map.” / “The fox dropped its map in the forest.” / “The fox searched beside a tree.” Put these supplied events in order: dropped, searched, found. For the setting swap, retell this same sequence in a city and adjust a linked place detail, such as searching beside a bench. Keep an imagined fox character and coherent events.

Explain and apply

Find plot problem and solution

Read: Mia lost a hat. At last, Mia found it. What was the problem and how was it solved?

Answer and teaching guidance

The problem is the lost hat; the solution is that Mia found it. Students should separate what went wrong from how it ended.

Character and setting

In A dog slept under a tree, who is the character and where is the setting?

Answer and teaching guidance

The dog is the character; under a tree is the setting clue.

Actions show character

A character shares the last apple. What could that action suggest about the character?

Answer and teaching guidance

The action may suggest the character is kind or generous. The answer needs the action as evidence.

Story feature map

Choose a short story and map its character, setting, problem, main event and ending.

Answer and teaching guidance

The map should use details from the selected story, not invented details from a different text.

Complete story for these quick checks

Original story: “At the park after lunch, Mina lost a ball. Jo found it beside a bench and returned it. Mina thanked Jo.” Mina and Jo are characters; the setting is the park after lunch. Losing, finding, returning and thanking are plot events. Retell them in the supplied order so the help explains the thanks.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E1LE03: discuss plot, character and setting, which are features of stories

  • recognising similar characters and settings in different types of literary texts; for example, traditional tales, narrative poems and fables
  • discussing whether features of settings including time (year, season) and place (country or city) are realistic or imagined
  • discussing how plots develop, including beginnings (orientation), how the problem (complication) is introduced and solved (resolution)
