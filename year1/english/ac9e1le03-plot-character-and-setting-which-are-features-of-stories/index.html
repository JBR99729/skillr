Learn the skill

Six small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

Learning goal: Discuss who is in a story, where and when it happens, and how its events fit together.

Useful words: Character: a person, animal or other story participant. Setting: where and when events happen. Plot: the connected events of a story. Problem: what goes wrong. Solution: how it is resolved.

1. Find the setting

Look for place and time clues. A setting can be realistic or imagined; use what the story says rather than a guess.

Example

Original mini-story: At sunrise, Ada carried a bucket to the beach. A tiny dragon warmed a shell beside her.

Try it: Where and when does this happen? Which detail makes this imagined?

See answer and explanation

The beach is the place; sunrise tells when. The dragon is an imagined detail. A story can combine a real-looking place with an imagined character.

2. Know story features

Keep who, where/when and what happens separate. Characters act or feel; the setting gives the context and the plot connects events.

Example

Ada and the dragon are characters. The beach at sunrise is the setting. Warming a shell is an event.

Try it: Sort these: Ada; the beach; the dragon warms a shell.

See answer and explanation

Character; setting; plot event. An ordinary bucket is an object here, not automatically a character. An object can be a character in another story if it acts as a participant.

3. Find the problem

A problem creates a difficulty for a character. A solution addresses it. Some short texts describe an event without giving a problem or a solution.

Example

Original mini-story: Leo’s kite caught in a low branch. He could not reach it. A neighbour lifted it down with a long stick.

Try it: What went wrong? Which action solved it?

See answer and explanation

The kite was caught and Leo could not reach it. Lifting it down solved that problem. Use the stated action; do not invent another ending.

4. Follow plot order

Retell connected main events in the order they happen. The beginning introduces the situation, the problem complicates it and the resolution tells how it ends.

Example

Leo flies the kite; it catches in a branch; the neighbour retrieves it.

Try it: Can retrieving the kite come before it gets stuck in this story? Explain.

See answer and explanation

No. Getting stuck creates the need to retrieve it. Track the story’s actual order, using first, next and finally as support.

5. Notice character choices

Describe what a character does and support an idea about their feelings or qualities with a text clue. A clue can support more than one sensible interpretation.

Example

Original mini-story: Noor saw a child sitting alone. She moved over and asked, “Would you like to play?”

Try it: What might Noor’s choice suggest? Give the clue.

See answer and explanation

She may be friendly or thoughtful because she invites the child to play. Accept another supported interpretation. The text does not tell us every feeling or intention.

6. Link story features

Explain how a character’s action or the setting affects an event. Compare similar features in prose, narrative poems, traditional tales or fables, using the actual texts.

Example

Original story: Rain began at the park, so Jo sheltered under a roof. Original mini-poem: Rain on the lane / Pat runs again / Under the shop / Pat comes to a stop.

Try it: What is alike in these texts? How does the rain affect the characters?

See answer and explanation

Both characters seek shelter from rain, in different places. The weather affects their actions. Use a suitable complete tale or fable to extend comparison; not every story has the same plot.

See the idea: Separate who, where and what happens

Separate who, where and what happens

Work through the example

  1. The character is who the story is about.
  2. The setting gives the place and may also tell when the story happens.
  3. The plot follows what happens. Losing the ball could introduce a problem to be solved.

Watch for this: The setting is not the event; “the park” and “losing the ball” tell different parts.

Try it together

Tell a possible next event that follows this problem.

Answer and teaching guidance

For example, Sam looks beside the slide or asks a friend to help. Explain how the event follows losing the ball.

Learning journey

What you’ll learn, strand by strand

This topic is taught through 6 focused strands. Each strand builds one part of the skill before learners move into independent practice.

  1. 1

    Characters and their roles

    Identify the people or animals in a story and describe what role they play.

  2. 2

    Character clues and evidence

    Use words and pictures to infer what a character is like, feels or wants.

  3. 3

    Settings and setting clues

    Identify where and when a story happens and use clues to describe the setting.

  4. 4

    Plot and event sequencing

    Recognise the main events and put them in the order they happen.

  5. 5

    How characters and settings shape events

    Explain how a character's choices or the setting can affect what happens next.

  6. 6

    Comparing stories and using story words

    Compare characters, settings or plots across stories using accurate literary vocabulary.

How to use this roadmap: learn the idea, study examples, explain your thinking, then practise and check mastery.

Key concept

  • Compare similar characters and settings across stories, poems and fables.
  • Decide whether a setting is realistic or imagined and use clues about time and place.
  • Follow how a plot begins, develops a problem and solves it.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: recognising similar characters and settings in different types of literary texts; for example, traditional tales, narrative poems and fables
  • E2: discussing whether features of settings including time (year, season) and place (country or city) are realistic or imagined
  • E3: discussing how plots develop, including beginnings (orientation), how the problem (complication) is introduced and solved (resolution)
Common misconceptions
  • Character = any object — Characters usually act or feel in the story.
  • Setting forgotten — Ask where and when the story happens.
  • Plot as one event only — Track events across beginning, middle and end.
Try these
  1. Feature sort — Sort cards into character, setting and plot.
    dragon / cave / found treasure
  2. Plot chain — Put three events in order.
    first / next / finally
  3. Setting swap — Change the setting and discuss how the story might change.
    forest → city
Explain and apply

Find plot problem and solution

Read: Mia lost a hat. At last, Mia found it. What was the problem and how was it solved?

Answer and teaching guidance

The problem is the lost hat; the solution is that Mia found it. Students should separate what went wrong from how it ended.

Character and setting

In A dog slept under a tree, who is the character and where is the setting?

Answer and teaching guidance

The dog is the character; under a tree is the setting clue. This matches IXL-style short evidence questions.

Actions show character

A character shares the last apple. What could that action suggest about the character?

Answer and teaching guidance

The action may suggest the character is kind or generous. The answer needs the action as evidence.

Story feature map

Choose a short story and map its character, setting, problem, main event and ending.

Answer and teaching guidance

The map should use details from the selected story, not invented details from a different text.
