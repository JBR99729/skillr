About This Year 1 Science Inquiry Topic

Scientific inquiry begins with curiosity. In this unit, students learn to ask questions about simple patterns and relationships they observe in the world around them.

Students explore familiar contexts including toy cars, push and pull forces, animal habitats, plant needs, gardens, ponds, weather and seasonal changes. They use prior experiences and observations to make simple predictions before carrying out investigations or making further observations.

The focus is not on producing perfect predictions. Students learn that a useful scientific prediction is based on what they have already observed, experienced or learned.

Learning Intentions

Students will learn to:

  • ask questions about things they observe;
  • identify simple patterns and relationships;
  • make predictions using prior experiences;
  • pose questions about pushes and pulls;
  • ask how animals meet their needs in habitats;
  • predict what plants need to stay healthy;
  • predict which living things may be found in a habitat;
  • predict seasonal changes in plants and temperature;
  • explain the reason for a prediction.

Teacher Guide

Recommended Materials

  • toy cars and ramps;
  • smooth and rough surfaces;
  • photographs of animals and habitats;
  • classroom plants or plant photographs;
  • garden and pond images;
  • seasonal photographs of the same location;
  • simple weather and temperature charts;
  • observation journals;
  • question cards and prediction sentence starters;
  • drawing and writing materials.

Suggested Teaching Sequence

  1. Introduce the difference between an observation and a question.
  2. Model how observations can lead to scientific questions.
  3. Discuss how experiences help people make predictions.
  4. Explore push and pull questions using toy cars.
  5. Ask questions about how animals meet their needs.
  6. Make and discuss predictions about plant needs.
  7. Predict living things likely to be found in habitats.
  8. Observe seasonal and temperature patterns.
  9. Record predictions before investigations or observations.
  10. Compare predictions with what was observed.

Teacher Questions

  • What did you notice?
  • What question could we ask about this?
  • Is there a pattern?
  • What do you think will happen?
  • What experience helped you make that prediction?
  • How could we find out?
  • What might change if we push harder?
  • What does this animal need in its habitat?
  • What might happen if this plant receives less water?
  • Which living things might we find here?
  • What may change next season?

Common Misconceptions

A prediction must be correct

Explain that a prediction is an idea about what may happen. It should be based on observations or experiences, but it does not have to be correct.

A scientific question can be answered with yes or no only

Encourage questions that can lead to observation, comparison or investigation, such as “What happens when…?” and “How does… affect…?”

All questions are predictions

Clarify that a question asks what students want to find out, while a prediction states what they think may happen.

Predictions are random guesses

Model how predictions use clues from previous experiences, observations and known patterns.

Year 1 Science Inquiry Activities

Activity 1: Observation to Question

Show students a familiar object, plant, animal photograph or weather image. Students record what they notice and turn one observation into a scientific question.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01

Activity 2: Toy Car Push Investigation

Students ask whether a toy car travels farther when pushed harder. They make a prediction before testing different pushes on the same surface.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E1

Activity 3: Surface and Distance Questions

Students compare a smooth and rough surface and pose questions about how each surface may affect the distance a toy car travels.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E1

Activity 4: Animal Habitat Question Cards

Students select an animal and pose questions about where it shelters, where it finds water and how it obtains food.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E2

Activity 5: What Does This Plant Need?

Students examine a plant and predict what may happen if it receives too little water, light or space.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E3

Activity 6: Garden Living Things Prediction

Before visiting or examining a garden, students predict the plants and animals they may observe and explain their reasons.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E4

Activity 7: Pond Habitat Prediction

Students use a pond image or local observation site to predict which living things may be present and what they might need to survive there.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E4

Activity 8: Seasonal Plant Changes

Students compare photographs of the same plant across seasons, identify a pattern and predict what it may look like in the next season.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E5

Activity 9: Temperature Pattern Prediction

Students examine a simple seasonal temperature chart and predict which season is likely to be warmer or cooler.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01_E5

Activity 10: Prediction Check

Students revisit an earlier prediction, compare it with what they observed and explain whether the evidence supported their original idea.

Curriculum connection: AC9S1I01

Differentiation

Support

  • use photographs, real objects and familiar contexts;
  • provide oral response options;
  • use question and prediction sentence starters;
  • allow students to draw their predictions;
  • model one question and prediction before independent work;
  • use paired discussion before whole-class sharing.

Extension

  • ask students to explain the evidence behind a prediction;
  • compare two possible predictions;
  • design a fair way to test a simple question;
  • identify variables that should stay the same;
  • record results in a simple table;
  • write a new question after observing the result.

Assessment Ideas

Observe whether the student can:

  • pose a relevant question about an observation;
  • identify a simple pattern or relationship;
  • make a prediction based on experience;
  • explain the reason for a prediction;
  • ask questions about pushes and pulls;
  • ask how animals meet their needs;
  • predict outcomes related to plant needs;
  • predict living things found in a habitat;
  • predict seasonal or temperature changes;
  • compare a prediction with an observation.

Quick Exit Ticket

  1. Write or say one science question.
  2. Make one prediction.
  3. Explain what helped you make that prediction.
