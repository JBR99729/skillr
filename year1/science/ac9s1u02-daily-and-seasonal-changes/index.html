YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1U02

What changed outside?

Describe changes during a day and across seasons. Connect the changes to what people, plants and animals do.

Learning intention: Describe changes during a day and across seasons. Connect the changes to what people, plants and animals do.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Cloudy morning, rainy afternoon

In this example, the morning is cloudy. Rain falls after lunch. We choose a raincoat for going outside. This is a change during one day; it does not identify the whole season.

Learn the skill

1. Record a daily change

Notice the sky, temperature or light at different times. A day's weather can change.

MorningAfter lunch
CloudyRainy
Try it · Are these records from two different seasons?

No. They describe different times on the same day.

2. Look across many days

Seasonal patterns involve changes over longer periods, such as typical weather, flowering or animal activity. One unusual day is not a whole seasonal pattern.

Local evidence matters

Compare dated observations over time. Different Australian places have different seasonal patterns.

Try it · Does a cold day prove it is winter?

No. Cold days can occur outside winter. Use the date, place and observations over time.

3. Connect changes to life

People adjust clothing and activities. Plants and animals may also change as local conditions change.

Observed conditionPossible response
RainyWear a raincoat
Hot and sunnySeek shade and follow sun protection advice
Try it · Must every Australian place have snow in winter?

No. Winter conditions vary across Australia. Describe local evidence.

Common mistakes

“Weather and season mean the same thing.” Weather describes conditions at a time; seasonal patterns are seen across a longer period.

“All seasonal calendars are the same.” First Nations seasonal knowledge is specific to particular Countries, Peoples and local indicators. Use an appropriate community-approved source.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make a weather-and-choice record

2 minutes: observe safely from a window. 5 minutes: draw the sky and choose clothing suited to today's conditions. 3 minutes: explain the connection. Add another dated record later to compare.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose from weather pictures. Extend: compare today's record with an earlier dated record; say what more is needed to study a season.

Never look directly at the Sun. Observe storms indoors.

Three tricky questions

1. It rains in summer. Has the season become winter?

No. A rainy day can occur in summer.

2. A tree has fewer leaves today. Can we say why from that alone?

No. Compare earlier records and local information. Different plants behave differently.

3. Can two places have different weather on the same day?

Yes. Record the place as well as the time.

FAQ

What counts as a daily change?

A change during one day, such as light, temperature or rainfall.

Can we use local First Nations seasonal knowledge?

Yes, with locally appropriate, community-approved sources and respect for cultural authority.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1U02.

1. Notice changes during a day

Compare morning, afternoon and evening records. Daylight, shade and weather can change during one day.

Try it: A morning record shows rain and an afternoon record is dry. What changed?

See answer and explanation

The recorded rain stopped by the afternoon; do not claim it was dry all day.

2. Describe today's weather

Use observed clues: moving leaves suggest wind; puddles may show earlier rain. Clouds alone do not prove that rain fell.

Try it: Does a dark cloud prove it is raining at the ground?

See answer and explanation

No. Check what is actually happening and record the evidence.

3. Look for seasonal changes

Compare observations over longer periods: leaf changes, flowers, daylight and usual temperatures. One cold day does not establish a season change.

Try it: Why should you compare more than one day?

See answer and explanation

Daily weather varies. A longer record helps show seasonal patterns.

4. Connect changes to everyday choices

Use current conditions and adult guidance to plan clothing or activities. Rain may change an outdoor plan; cold weather may call for a jumper.

Try it: What would help protect outdoor books when rain starts?

See answer and explanation

Move them under suitable cover with an adult. Explain the observed reason.

5. Use local seasonal knowledge

Seasonal patterns differ across Australian places. Read a named, community-endorsed local seasonal calendar with an adult, using its guidance and attribution.

Try it: Must every Australian place have the same seasonal pattern?

See answer and explanation

No. Use local evidence; do not generalise one community's calendar to all places.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Weather equals season — Weather can change daily; seasons are longer patterns.
  • Only naming the season — Describe what changes and how it affects life.
  • Forgetting daily changes — Light, shadows and temperature can change within one day.
