YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1I03

Observe, measure and record

Record what you observe. Use informal measurements and appropriate tools so another person can understand your record.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

Learning intention: Record what you observe. Use informal measurements and appropriate tools so another person can understand your record.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Five of which units?

A strip exactly spans five identical blocks laid end to end with no gaps or overlaps. Record “5 block lengths”, not just “5”. A labelled drawing or photograph can preserve details; it still needs the object and unit explained.

Learn the skill

1. Observe rather than invent

Use appropriate senses and safe tools to notice features. Separate what you see from a possible explanation.

Observation

I can see two holes in this leaf. That does not by itself show what made them.

Try it · Does a leaf with holes prove a caterpillar made them?

No. Record the holes; the cause needs more evidence.

2. Measure consistently

Use equal informal units, align the start, and leave no gaps or overlaps. Include the unit name.

Clear recordUnclear record
Length: 5 block lengthsLength: 5
Try it · Could five large blocks and five small blocks represent different lengths?

Yes. Use the same-size units when comparing measurements.

3. Choose a useful record

A labelled drawing, dated photograph, tally or table can show observations. Choose a tool that preserves the detail you need.

Record label

Object: strip A. Date: __. Length: 5 block lengths. Unit: our identical wooden blocks.

Try it · Is an unlabelled photograph enough to explain when and what you measured?

Not always. Add the object, date and measurement information.

Common mistakes

“Bigger units give a bigger count.” A longer unit may fit fewer times into the same length.

“A photograph explains everything.” Add labels and context; do not claim details the image does not show.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Record one safe object

2 minutes: choose a strip and identical large blocks with an adult. 5 minutes: measure with no gaps, draw the result and name the unit. 3 minutes: ask a partner to explain your record.

Adjust the challenge Support: use a strip that exactly spans whole blocks. Extend: explain why a different-sized unit could change the count.

Use safe objects and an adult-approved digital device. Photograph objects rather than people's private details.

Three tricky questions

1. Two children record 5 units but use different unit sizes. Must the lengths match?

No. The unit sizes matter.

2. You forget the measurement. Should you write a likely number?

No. Measure again if possible and record the observation.

3. A strip ends between two blocks. Should you claim it is exactly 5 blocks?

No. Describe it as between the nearby whole-block measurements or use a more suitable unit with guidance.

FAQ

What is an informal unit?

An everyday unit such as an identical block, rather than a standard unit such as a centimetre.

Must every record be written?

No. Labelled drawings, photographs and dictated observations can also communicate evidence.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1I03.

1. Observe rather than guess

Describe what you actually see or hear. “Four leaves” is a count; “it is happy” is an interpretation, not an observation.

Try it: A plant has four visible leaves. What should you record?

See answer and explanation

Four leaves, with its identity and observation time where useful.

2. Count carefully

Point to each item once and record the total. Separate zero observed from a missing record.

Try it: If nobody counted the seeds, can the blank be recorded as zero?

See answer and explanation

No. Mark the count as missing or count them; do not invent evidence.

3. Measure with informal units

Use matching blocks from the same start, end-to-end without gaps. A toy travelling six blocks has a distance of six blocks.

Try it: Why must you include “blocks” after six?

See answer and explanation

The number needs a unit so someone can interpret the measurement.

4. Choose a useful record

Use labels, words, drawings, tables or adult-supported photographs. A photograph needs context such as which plant and when.

Try it: Do decorative colours prove growth?

See answer and explanation

No. A dated, labelled comparison of the same plant can show growth evidence.

5. Compare consistent observations

Use the same object, feature and unit when checking change. Four blocks tall then six matching blocks tall shows an increase of two blocks.

Try it: Can six small blocks be directly compared with four larger blocks?

See answer and explanation

Not by the counts alone. Unit sizes differ.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Memory only — Write, draw or count during the investigation.
  • Unequal units — Use similar informal units end-to-end or consistently.
  • Record lacks detail — Include what changed, how much, or what was noticed.
