Curriculum examplesCopied content

These curriculum examples extend the central model into varied contexts. They are learning contexts, not extra definitions to memorise.

E1

exploring what an observation is, and different ways to make observations through guided discussion

Try and explain: Which is an observation: ‘The leaf has three holes’ or ‘An insect ate it last night’?

Answer and teaching point

‘The leaf has three holes’ describes something seen. The insect statement suggests an explanation that needs further evidence.

E2

counting and using informal measurements such as cups, handspans, walking paces, blocks, pencil lengths or lengths of string

Try and explain: A toy travels the length of seven identical blocks placed end-to-end. How should the distance be recorded?

Answer and teaching point

Seven blocks. Include the informal unit and check that there are no gaps or overlaps between blocks.

E3

making suggestions about types of measurements that may be made during an investigation, including using blocks to measure plant growth or paces to measure how far an object has moved

Try and explain: To compare two seedlings' heights, should you count their leaves or measure from the soil to the top using the same blocks?

Answer and teaching point

Measure their heights using the same-sized blocks and the same start and end points. Leaf count answers a different question.

E4

recording observations through text, drawing, counts, informal measurements, digital photography or video

Try and explain: A class photographs a seedling on Monday and Friday. Which labels make the photographs more useful as a record?

Answer and teaching point

Add the plant identity, dates and a consistent size reference where possible. This helps readers identify what changed and when.

Record what you observe, including the unit

Make and record observations and informal measurements accurately.

Worked example

A toy car rolls a distance of 7 equal blocks. How should this be recorded?

  1. Place equal blocks end-to-end along the distance from the marked start to the car's stopping place.
  2. Count seven blocks and record ‘7 blocks’, not just ‘7’.
  3. Add a labelled drawing or table entry so another person can understand what was measured.

Watch for this mistake: ‘It went far’ is hard to compare with another result. A recorded distance with the same unit gives clearer evidence.

Try it, then explain

  1. One result says 5 hand spans and another says 5 paper clips. Are the distances necessarily equal?

    Hint

    Compare the units, not only the number.

    Answer and explanation

    No. The units have different lengths.

  2. A leaf has three brown spots. Is ‘three brown spots’ an observation or a prediction?

    Hint

    Was it seen, or expected before looking?

    Answer and explanation

    An observation, because it describes what was seen.

Quick questions

  • Name two ways to record observations.
  • What is an informal measurement?
  • Why should observations be recorded?
  • When might a photo help?

Return to the concept model if an answer is uncertain. Use the model to explain, not just to guess.

Practice and reviewReady for practice
  • Memory only — Write, draw or count during the investigation.
  • Unequal units — Use similar informal units end-to-end or consistently.
  • Record lacks detail — Include what changed, how much, or what was noticed.
