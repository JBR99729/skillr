YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1I01

Notice, wonder, predict

Ask questions about simple patterns you observe. Make a prediction using relevant experience.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

Learning intention: Ask questions about simple patterns you observe. Make a prediction using relevant experience.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Will the car go farther on the board again?

In earlier similar trials, a toy car travelled farther on a board than on a rug. We ask whether that pattern will happen again. We predict it may travel farther on the board, using the earlier records as our reason.

Learn the skill

1. Say what you noticed

An observation reports what happened. It is different from a guess about the next trial.

Observation

In trial 1, the car travelled 6 block lengths on the board.

Try it · Which reports an observation: “It travelled 6 blocks” or “It may travel 6 blocks next time”?

“It travelled 6 blocks” reports what happened.

2. Ask about the pattern

Choose a question that observation or a safe investigation could help answer.

QuestionCan this investigate rolling distance?
Does the car go farther on the board or rug?Yes
Which surface do I like best?No: that asks a preference
Try it · What question could you ask after noticing a plant lean towards a window?

For example: “Does the plant keep leaning towards the window as it grows?” Observe without harming it.

3. Predict and give a reason

Predict before the next observation. Use relevant earlier evidence and words such as may or might.

Sentence frame

I predict __ because I noticed __.

Try it · Must you change your prediction if the next result is different?

Keep the original prediction and record the actual result. You can revise a later prediction using the new evidence.

Common mistakes

“Any guess is a reasoned prediction.” Give relevant experience or a pattern as your reason.

“One result proves it always happens.” Look for more observations and consider the conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Ask a question from a record

2 minutes: read the supplied car records. 5 minutes: say one observation, ask one question and write or dictate a prediction with a reason. 3 minutes: explain which evidence supports it.

Adjust the challenge Support: use sentence starters. Extend: name one condition that would need to stay similar in another trial.

If testing, use an adult-checked floor-level setup and sturdy toys.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “The car is blue” a reason it will travel farther?

No. Colour alone is not relevant distance evidence in this comparison.

2. Can a question be useful even if we do not know its answer yet?

Yes. The question guides what to observe or investigate.

3. A result disagrees with your prediction. Did you fail to learn anything?

No. Record it accurately and ask a connected question.

FAQ

What is the difference between a question and a prediction?

A question asks what we want to find out. A prediction says what we think may happen.

Do predictions need reasons?

Use relevant experiences or observed patterns to support them.

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1I01.

1. Start with an observation

“The car went farther on the board than on the rug” reports something noticed. Use that observation to ask a question.

Try it: Which is an observation: “It rolled four blocks” or “It will roll farther”?

See answer and explanation

It rolled four blocks. The other statement is a prediction.

2. Ask a question you can investigate

“Does this car roll farther on the board or the rug?” names a comparison. A preference alone does not answer it.

Try it: Is “Which car do I like best?” a test of rolling distance?

See answer and explanation

No. Ask about an observable movement or measurement.

3. Notice a pattern

Compare several supplied records. If a plant repeatedly leans towards a window, that pattern suggests a question about light.

Try it: Does one record prove a reliable pattern?

See answer and explanation

No. Look for supporting observations and relevant conditions.

4. Predict with a reason

“I predict the car may travel farther on the board because it did so in our earlier trials.” Link the prediction to relevant evidence.

Try it: Does “because I like blue” support a distance prediction?

See answer and explanation

No. Use evidence about movement or the test conditions.

5. Compare the prediction with results

Write a prediction before the test, then record what happens. Predictions can differ from observations without making the investigation a failure.

Try it: The car stops sooner than predicted. What should you record?

See answer and explanation

The actual distance. Keep the original prediction for comparison and ask what might explain the difference.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Question too broad — Ask one thing that can be observed or compared.
  • No evidence — Use a pattern or experience before predicting.
  • Prediction stated as certainty — Use I think or I predict language.
