Worked example

Question: Why might someone take an umbrella after seeing dark clouds? They use the observed weather pattern to predict rain and choose a useful action.

Use a pattern to make a practical prediction

observationpatternpredictionaction

Name the pattern, say what you predict, then explain why it matters.

Explain your thinking: Say what each part of the model represents, then explain why the answer or conclusion follows.

Use a pattern to make a useful prediction

Connect an observed pattern to an everyday decision and check the result.

Worked example

A gardener notices that a pot's soil is often dry after warm, windy days. How could this observation help?

  1. Name the repeated observation: the soil often becomes dry after those conditions.
  2. Predict that the soil may be dry after another warm, windy day.
  3. Check the soil before deciding whether to water. A prediction helps a decision but still needs checking.

Watch for this mistake: A pattern supports a prediction; it does not guarantee that every future event will be identical.

Try it, then explain

  1. A family sees that washing usually dries faster in moving air. How could they use this pattern?

    Hint

    Choose an action connected to the observation.

    Answer and explanation

    Place washing where air can move around it, then check whether it dries.

  2. Why should the gardener check the soil rather than water only because the day was warm?

    Hint

    Other conditions may also affect the soil.

    Answer and explanation

    The soil might still be wet, for example after rain or recent watering.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

These curriculum examples extend the central model into varied contexts. They are learning contexts, not extra definitions to memorise.

E1

learning from farmers, bush care volunteers, gardeners or nursery owners about how they observe the needs of plants, and how they have designed or managed habitats to meet those needs

Try and explain: A gardener notices seedlings leaning towards a window. How could that observation guide care?

Answer and teaching point

The gardener can check whether the plants receive suitable light and adjust their position. Observations inform a care decision.

E2

identifying ways that science knowledge is used in the care of the local environment and suggesting ways local gardens or parks could better meet the needs of native animals

Try and explain: A garden has very little shelter for small animals. Suggest one feature to discuss with a local habitat expert.

Answer and teaching point

For example, suitable local plants or sheltered spaces. Explain which animal need the feature could meet and check its suitability for that place.

E3

investigating how First Nations Australians use science to meet their needs, such as food and water supply and shelter

Cultural safety: Use a community-approved source, name the specific Nation or community where the source does, and avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation.

E4

recognising how First Nations Australians use changes in the landscape and the sky to answer questions about when to gather certain resources

Cultural safety: Use a community-approved source, name the specific Nation or community where the source does, and avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation.

E5

learning from local ecologists or wildlife carers about native animals’ needs and how they observe animal behaviour to design supports for them to meet those needs, such as building frog and insect hotels and nesting boxes or recycling materials to provide habitat

Try and explain: A wildlife carer observes where an animal rests before designing a shelter. Why observe first?

Answer and teaching point

The animal's behaviour gives evidence about the kind of shelter it uses. A useful design should respond to the animal's needs.

E6

sharing examples of how they have used science knowledge at home, such as by listening to or viewing weather forecasts or observing weather patterns when planning family events or outings, or wearing appropriate clothing for the season

Try and explain: A family sees a forecast of strong rain before a picnic. How are they using science if they change the plan?

Answer and teaching point

They use information about likely weather to predict conditions and make a practical decision.

E7

identifying how we use pushes and pulls when preparing meals, and the tools that help us push or pull objects

Try and explain: When stirring a bowl with a spoon, how do pushes help move the mixture?

Answer and teaching point

The hand moves the spoon, and the spoon pushes the mixture along. The tool helps transfer the applied force.

E8

exploring how engineers use knowledge of forces to create new playground equipment or toys

Try and explain: An engineer tests a toy with a gentle push and then a stronger push. What could the results help them decide?

Answer and teaching point

The results can help them predict how the toy moves under different pushes and improve its design for its intended users.

Common misconceptions and traps
  • Prediction without pattern — Point to the observation or repeated pattern first.
  • Science only in labs — Science is also used in cooking, weather choices, gardening and safety.
  • Guess equals prediction — A prediction uses evidence or experience.
Guided learning activities

Weather decision

Use signs in the sky or forecast symbols to choose what to wear or carry.

Model: clouds → rain? → umbrella

Shadow pattern

Observe shadows at two times and predict what may happen later.

Model: morning shadow ≠ afternoon shadow

Home science

List times people use science at home, school or outdoors.

Model: cook • clean • garden • travel
