Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Open the focus you need for today’s worksheet. Read, try and explain before moving on.

1. See science in everyday actions

People observe and use knowledge to make decisions. A gardener checks a plant's soil and growing place before choosing care.

Try it: Why observe before watering?

See answer and explanation

The observations help choose suitable care rather than assuming every plant needs the same amount.

2. Use weather information

A family sees a forecast of strong rain and considers a sheltered outing. The forecast informs a decision; it does not guarantee the result.

Try it: Why might the family change its outdoor plan?

See answer and explanation

Rain could affect comfort and safety. Check conditions and adult advice too.

3. Connect habitats to care

Wildlife carers observe where an animal rests and feeds before planning support. Different species need different habitats.

Try it: Does a beautiful shelter automatically meet an animal's needs?

See answer and explanation

No. Check whether it provides suitable protection and access to the animal's other needs.

4. Use patterns for predictions

If a known plant repeatedly grows towards a window, that pattern can guide a prediction about its light needs. A prediction states what may happen and why.

Try it: Must a prediction be right to be useful?

See answer and explanation

No. Compare it with observations and revise the idea when evidence differs.

5. Notice forces in useful tools

A spoon's pushes move a mixture. Engineers test how forces affect toys before improving their design.

Try it: Why test a toy with a gentle and a stronger push?

See answer and explanation

To compare its behaviour under stated conditions and inform a safer or more useful design.

6. Respect local knowledge

Use a named, community-endorsed First Nations account of local seasonal observations and practices. Keep its place, attribution and guidance together.

Try it: Should one local account be presented as what all First Nations people do?

See answer and explanation

No. Knowledge and protocols are specific; do not invent or generalise practices.

Try a worked example together

Use a pattern to make an everyday decision

Work through the example

  1. Repeated observations can help us predict what may happen.
  2. The gardener expects dry soil after another warm day, then checks before deciding to water.
  3. The check matters: a prediction helps a decision but does not replace an observation.

Watch for this: A pattern can support a prediction without making the outcome certain.

Try it together

How could noticing dark clouds help someone plan a walk?

Answer and teaching guidance

They might prepare for rain, for example by taking a raincoat, while checking the actual weather and adult advice.

An adult can read the directions, model the idea and accept an oral, pointed or drawn explanation where appropriate. For reading, spelling, handwriting or speaking tasks, observe the child doing the stated skill.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Prediction without pattern — Point to the observation or repeated pattern first.
  • Science only in labs — Science is also used in cooking, weather choices, gardening and safety.
  • Guess equals prediction — A prediction uses evidence or experience.
