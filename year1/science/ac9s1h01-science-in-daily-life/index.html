YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1H01

Use science in everyday life

Describe how people use science each day. Use observed patterns to make useful predictions.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

Learning intention: Describe how people use science each day. Use observed patterns to make useful predictions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Notice, predict, check, care

A gardener has noticed that this pot's soil often dries more quickly on warm days. On another warm day, the gardener predicts it may be dry. They check the plant's needs and soil before deciding whether to water.

EvidencePredictionUseful action
This pot often dries on warm daysIt may be dry todayCheck the plant and soil with an adult

Learn the skill

1. Find an everyday use

Science helps people care for plants and animals, plan for weather and design useful tools.

PersonUses observations to…
GardenerCheck plant care
Wildlife carerProvide suitable shelter
FamilyPlan clothing for the weather
Try it · Is science only used in a laboratory?

No. People use observations and scientific knowledge in many daily activities.

2. Use a pattern to predict

A prediction uses relevant experience to say what may happen. It is not a guarantee.

Pattern → prediction

The path was slippery after earlier rain. After more rain, it may be slippery again.

Try it · Is “It may be wet because rain is forecast” a prediction?

Yes. It predicts a possibility and gives a relevant reason.

3. Check before deciding

Observe what happens and adjust the action. People also learn from local experts and knowledge holders.

Knowledge and place

Learn about local living things from gardeners or carers. First Nations knowledge belongs to particular Peoples and Countries; use authorised local sources.

Try it · Should a gardener water every plant just because today is warm?

No. Check the particular plant, soil and care guidance first.

Common mistakes

“A prediction must come true.” A prediction can differ from what happens. Keep the evidence and check again.

“One care rule suits every habitat.” Different plants, animals and places have different needs.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Spot a science decision

2 minutes: choose a familiar task such as checking a plant or planning clothing. 5 minutes: draw the evidence, prediction and decision. 3 minutes: explain how science helped.

Adjust the challenge Support: complete “I noticed… so I might…”. Extend: explain what observation could make you change your decision.

An adult makes care and weather-safety decisions.

Three tricky questions

1. An outing forecast changes. Must the family keep its first plan?

No. Use new information to adjust the plan.

2. Can a wildlife carer use science without wearing a lab coat?

Yes. Observing animals' needs and designing suitable support use science.

3. Is “because I like yellow” evidence that a raincoat is needed?

No. Use weather information or observations.

FAQ

What is a scientific prediction?

A statement about what may happen, supported by relevant observations or experience.

Can tools use science?

Yes. Designers use knowledge of materials and pushes and pulls to make tools useful.

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1H01.

1. See science in everyday actions

People observe and use knowledge to make decisions. A gardener checks a plant's soil and growing place before choosing care.

Try it: Why observe before watering?

See answer and explanation

The observations help choose suitable care rather than assuming every plant needs the same amount.

2. Use weather information

A family sees a forecast of strong rain and considers a sheltered outing. The forecast informs a decision; it does not guarantee the result.

Try it: Why might the family change its outdoor plan?

See answer and explanation

Rain could affect comfort and safety. Check conditions and adult advice too.

3. Connect habitats to care

Wildlife carers observe where an animal rests and feeds before planning support. Different species need different habitats.

Try it: Does a beautiful shelter automatically meet an animal's needs?

See answer and explanation

No. Check whether it provides suitable protection and access to the animal's other needs.

4. Use patterns for predictions

If a known plant repeatedly grows towards a window, that pattern can guide a prediction about its light needs. A prediction states what may happen and why.

Try it: Must a prediction be right to be useful?

See answer and explanation

No. Compare it with observations and revise the idea when evidence differs.

5. Notice forces in useful tools

A spoon's pushes move a mixture. Engineers test how forces affect toys before improving their design.

Try it: Why test a toy with a gentle and a stronger push?

See answer and explanation

To compare its behaviour under stated conditions and inform a safer or more useful design.

6. Respect local knowledge

Use a named, community-endorsed First Nations account of local seasonal observations and practices. Keep its place, attribution and guidance together.

Try it: Should one local account be presented as what all First Nations people do?

See answer and explanation

No. Knowledge and protocols are specific; do not invent or generalise practices.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Prediction without pattern — Point to the observation or repeated pattern first.
  • Science only in labs — Science is also used in cooking, weather choices, gardening and safety.
  • Guess equals prediction — A prediction uses evidence or experience.
