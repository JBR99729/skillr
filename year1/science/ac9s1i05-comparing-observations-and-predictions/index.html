Comparing Observations and Predictions

After an investigation, scientists compare what they predicted with what they observed, listen to others’ observations and consider whether the investigation was fair.

Learning goals

After an investigation, scientists compare what they predicted with what they observed, listen to others’ observations and consider whether the investigation was fair.

Learning routine: Read prediction → Compare observation → Check fairness → Ask next question

Success looks like

  • Compare prediction and observation
  • Listen to others’ observations
  • Consider fairness
  • Identify next questions
  • Use evidence
Concept model and worked application

Prediction and observation may match or differ

predictionobservationsame?different?

A different result is not failure. It gives evidence and can lead to a new question.

Worked example

Prediction: the big ball will roll farther. Observation: the small ball rolled farther. Comparison: my prediction was different, so I need to look at the evidence and ask why.

Fair investigations change only what is being tested

choose what changeskeep other relevant conditions alikecompare what happens

Fairness helps observations answer the question properly.

Explain your thinking: Say what each part of the model represents, then explain why the answer or conclusion follows.

Compare the prediction, the evidence and the test

Compare results with predictions and notice whether an investigation was fair.

Worked example

You predict a car will roll farther on the board. It travels 8 equal blocks on the board and 5 on the rug, from the same low ramp and release point.

Recorded observations
SurfaceDistance in equal blocks
Board8
Rug5
  1. Compare the two recorded distances: eight is farther than five.
  2. State that this result matches the prediction for these trials.
  3. Check what stayed the same and ask whether repeating the test gives a similar result. The surface was the intended change.

Watch for this mistake: If different cars are being compared, the car is the thing that changes. Keep the other relevant conditions alike; do not claim the same object must always be used.

Try it, then explain

  1. Two groups get slightly different distances using the same procedure. Should one erase its result?

    Hint

    Keep the evidence and look for possible reasons.

    Answer and explanation

    No. Compare the observations, check the procedure and repeat if useful.

  2. One car is pushed hard and another is gently released. Is that a fair way to compare the cars? Explain.

    Hint

    More than the car has changed.

    Answer and explanation

    No. The starting push also changed. Release them in the same agreed way.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

These curriculum examples extend the central model into varied contexts. They are learning contexts, not extra definitions to memorise.

E1

comparing observations with those of others, such as how many birds each group counted in the playground or how much each group’s seedling has grown in a week

Try and explain: Two groups count five and seven birds. Before deciding who is correct, what should they compare?

Answer and teaching point

Compare where and when they counted, how long they watched and whether birds moved or were counted twice. Different observations do not automatically mean carelessness.

E2

consulting with First Nations Australians to compare observations and evaluate identifications of animal tracks

Cultural safety: Use a community-approved source, name the specific Nation or community where the source does, and avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation.

E3

exploring if making weather observations at different times of day makes a difference and considering how they could compare weather across each day more fairly

Try and explain: One day's weather is observed at 8 am and another day's at 2 pm. How could the comparison be made more consistent?

Answer and teaching point

Observe at the same time and place on each day, or clearly record time as part of the comparison. Time of day may affect the conditions.

E4

comparing observations of movement with predictions, such as how far an object travels

Try and explain: You predict a toy will roll six blocks. It rolls four. What should your report say?

Answer and teaching point

Report the prediction of six and the observation of four separately. Do not change the result to match the prediction; ask what might explain the difference.

E5

exploring if all ‘big’ pushes are the same by comparing how far an object travels with different students doing the pushing, and discussing how they could have made the investigation fairer

Try and explain: Two children both call their pushes ‘big’, but the toy travels different distances. What makes the comparison uncertain?

Answer and teaching point

‘Big’ does not specify an equal push. With teacher guidance, use a more repeatable method and keep the other relevant conditions alike.

Common misconceptions and traps
  • Wrong prediction means bad science — A different result can still teach something.
  • Ignoring other observations — Compare your evidence with others’ evidence.
  • Unfair test accepted — Check what changed and what stayed the same.
