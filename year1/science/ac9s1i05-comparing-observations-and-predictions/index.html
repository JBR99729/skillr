YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1I05

Did the result match?

Compare results with predictions and others' observations. With guidance, consider fairness and ask a further question.

Learning intention: Compare results with predictions and others' observations. With guidance, consider fairness and ask a further question.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Predicted three, observed five

Before a car trial, Mia predicts 3 block lengths. Afterwards, she measures 5 block lengths. She keeps both records: the observation is farther than predicted. Another group records 4 block lengths; they compare their units and setup before explaining the difference.

Prediction, before trial3 block lengths
Observation, after trial5 block lengths
Another group4 block lengths

Learn the skill

1. Compare prediction and observation

A prediction says what may happen. An observation records what did happen. Keep both.

BeforeAfter
I predict 3 block lengthsWe observed 5 block lengths
Try it · Should Mia change her prediction to 5 after measuring?

No. Keep the prediction as 3 and the observation as 5.

2. Compare other records

Describe similarities and differences. Ask about the car, surface, push and units before assuming anyone is wrong.

Our result / their result

We recorded 5 block lengths. They recorded 4. Did we use the same-sized blocks and similar conditions?

Try it · Do different records prove someone was careless?

No. Conditions or measurement methods may differ. Investigate with guidance.

3. Check fairness and ask next

A useful comparison changes what is being investigated while keeping other important conditions similar.

Comparing surfacesWhat should stay similar?
Board versus rugSame car, starting point, push approach and measurement units
Try it · What further question follows these results?

For example: “Will the same car travel similar distances if we repeat the trial using the same setup?”

Common mistakes

“A wrong prediction ruins the investigation.” An honest result can teach us something even when it differs from the prediction.

“A fair test means everyone gets a turn.” Taking turns is fair behaviour. A fair comparison also needs suitable test conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare without changing the record

2 minutes: read the prediction and observation table. 5 minutes: mark match or differ and explain the difference. 3 minutes: ask about another group's setup and suggest one connected question.

Adjust the challenge Support: use before/after cards. Extend: identify two changes that could make a surface comparison unclear.

Use supplied records or an adult-checked safe setup.

Three tricky questions

1. The result is different from the prediction. Which should be erased?

Neither. Keep both so you can compare them.

2. One group uses a ramp and another a flat start. Can surface alone explain the different distances?

No. The starting setup changed too.

3. Can a repeated investigation give a slightly different result?

Yes. Compare methods and evidence before making a claim.

FAQ

What does compare mean?

Describe what is the same and what is different.

What makes a further question useful?

It connects to the evidence and can guide another observation or safe investigation.

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1I05.

1. Keep prediction and observation separate

Prediction: “It may travel six blocks.” Observation: “It travelled four blocks.” Keep both statements so they can be compared.

Try it: Should you change the result to match the prediction?

See answer and explanation

No. Record the actual four-block result.

2. Compare what happened with what was expected

The four-block observation is two blocks shorter than the six-block prediction. Describe the difference without calling the investigation a failure.

Try it: If the distance matches the prediction, does that prove the test was fair?

See answer and explanation

No. Check the procedure and conditions separately.

3. Compare others' observations

Check object, time, start and units before comparing records. Different conditions can explain different observations.

Try it: One child uses large blocks and another small blocks. Can counts alone settle who is right?

See answer and explanation

No. Check the units and measure consistently before comparing.

4. Consider fairness with guidance

When testing surfaces, keep the toy and release point the same. Changing several things makes the comparison harder to interpret.

Try it: What is unclear if both toy and surface change?

See answer and explanation

Which change affected the distance. An adult helps plan a clearer comparison.

5. Improve and ask a connected question

Repeat consistently to check evidence, rather than repeating until a favourite result occurs. A follow-up should connect to what was observed.

Try it: The same toy rolled different distances on board and rug. Suggest a follow-up.

See answer and explanation

For example, “What happens on a third adult-approved surface?” Use the same toy and starting method.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Wrong prediction means bad science — A different result can still teach something.
  • Ignoring other observations — Compare your evidence with others’ evidence.
  • Unfair test accepted — Check what changed and what stayed the same.
