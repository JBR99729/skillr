YEAR 1 SCIENCE · AC9S1I06

Make your science message clear

Write and create texts that communicate observations, findings and ideas. Use clear everyday and scientific vocabulary.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

Learning intention: Write and create texts that communicate observations, findings and ideas. Use clear everyday and scientific vocabulary.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Tell the reader what the car test showed

A class's supplied records show 6 block lengths on a board and 3 on a rug. A useful report names the object, surfaces and unit: “In our trial, the toy car travelled farther on the board than on the rug.” It does not claim the same result will happen in every possible trial.

SurfaceObserved distance
Board6 block lengths
Rug3 block lengths

Illustration for discussion. Any example measurements are supplied in the table, not measured from the artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Choose the evidence

Tell what you observed. Keep guesses about a cause separate from findings.

Evidence-based sentence

In our trial, the car travelled 6 block lengths on the board.

Try it · Is “The car was happy on the board” a scientific observation?

No. Report observable movement or measurements rather than an imagined feeling.

2. Use labels and useful words

A drawing, table or short text should name the object and important features. Use words such as push, surface and distance when they help.

UnclearClearer
It went over thereThe toy car moved to the right
It went 6It travelled 6 block lengths
Try it · Which label would improve a drawing of this test?

For example: toy car, board, rug, start mark or the measurement unit, placed beside the correct feature.

3. Organise and check the message

Choose a short report, labelled drawing or table for your audience. Re-read it with the records. Ask a partner what they understood.

Mini-report

We tested a toy car on two surfaces. In our trial it travelled 6 block lengths on the board and 3 on the rug. It travelled farther on the board.

Try it · Can we conclude that every car always travels twice as far on every board?

No. These records describe this trial and setup; they do not establish a universal rule.

Common mistakes

“More science words always make a report better.” Choose accurate words your audience can understand.

“A neat picture can replace the evidence.” Include labels and observations that match the actual record.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create a mini science report

2 minutes: read the car results. 5 minutes: create a labelled drawing, table or two-sentence report. 3 minutes: ask a partner to explain the finding and improve one unclear label.

Adjust the challenge Support: dictate a sentence and add picture labels. Extend: include a further question and clearly separate it from the finding.

Use supplied records. Share images or recordings only with an adult's guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a report be clear even if it uses everyday words?

Yes. Accurate everyday words can communicate well; add scientific vocabulary where useful.

2. The table says 3 blocks. Should the report say 4 because the picture looks longer?

No. Check the record and report its evidence accurately.

3. Can a finding and a new question appear in the same report?

Yes. Label them clearly so the reader knows which is evidence and which asks what to investigate next.

FAQ

What is a finding?

What the observations or results show in the investigation.

Can we learn other ways of sharing observations?

Yes. Use culturally appropriate authorised examples when learning about particular First Nations Peoples' ways of communicating knowledge.

Additional explanations and guided checks for AC9S1I06.

1. Tell what you observed

Use a clear message about actual evidence. “Our plant had six leaves on Friday” identifies what was counted and when.

Try it: Is “Our plant is the best” an observation report?

See answer and explanation

No. It is a preference without an observable finding.

2. Use a drawing and labels together

A useful drawing shows the observed feature; labels identify the object and details. Labels must agree with the picture.

Try it: A picture shows four leaves but its label says six. What should you check?

See answer and explanation

Compare both with the actual observation and correct the mismatch.

3. Choose useful science words

Use words such as push, pull, leaf, shelter and measure when they explain the idea. Unfamiliar words alone do not improve evidence.

Try it: Which is clearer: “six” or “six equal blocks tall”?

See answer and explanation

Six equal blocks tall. The unit explains the measurement.

4. Show results in an organised text

A small report can use a title, labelled drawing and finding. A table needs headers; sequence words help explain steps.

Try it: Why does a table need category names?

See answer and explanation

They explain what the entries represent; numbers alone can be unclear.

5. Explain a finding without inventing a cause

“The car travelled four blocks on the rug and six on the board” compares observed results. It does not prove every car behaves identically.

Try it: Can one trial show that every stronger push always goes farther?

See answer and explanation

No. Communicate the supplied evidence and conditions, then discuss further testing with an adult.

6. Share and check understanding

Read or present the message to someone. Ask them to describe the finding; revise an unclear label or missing detail.

Try it: Does a longer report always communicate better?

See answer and explanation

No. Relevant evidence, clear wording and correct labels matter more than length.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Opinion without evidence — Say what you observed or found, not only what you liked.
  • No labels — Labels help readers understand drawings and models.
  • Everyday words only — Add useful science vocabulary when it makes meaning clearer.
