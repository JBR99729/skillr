Learning goals

Science ideas can be communicated with words, drawings, labels, tables, posters or digital texts. Clear communication uses evidence and useful scientific vocabulary.

Learning routine: Choose evidence → Use science words → Create text → Share → Respond

Success looks like

  • Use scientific vocabulary
  • Write observations
  • Create labelled texts
  • Communicate findings
  • Use evidence clearly
Concept model and worked application

Use evidence plus science vocabulary

I observedmy findingbecauseevidence

Science words such as force, habitat, prediction and observation make ideas clearer.

Worked example

Weak message: It was good. Stronger science message: I observed the car rolled 8 blocks, so my finding is that the smooth floor helped it move farther.

Choose a text type that helps the audience understand

labelled drawingshort reporttableposterdigital slide

The best text type depends on what you need to communicate.

Explain your thinking: Say what each part of the model represents, then explain why the answer or conclusion follows.

Make your science report useful to someone else

Communicate an observation with labels, evidence and suitable vocabulary.

Worked example

Improve this report: ‘It went better there.’ The result was a car travelling 9 blocks on a board and 4 on a rug.

  1. Name the object and the two places being compared.
  2. Include the observed results and the measurement unit.
  3. Write: ‘The toy car travelled 9 blocks on the board and 4 blocks on the rug. In this test it travelled farther on the board.’ Add a labelled drawing or table.

Watch for this mistake: Do not write a prediction as if it were a result. Use ‘I predict’ before a test and ‘I observed’ when reporting evidence.

Try it, then explain

  1. Why is ‘the sponge changed shape when I pushed it’ clearer than ‘it changed’?

    Hint

    Look for the named object, action and change.

    Answer and explanation

    It tells the reader what was tested, what was done and what was observed.

  2. Which label helps a drawing of a measured plant: ‘nice’ or ‘height: 6 blocks’? Explain.

    Hint

    Choose information someone can use to understand the result.

    Answer and explanation

    ‘Height: 6 blocks’ communicates an observation and its unit.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

These curriculum examples extend the central model into varied contexts. They are learning contexts, not extra definitions to memorise.

E1

exploring the difference between everyday and scientific vocabulary when describing objects or events

Try and explain: Improve ‘I made it go’ by naming the action and direction for a toy moved away from your hand.

Answer and teaching point

For example, ‘I pushed the toy forwards.’ The words identify both the force and its direction.

E2

acknowledging and learning about First Nations Australians’ ways of representing and sharing observations

Cultural safety: Use a community-approved source, name the specific Nation or community where the source does, and avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation.

E3

creating models of the place a plant or animal lives using recycled objects, modelling clay, toys or drawings

Try and explain: A habitat model includes water and a sheltered resting place. How can labels improve your explanation?

Answer and teaching point

Label what each feature provides: water for drinking and shelter for resting or protection. Explain the need instead of only naming the material used in the model.

E4

representing seasonal changes of plants using sequential drawings, calendars or digital photographs

Try and explain: You want to show how a tree changed over three months. What should accompany three drawings?

Answer and teaching point

Dates or month labels, placed in order, and descriptions of observed changes. Consistent viewpoints make comparisons easier.

E5

representing push and pull forces using role-play, labels, arrows or time lapse drawings and describing their representation using everyday and scientific vocabulary

Try and explain: On a drawing, a hand pulls a wagon left. Add an arrow and words to communicate the force.

Answer and teaching point

Draw an arrow pointing left at the wagon and label it ‘pull to the left’. Match the words, arrow and depicted action.

E6

role-playing or recounting how people they know or have observed identify and use patterns to make predictions at work or in their daily lives

Try and explain: Retell how a gardener uses a repeated observation to make a prediction. Include the pattern and the predicted result.

Answer and teaching point

For example, ‘The soil usually dries after several warm days, so the gardener predicts it may need checking for water tomorrow.’ Identify the evidence and keep the prediction distinct from a later observation.

Common misconceptions and traps
  • Opinion without evidence — Say what you observed or found, not only what you liked.
  • No labels — Labels help readers understand drawings and models.
  • Everyday words only — Add useful science vocabulary when it makes meaning clearer.
Guided learning activities

Labelled drawing

Create a labelled drawing to show a plant or animal habitat need.

Model: label: water / shelter / food

Finding sentence

Write one observation and one finding using because.

Model: I found ___ because ___.

Vocabulary upgrade

Replace everyday words with scientific words where helpful.

Model: push / pull / habitat / observation
