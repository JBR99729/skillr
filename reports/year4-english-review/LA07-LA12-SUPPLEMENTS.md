## LA07 — quoted and reported speech

**Read:** British Council LearnEnglish Teens, [Reported speech](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/reported-speech), the complete visible scripted transcript and explanatory conversation, through the reporting-verb discussion. It distinguishes exact speech from a report, models speaker/pronoun changes and explains that reporting time/place depends on context. Same-day reporting can retain a time expression; continuing truths can retain present tense. Read the statements, question and request explanations; the last two are supplementary background, not added compulsory Year4 formalism. No update date was verified. **B1–B2/teen audience**, not an exact Year4 lesson. Embedded exercises, downloadable worksheets, audio and video were not attempted or watched.

**Original implications:** write paired messages with an explicit speaker, listener and reporting moment. Preserve who did what, and change pronouns/time language only when the context requires it. Ask why exact wording is useful in one situation and a concise report in another. Include an actual short quotation and an original report of it; do not turn “often changes tense” into “always moves back one tense”. Avoid copying the teen job-interview dialogue or importing perfect-tense drills as core content.

**Cross-check:** [NESA English K–10 glossary](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/english/english-k-10-2022/glossary), reported-speech entry, explicitly describes tense change as frequent rather than mandatory. This is a terminology check, not a complete lesson.

## LA08 — adverb groups and prepositional phrases

**Read:** Australian Government Style Manual [Adverbs](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/grammar-punctuation-and-conventions/types-words/adverbs), explanation and examples through the degree/comparison section; [Prepositions](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/grammar-punctuation-and-conventions/types-words/prepositions), relationship and function examples. The former shows an adverb modifying another adverb and includes non-`-ly` forms. The latter shows time/place relationships and how the same word can perform different jobs in context. These are professional writing references, not Year4 lesson equivalence. Do not import advice to minimise adverbs as a ban on the code’s required sentence expansion, or impose government register preferences on all varieties of English.

**Read:** British Council [Adverbials of manner](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-manner), main explanation through the linking-verb section. It includes regular/irregular manner forms, `well`, and `hard` versus `hardly`; shows that some manner detail is expressed by a phrase. Beginner/intermediate reference, no exercise or audio interaction. No independently retained update date.

**Read with a material caution:** Style Manual [Phrases](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/grammar-punctuation-and-conventions/parts-sentences/phrases), updated22 December2022, main definitions and adverbial/adjectival sections. Its account is too compressed to serve as the definition model for this code: the prepositional-phrase constituent list omits the complement, and the time/place wording can blur function and form. Do not reproduce those simplifications. Its examples may support discussion, but root/author must preserve accurate distinctions.

**NESA cross-check:** the [glossary](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/english/english-k-10-2022/glossary) adverb, adverbial-phrase, preposition and prepositional-phrase entries were actually read. They support circumstance functions and the connection of a preposition to a noun/group/pronoun. These brief entries also are not a full grammatical taxonomy.

**Original implications:** contrast an adverb-headed group such as *rather quietly* with a prepositional phrase such as *in a quiet voice*. Both can describe manner, but their form differs. Supply the whole sentence, ask what activity the phrase describes, and require a new sentence plus explanation. Include clear time/place/manner examples, alternative valid positions and non-`-ly` adverbs. Do not label every time expression a prepositional phrase or every multiword circumstance an adverb-headed group.

**NESA cross-check:** read the [glossary](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/english/english-k-10-2022/glossary) tense and verb-group entries: verb forms communicate time, including multiword groups. No new exact NSW outcome mapping is inferred from a glossary.

**Original implications:** teach the same simple event at different times; use complete short reports, recounts and plans rather than isolated suffix drills. Explain what changed in meaning. Collect an actual short recount/revision with a justified time shift; do not mark every tense shift wrong merely because two forms differ.

## LA10 — framing, placement, salience and moving images

**Read:** ACMI [Film It: Cinematography](https://www.acmi.net.au/education/school-program-and-resources/film-it-cinematography/), introduction and all six written sections: shots, angles, movement, composition, crash zoom and focus pull, with their written activity/reflection directions. This official page is explicitly recommended **Years5–VCE**, so use it as teacher background, not an approved Year4 watch assignment. It connects shot distance with included information and audience attention, asks learners to compare arrangements, and discusses changing focus/placement. It supplies concrete reframing, illustration and filming tasks. No embedded video, GIF, linked film extract or downloadable template was visually inspected. No update date verified for this resource page.

**Source correction caution:** its rule-of-thirds prose says three horizontal and three vertical lines while describing a nine-part grid. A thirds grid has **two interior horizontal and two interior vertical lines**. Do not copy that wording or make thirds a mandatory rule. Camera-angle emotional effects are possibilities, not guaranteed responses. Crash zoom/focus-pull equipment skills are unnecessary Year4 core content.

**NESA cross-check:** [glossary salience entry](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/english/english-k-10-2022/glossary) actually read; it identifies foreground framing, size and distinct colour as ways to emphasise elements. This supports several potential cues, not “largest/central is always most salient”.

**Original implications:** create actual original pairs of the same scene with controlled changes in framing, placement or contrast. Ask learners to point to visible evidence and explain a plausible response. Include an actual authored moving clip whose framing/composition can be examined; a static storyboard may support planning but must not be described as a watched video. Adults assess choices and explanations, not artistic polish. Child-facing tasks can use objects or invented characters without requiring personal photographs. Existing prose-only LA10 banks and vague invisible-image questions are insufficient.

## LA12 — punctuation signalling dialogue

**Read:** Australian Government [Style Manual: Quotation marks](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/grammar-punctuation-and-conventions/punctuation/quotation-marks), updated **19 March2025**, direct-speech and punctuation-placement sections, through split speech and avoiding doubled end marks. Worked examples distinguish a statement followed by an attribution, a quoted question, attribution-first speech and a quotation interrupted by a reporting clause. The government guide prefers single quotation marks; that is a house style, not proof that consistent double marks are ungrammatical. Its notes on plays/transcripts distinguish labelled speaker turns from prose dialogue.

**Original implications:** preserve the site’s consistent illustrated quotation convention; state it in focused editing tasks rather than creating two valid single/double variants as competing answers. Have learners locate actual spoken words, add paired quotation marks, capitals, commas and appropriate boundaries, then compose and punctuate their own short dialogue. Keep a question mark/exclamation mark where it belongs; do not add a redundant comma immediately after it. A reporting clause continues the sentence unless the model starts a genuinely new sentence. New-speaker paragraphing can support clarity, but do not confuse automatic line wrapping with a speaker change. No legal consequences or professional editorial apparatus need be imported into child-facing lessons.
