AC9M9N01 — REAL NUMBERS AND DIGITAL TOOLS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Recognise that the real number system includes the rational numbers and the irrational numbers, and solve problems involving real numbers using digital tools.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Connect natural, whole, integer, rational and irrational subsets within the real numbers. Classify terminating/repeating decimals, non-perfect roots and π; compare/order exact and approximate values; locate irrational numbers; solve mixed real-number problems with calculators/spreadsheets while interpreting rounding and display limits. Complex numbers and formal irrationality proofs are outside the required Year 9 endpoint.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: A.1 classify numbers; A.2–A.4 compare/order/convert rational numbers; A.5 square roots; A.6 cube roots; A.7 irrational numbers on number lines; B.1–B.7 real-number operations.
A.7 example actually opened: locate √95. IXL lists perfect squares 1 through 100, brackets 81<95<100, takes roots to obtain 9<√95<10, and selects the only point between 9 and 10. This models exact bracketing before decimal approximation.
Progression: subset hierarchy → decimal behaviour → perfect/non-perfect roots → exact versus approximate forms → ordering/number-line placement → operations and contextual/digital problem solving.
Misconceptions: irrational means “not real”; all roots are irrational; all non-terminating decimals are irrational; π=3.14 exactly; calculator display is the complete value; √95 lies halfway between 9 and 10; rounding early preserves exact comparisons; division by zero is a real number.

AC9M9A01 — EXPONENT LAWS WITH INTEGER EXPONENTS AND VARIABLES
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Apply the exponent laws to numerical expressions with integer exponents and extend to variables.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Apply same-base product/quotient, power of a power, power of a product/quotient, zero and negative exponent laws to numerical and variable expressions. State non-zero-base/denominator conditions, write answers with requested positive exponents, and distinguish coefficients from variable powers. Fractional exponents are outside this descriptor.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: R.1–R.13 powers and exponent laws through integer exponents/equivalence; T.2–T.5 monomial operations and powers.
- R.10 example actually opened: simplify 796⁰×796⁰ using positive exponents. IXL adds same-base exponents to get 796⁰, then uses 796⁰=1.
- T.5 example actually opened: simplify (5p³)². IXL raises each factor, evaluates 5²=25 and applies the power rule (p³)²=p⁶, giving 25p⁶.
Progression: repeated factors → positive laws → zero exponent via quotient → negative exponent as reciprocal → mixed integer exponents → variables/monomials → equivalence/conditions.
Misconceptions: multiplying bases or exponents in a same-base product; a⁰=0; a⁻ⁿ is negative; (a+b)ⁿ distributes; forgetting to power the coefficient; dividing unlike bases by subtracting exponents; cancelling across addition; allowing zero under a negative exponent; leaving negative exponents when positive form is requested.

AC9M9A02 — ALGEBRAIC EXPRESSIONS, BINOMIAL EXPANSION AND MONIC FACTORISATION
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Simplify algebraic expressions, expand binomial products and factorise monic quadratic expressions.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Collect like terms, apply distributive law, operate with polynomials, expand two binomials including sign/special cases, factorise by common factor and factorise x²+bx+c with integer factors. Non-monic general quadratic factorisation is beyond the stated endpoint.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: L.2–L.3 simplification/equivalence; U.1–U.13 polynomial vocabulary, operations, binomial products, common factors and monic quadratics.
- U.8 example actually opened: expand (3h−2)(3h−4). IXL uses distributive/FOIL products 9h², −12h, −6h and 8, then combines middle terms to obtain 9h²−18h+8.
- U.13 example actually opened: factorise w²+4w+3. IXL seeks integers with product 3 and sum 4, chooses 1 and 3, writes (w+1)(w+3), then expands to check.
Progression: vocabulary/like terms → polynomial addition/subtraction → monomial distribution → binomial grid/distribution → special products → common factors → monic trinomials → expand-to-check.
Misconceptions: combining unlike powers; losing negative signs; multiplying only first/last terms; (a+b)²=a²+b²; choosing numbers that sum to c and multiply to b; ignoring sign combinations; factorising only part of an expression; using non-monic shortcuts here without understanding; failing to check by expansion.

AC9M9A03 — GRADIENT, MIDPOINT AND DISTANCE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Find the gradient of a line segment, the midpoint of the line interval and the distance between 2 distinct points on the Cartesian plane.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Use ordered coordinates in all quadrants; calculate signed gradient as Δy/Δx, recognise horizontal gradient 0 and undefined vertical gradient; average coordinates for midpoint; derive distance from horizontal/vertical changes and Pythagoras; retain exact radicals or round appropriately. Do not confuse segment length with coordinate differences.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: Q.2–Q.4 gradient; J.2–J.3 midpoint/endpoint; J.4 distance.
- Q.3 example actually opened: through (10,2) and (3,4), IXL substitutes (4−2)/(3−10)=2/(−7)=−2/7. Consistent point order preserves the sign.
- J.2 example actually opened: endpoints (4,8) and (8,10). IXL averages x- and y-coordinates separately to obtain midpoint (6,9), then displays it between the endpoints.
- J.4 example actually opened: distance between (9,6) and (3,−2). IXL builds a right triangle, finds vertical/horizontal changes 8 and 6 using absolute differences, applies 8²+6²=c²=100 and selects positive distance 10.
Progression: coordinate changes → gradient and special cases → midpoint → distance via Pythagoras → missing endpoint/coordinate → integrated geometry/context problems.
Misconceptions: rise/run with mismatched point order; using absolute change for gradient; calling vertical slope zero; averaging all four numbers together; subtracting rather than averaging midpoint coordinates; Manhattan distance; forgetting square root; accepting negative distance; rounding radical distances too early.

AC9M9A04 — QUADRATIC FUNCTIONS, GRAPHS AND EQUATIONS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Identify and graph quadratic functions, solve quadratic equations graphically and numerically, and solve monic quadratic equations with integer roots algebraically, using graphing software and digital tools as appropriate.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Recognise quadratics from rules, parabolas and constant second differences; generate tables and graph; identify vertex, axis, intercepts and opening; solve from x-intercepts, numerical trial/table and factorised zero-product form for monic integer-root equations. Quadratic formula, completing the square and general non-monic algebraic solving are outside the stated endpoint.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: V.1–V.6 identify/characterise/table/graph quadratics; V.7 square-root equations; V.8 zero-product; V.9 monic factorisation solving; V.10 transformations.
- V.6 example actually opened: graph y=6x². IXL rewrites as 6(x−0)²+0, identifies vertex (0,0), notes positive a opens upward, finds another point (1/2,3/2) and plots the parabola.
- V.9 example actually opened: solve z²−6z−7=0. IXL finds factor pair 1 and −7 (product −7, sum −6), writes (z+1)(z−7)=0, applies zero-product property and obtains z=−1 or 7.
Progression: linear-versus-quadratic/second differences → tables → graph features → software parameter exploration → graphical/numerical roots → factorise monic quadratics → zero-product solving → cross-check representations.
Misconceptions: constant first difference; plotting only a vertex and one unsymmetrical point without reflecting; assuming all parabolas open upward; confusing y-intercept and root; reading approximate graph roots as exact; factoring without first setting equation to zero; solving only one factor; product zero means both factors zero; using vertex-form transformations without accounting for signs.
