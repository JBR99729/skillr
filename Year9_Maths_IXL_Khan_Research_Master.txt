AC9M9N01 — REAL NUMBERS AND DIGITAL TOOLS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Recognise that the real number system includes the rational numbers and the irrational numbers, and solve problems involving real numbers using digital tools.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Connect natural, whole, integer, rational and irrational subsets within the real numbers. Classify terminating/repeating decimals, non-perfect roots and π; compare/order exact and approximate values; locate irrational numbers; solve mixed real-number problems with calculators/spreadsheets while interpreting rounding and display limits. Complex numbers and formal irrationality proofs are outside the required Year 9 endpoint.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: A.1 classify numbers; A.2–A.4 compare/order/convert rational numbers; A.5 square roots; A.6 cube roots; A.7 irrational numbers on number lines; B.1–B.7 real-number operations.
A.7 example actually opened: locate √95. IXL lists perfect squares 1 through 100, brackets 81<95<100, takes roots to obtain 9<√95<10, and selects the only point between 9 and 10. This models exact bracketing before decimal approximation.
Progression: subset hierarchy → decimal behaviour → perfect/non-perfect roots → exact versus approximate forms → ordering/number-line placement → operations and contextual/digital problem solving.
Misconceptions: irrational means “not real”; all roots are irrational; all non-terminating decimals are irrational; π=3.14 exactly; calculator display is the complete value; √95 lies halfway between 9 and 10; rounding early preserves exact comparisons; division by zero is a real number.

STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Apply the exponent laws to numerical expressions with integer exponents and extend to variables.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Apply same-base product/quotient, power of a power, power of a product/quotient, zero and negative exponent laws to numerical and variable expressions. State non-zero-base/denominator conditions, write answers with requested positive exponents, and distinguish coefficients from variable powers. Fractional exponents are outside this descriptor.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE

AC9M9A03 — GRADIENT, MIDPOINT AND DISTANCE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Find the gradient of a line segment, the midpoint of the line interval and the distance between 2 distinct points on the Cartesian plane.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Use ordered coordinates in all quadrants; calculate signed gradient as Δy/Δx, recognise horizontal gradient 0 and undefined vertical gradient; average coordinates for midpoint; derive distance from horizontal/vertical changes and Pythagoras; retain exact radicals or round appropriately. Do not confuse segment length with coordinate differences.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Direct mappings: Q.2–Q.4 gradient; J.2–J.3 midpoint/endpoint; J.4 distance.
- Q.3 example actually opened: through (10,2) and (3,4), IXL substitutes (4−2)/(3−10)=2/(−7)=−2/7. Consistent point order preserves the sign.
- J.2 example actually opened: endpoints (4,8) and (8,10). IXL averages x- and y-coordinates separately to obtain midpoint (6,9), then displays it between the endpoints.
- J.4 example actually opened: distance between (9,6) and (3,−2). IXL builds a right triangle, finds vertical/horizontal changes 8 and 6 using absolute differences, applies 8²+6²=c²=100 and selects positive distance 10.
Progression: coordinate changes → gradient and special cases → midpoint → distance via Pythagoras → missing endpoint/coordinate → integrated geometry/context problems.
Misconceptions: rise/run with mismatched point order; using absolute change for gradient; calling vertical slope zero; averaging all four numbers together; subtracting rather than averaging midpoint coordinates; Manhattan distance; forgetting square root; accepting negative distance; rounding radical distances too early.

AC9M9A05 — MATHEMATICAL MODELLING OF CHANGE
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Use mathematical modelling to solve applied problems involving change, including financial contexts; formulate problems, choose either linear or quadratic functions, interpret solutions in context, evaluate the model, and report methods and findings.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must complete the modelling cycle rather than merely substitute into a supplied rule: define variables and assumptions, decide whether constant first differences/rates suggest a linear model or constant second differences/curvature suggest a quadratic model, construct the rule, solve with suitable algebra/graphs/digital tools, interpret units and domain, test reasonableness and limitations, and communicate a conclusion. Financial settings can include fixed-plus-variable cost, revenue/profit, depreciation and break-even; compound interest formulas are not implied by this descriptor.

IXL MAPPING AND ACTUAL EXAMPLE EVIDENCE
Closest mappings: X.4 rate-of-travel word problems; Q linear functions/graphs; V quadratic functions/graphs; financial arithmetic skills provide context. The full formulate–choose–evaluate–report cycle is SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED as one IXL skill.
X.4 example actually opened: two lifts leave the same floor in opposite directions at 110 m/min and 220 m/min; when are they 120 m apart? IXL uses d=rt, adds rates because separation grows at 330 m/min, solves 120=330t to get 0.36363… minutes, then converts to about 22 seconds. The method exposes variables, rate-of-change structure, units and contextual interpretation, but future content must also make students state assumptions and evaluate model fitness.
Progression: read context/data → define quantities and domain → compare difference patterns/shape → select linear or quadratic family → estimate parameters/rule → solve → interpret with units → validate against data/constraints → report.
Misconceptions: choosing from keywords alone; assuming every change is linear; using distance rates with the wrong relative-motion operation; confusing revenue with profit; accepting negative time or impossible values; reporting a graph coordinate without meaning/units; overfitting limited data; treating extrapolation as certain; omitting assumptions or evaluation.
