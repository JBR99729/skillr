YEAR 7 SCIENCE · AC9S7I08

Keep the evidence while changing the audience

Learning intention: Write and create accurate science texts for specific readers and purposes, selecting useful language, features and digital tools.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One investigation, two useful explanations

  1. Same evidence: 12 cm rotor, mean 1.2 s; 20 cm rotor, mean 1.7 s.
  2. Different readers: explain fall time for a family; give method context for classmates.
  3. Preserved meaning: 0.5 s difference in these two tested configurations.
  4. Revision: check values, units, limits, captions and source/use conditions.

A family reader may need “fall time” explained as the time taken to reach the ground. A peer report needs the measured comparison, method context and limits in a more structured form. Both versions must preserve the 0.5 s mean difference and the fact that only two configurations were compared. Changing the audience is not permission to change the evidence.

Model for a family reader

In our supplied paper-spinner records, the longer rotor took more time to reach the ground. The 20 cm rotor averaged 1.7 seconds, compared with 1.2 seconds for the 12 cm rotor. Only two sizes and these conditions were compared.

Model for a peer investigation report

Mean fall time was 1.7 s for the 20 cm rotor and 1.2 s for the 12 cm rotor, a difference of 0.5 s. Each configuration had three reset drops from 1.5 m under matched mass and release conditions. The records support this comparison; they do not establish a rule for every rotor length or independently made spinner.

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Plan purpose before choosing words

Decide who will read the text, what they already know and what they need to understand or do. Select the core claim and evidence before choosing a format. A report, explanation and persuasive letter serve different purposes.

Reader needs A younger reader may need “fall time” defined. Classmates comparing methods need rotor sizes, units, release height, repeat structure and a limitation.

Try it · Write one accurate sentence for a younger reader using 12 cm/1.2 s and 20 cm/1.7 s.

For example: “In these records, the 20 cm paper rotor took 1.7 seconds to reach the ground on average, longer than the 1.2 seconds for the 12 cm rotor.” Explain that these are the tested sizes.

2. Make features explain the science

A table preserves exact records; a labelled graph highlights a comparison; a caption states what the reader should notice. A diagram or animation should explain a relationship or process, rather than only decorate it.

Try it · Write a useful caption for a graph of the two mean fall times.

For example: “Mean fall time for two rotor lengths under the supplied conditions: 1.2 s at 12 cm and 1.7 s at 20 cm, based on three reset drops per configuration.”

3. Create, check and revise

Write a first version, check it against the source data and ask for specific feedback about meaning, audience and evidence. Use digital tools where they help; verify charts, units, captions, source information and accessibility.

Revision checklist Accurate claim and values; relevant units; defined unfamiliar terms; useful structure and caption; visible limitations; appropriate source/use conditions; readable layout and alternative text for meaningful images.

Try it · Repair “Long wings always make every spinner fall exactly half as fast.”

A suitable revision is: “In the supplied records, the 20 cm rotor had a longer mean fall time than the 12 cm rotor: 1.7 s versus 1.2 s. Other sizes and spinners were not tested.” The original also confuses fall time with an unsupported exact speed claim.

Common mistakes

“Simpler language means dropping important limits.” Use clear words while preserving qualifications that change the scientific meaning.

“A polished digital chart must be correct.” Check the input values, axes, units and message. Software presentation does not validate the evidence.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create two versions and revise one

Using the spinner table, write a short explanation for a family reader and a results paragraph for classmates. Add a graph caption or a simple storyboard panel that explains the comparison. Ask a partner to identify one unclear term or unsupported claim, then revise that exact part.

Adjust the challenge Support: use the same values in both versions and define fall time. Extend: justify a useful digital format and write alternative text that preserves the graph’s main comparison and limitation.

Use paper or an appropriate tool with supplied data. Do not climb to perform drops. Do not include personal information, unauthorised photographs or restricted cultural material in a public text.

Three tricky questions

1. Do these records prove a 30 cm rotor would have an even longer fall time?

No. Only 12 cm and 20 cm configurations were supplied; a new size needs evidence. State a tentative prediction as such rather than reporting it as a finding.

2. Why can “fell slower” be less precise than a measured fall-time statement here?

The records directly give elapsed times for a set height. Reporting those times with units and conditions is clearer than implying a detailed speed measurement throughout the fall.

3. Write an informative comparison that includes the difference and a limitation.

For example: “The 20 cm rotor’s mean fall time was 0.5 s longer than the 12 cm rotor’s in these three reset drops per configuration; this does not establish a result for all spinner designs.”

FAQ

Must every science text be a full laboratory report?

No. Use a genre suited to the purpose and audience while keeping the scientific meaning accurate.

Does alternative text need to name every decorative detail?

No. Describe the important information or relationship the image contributes. For a data chart, include its main comparison and relevant limits.

Key concept

Scientific communication is audience-aware, evidence-based and purposeful. You may change vocabulary, depth, structure and visual support for different audiences, but you must not change the scientific meaning or overstate the evidence.

Fast test: Who is the audience? What is the purpose? Which language and text features help that audience understand the evidence?

Topic guide

1. Purpose, audience, language and text features (E1)

Scientific texts can inform, explain, report, persuade or argue. Infer the intended audience from vocabulary, tone, assumed knowledge, amount of detail and representations such as diagrams, graphs, headings and captions.

2. Investigation reports (E2)

A strong report communicates the aim, hypothesis, materials, reproducible method, results, discussion and conclusion. Use tables and graphs when they reveal patterns. Discuss accuracy and whether another person could repeat the method and obtain comparable findings.

3. Dichotomous-key app prototype (E3)

Use pairs of clear, mutually exclusive choices based on observable features. For a community audience, keep questions concise, navigation obvious and images or icons purposeful.

4. Letter to the editor (E4)

State a clear environmental viewpoint, support it with relevant evidence, use respectful persuasive language and finish with a realistic call to action. Persuasion does not justify exaggerating what the evidence shows.

5. Explaining seasons to a younger audience (E5)

Use simple language and a helpful diagram. Earth rotates to produce day and night. Earth’s axis is tilted; as Earth revolves around the Sun, each hemisphere receives changing sunlight angles and day lengths across the year, producing the seasons.

6. Particle-theory animation (E6)

Animation is useful because it can represent movement and change over time. Show solids as closely packed particles vibrating, liquids as close particles moving past one another and gases as widely spaced particles moving freely. Use labels or narration so the motion is interpreted correctly.

10 Important Questions & Answers
  1. What determines how a scientific text should be written? Its purpose, intended audience and the evidence or idea being communicated.
  2. Can you simplify science for younger readers? Yes—simplify language and support, but preserve the scientific meaning.
  3. What makes a method reproducible? Clear, measurable steps with equipment, quantities, units and controlled conditions.
  4. When should a graph be used? When a graph reveals a pattern, comparison or relationship more clearly than prose alone.
  5. What makes dichotomous-key choices effective? Two objective, mutually exclusive options based on observable features.
  6. What makes a letter to the editor scientifically responsible? A clear position supported by relevant evidence without overstating certainty.
  7. Why are captions useful? They tell the audience what a visual shows and how it relates to the scientific message.
  8. What causes the seasons? Earth’s axial tilt combined with its revolution around the Sun changes sunlight angle and day length through the year.
  9. Why is animation useful for particle theory? It can show motion, spacing and changes of state dynamically.
  10. How do you evaluate whether communication works? Check whether the intended audience can follow the structure, understand the language and interpret the science accurately.
Common mistakes
  • Changing scientific facts while trying to simplify language.
  • Using visuals as decoration rather than evidence or explanation.
  • Calling repeated measurements “accurate” just because they are close together.
  • Writing a method too vaguely for another person to repeat.
  • Using subjective or overlapping dichotomous-key choices.
  • Using persuasive language that claims more than the evidence supports.
  • Saying Earth’s changing distance from the Sun causes the seasons.
Revision Notes

Purpose & Audience

  • Purpose: inform, explain, report, persuade or argue.
  • Audience: younger students, peers, public, experts or decision-makers.
  • Adapt vocabulary, tone, detail, structure and visuals—not scientific truth.

Investigation Reports

  • Aim → hypothesis → materials → method → results → discussion → conclusion.
  • Use diagrams/tables/graphs only when they help communicate evidence.
  • Discuss accuracy, anomalies, limitations and reproducibility.

Dichotomous Keys

  • Two choices per step.
  • Use observable, non-overlapping features.
  • Prototype for users: clear buttons, simple navigation, useful images/icons.

Letters to the Editor

  • Viewpoint + evidence + reasoning + respectful persuasion + call to action.

Younger Audiences

  • Shorter sentences, define key words, use purposeful diagrams and relatable examples.

Animations

  • Storyboard first; use movement only when it represents the science; label or narrate what viewers should notice.
