Build a claim that the evidence can carry

Learning intention: Construct an argument with relevant evidence and reasoning, consider limits and use secondary information ethically.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two cover layers lost less water in these trials

A bounded claim is that the two-layer group had lower water loss than the one-layer group under the supplied conditions. The mean losses were 9 g and 12 g over 24 h, a 3 g difference. Water leaving as vapour reduces the recorded mass, under the stated assumptions. Covering may affect energy input or air movement, but this design does not isolate those mechanisms. These records do not establish that every crop or container will save the same amount of water.

Learn the skill

1. Connect claim, evidence and reasoning

The claim answers the question. Evidence gives relevant, accurately reported observations. Reasoning explains how the evidence bears on the claim using an appropriate science idea. A list of numbers without the connection is incomplete.

Try it · Give a claim and one numerical comparison for two layers versus no cover.

In the supplied conditions, two-layer cups lost less water on average: 9 g versus 18 g over 24 h. The comparison concerns these groups, not every possible container.

2. Consider limits and counterevidence

Ask whether the method and sample support the claim’s scope. Relevant contrary evidence needs examination. Repeating the same study in two news articles is not independent confirmation.

Compare claims “Lower mean water loss in these cups over 24 h” fits the records. “Two layers always double plant growth” introduces an unmeasured outcome and a universal claim.

Try it · Why does the plant-growth claim fail even though the table has several readings?

Plant growth was not measured. More water-loss numbers cannot supply missing growth evidence or justify a claim about all plants.

3. Check source meaning and permitted use

For secondary information, identify creator, title/date, original evidence, source and relevant limits. A citation does not automatically make a claim valid or grant permission to reproduce culturally governed knowledge.

Knowledge-governance case A public project description names a particular First Nations community and knowledge holders, with specific conditions for using its material. A proposed public adaptation has not been authorised.

Try it · Does adding the source name permit the adaptation?

No. Check the relevant knowledge holders’ authority and actual conditions for that intended use. Respect restrictions, avoid sensitive disclosure and seek appropriate permission or choose a permitted alternative.

Common mistakes

“A citation makes any claim scientific.” Check what the source actually establishes, the evidence quality and whether the claim preserves its scope.

“More sources always mean more independent evidence.” Several sources may repeat the same underlying study or weak claim. Trace the evidence rather than just counting links.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write and qualify an argument

Use the supplied cup data to argue whether two layers reduced water loss compared with one. Include the mean comparison, a scientific connection, one limitation and a condition you would check before applying the result elsewhere. Then identify one source-use or cultural-permission check for a secondary source.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “My claim… The evidence… This matters because… A limit is…”. Extend: write a counterargument to a universal crop-growth claim without dismissing the useful cup result.

Use the supplied data. Do not taste liquids, heat sealed containers or reproduce cultural knowledge or artwork on the assumption that a public source grants unrestricted use.

Three tricky questions

1. Is lower water loss in these cups evidence that plants in them would grow faster?

Not by itself. Plant growth, species needs and other conditions would need investigation. Keep the claim tied to the measured water-loss outcome.

2. Could a reputable secondary review be useful evidence?

Yes. Judge its methods, relevance, original evidence and limitations. Being primary or secondary alone does not settle quality for every question.

3. Should you hide an inconvenient independent result to make your argument stronger?

No. Explain the relevant counterevidence and compare methods or scope. A qualified, honest judgement is stronger than selective reporting.

FAQ

Does evidence-based argument mean everyone must choose the same policy?

No. People may weigh justified criteria differently, but the scientific evidence and its limits must be represented accurately.

Is public access the same as permission to share cultural material?

No. Access, attribution, adaptation and publication conditions can differ. Follow the relevant knowledge holders and the intended-use conditions.

Key concept

A scientific argument is not an opinion. It combines a clear claim, relevant evidence and reasoning that explains why the evidence supports or challenges the claim. Strong arguments also acknowledge limitations, source credibility, uncertainty and ethical responsibilities.

Topic guide

1. Construct evidence-based arguments (E1)

Start with a testable hypothesis or claim. Decide whether the evidence supports, challenges or is insufficient to decide. Select relevant evidence, then explain the scientific reasoning that links it to the conclusion. Avoid saying a hypothesis is “proved” by one investigation.

2. Draw logical conclusions from evidence quality (E2)

Before making a broad conclusion, examine how the data were collected, sample size, uncontrolled variables, measurement quality, duration, missing evidence and uncertainty. A strong conclusion matches the scope of the evidence.

3. Determine source credibility (E3)

Credibility is not decided by one feature alone. Check authorship or responsible organisation, expertise, evidence, methods or references, publication date, publisher, purpose, possible bias, and whether claims can be traced to original evidence.

4. Megafauna extinction debate (E4)

Students investigate cultural, historical, archaeological, fossil and climate evidence used in debate about Australian megafauna extinction. The goal is not to memorise a single cause. Compare what each line of evidence supports, where it is limited, and whether conclusions are local or broad.

Archaeological

Balanced conclusion: evidence may support several interacting explanations; uncertainty should be acknowledged rather than hidden.

5. Ethical use and commercialisation of First Nations knowledge (E5)

First Nations knowledges are diverse, place-based and culturally governed. Responsible research or commercial use should identify the relevant knowledge holders and follow appropriate cultural authority, consent, attribution, intellectual-property arrangements and benefit-sharing. Do not assume information is free to use simply because it appears in a secondary source.

10 Important Questions & Answers
  1. What is a scientific argument? A claim supported by relevant evidence and reasoning.
  2. Does evidence have to support a hypothesis? No. Good science can support, challenge or leave a hypothesis unresolved.
  3. What makes a conclusion logical? It matches the evidence, method, limitations and scope of the investigation.
  4. How do you judge source credibility? Check authorship, evidence, methods/references, date, purpose, bias and traceability.
  5. Is a peer-reviewed source automatically correct? No. Peer review strengthens scrutiny, but methods, evidence and later research still matter.
  6. Why use multiple evidence types in the megafauna debate? Each type answers different questions and has different limitations.
  7. How should First Nations knowledge be cited? According to the relevant community’s cultural authority, permissions, context and attribution protocols.
  8. What is biopiracy? Exploitation of biological resources or associated traditional knowledge without appropriate permission, recognition or fair arrangements.
  9. Why is benefit-sharing important? It recognises the contribution and rights of knowledge holders and supports fair negotiated outcomes.
  10. What is the best exam structure? Claim → evidence → reasoning → limitations/alternative evidence → qualified conclusion.
Common mistakes
  • Listing evidence without explaining how it supports the claim.
  • Saying a hypothesis is “proved” by one investigation.
  • Choosing sources only because they agree with the preferred conclusion.
  • Assuming newer or official-looking sources are automatically correct.
  • Generalising one First Nations community’s knowledge to all First Nations Australians.
  • Using published cultural knowledge without checking whether it is appropriate to reproduce.
  • Treating uncertainty as weakness instead of part of a balanced scientific conclusion.
Revision Notes

Argument checklist

  • State a clear claim.
  • Select relevant evidence, not just evidence you like.
  • Explain reasoning.
  • Address limitations or conflicting evidence.
  • Qualify the conclusion to match confidence.

Source credibility checklist

  • Author / organisation and expertise
  • Evidence and methods
  • References and traceability
  • Publication date and context
  • Purpose, funding or possible bias

Ethical use checklist

  • Identify the relevant First Nations knowledge holders.
  • Check cultural authority and permission.
  • Use appropriate attribution.
  • Do not reproduce restricted information.
  • Consider consent, IP arrangements and benefit-sharing in commercial contexts.
