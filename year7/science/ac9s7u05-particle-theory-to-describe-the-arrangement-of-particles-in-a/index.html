Particle Theory, States of Matter & Diffusion

Use particle models to explain why solids, liquids and gases behave differently, how heating and cooling change particle motion, and how particle spacing and attraction help explain density, melting point, compressibility and diffusion.

What you need to know

Particle theory is a model used to explain the behaviour of matter. The model treats a substance as made of tiny particles that are always moving, have spaces between them and attract one another. Their arrangement, motion, energy and attraction help explain the observable properties of solids, liquids and gases.

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

  • draw and interpret particle models for solids, liquids and gases
  • describe how heating and cooling change particle motion and energy
  • explain melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation using particles
  • compare particle attraction and freedom of movement in the three states
  • explain shape, volume and compressibility using particle theory
  • use particle ideas to reason about density and melting point
  • explain diffusion in liquids and gases and why temperature affects its rate
  • identify limitations of simplified particle diagrams

1. Particle arrangement in solids, liquids and gases — E1, E2 & E3

Solid

Particles are close together and held in fixed average positions. They still vibrate.

Liquid

Particles remain close but can move and slide past one another.

Gas

Particles are widely separated and move freely throughout the available space.

Important model limit: the dots are not drawn to scale. The spaces in a particle diagram are not pockets of air, and the particles themselves do not grow when a substance is heated. The model represents changes in average motion, arrangement and spacing.

2. Heating, cooling and changes of state — E1 & E4

When a substance absorbs heat energy, its particles generally gain kinetic energy and move or vibrate more strongly. When heat energy is released, particle motion decreases. During a physical change of state, the substance remains the same substance: the particle arrangement, movement and interaction change.

solid→ melting →liquid→ boiling / evaporation →gas→ condensation →liquid→ freezing →solid

Energy absorbed

Melting: particles gain enough energy to leave fixed positions and move past one another.

Evaporation/boiling: particles gain enough energy for gas formation.

Energy released

Condensation: gas particles lose energy and become much closer together as a liquid forms.

Freezing: liquid particles lose energy and become fixed in a solid structure.

Boiling is not the same as evaporation

Evaporation occurs at the surface and can happen over a range of temperatures. Boiling occurs throughout a liquid at its boiling point under the stated conditions, producing bubbles of vapour within the liquid.

3. Why temperature can stay constant during a state change

Between state changes, added energy increases particle motion and temperature. During the idealised flat sections, energy is still being transferred, but it is used in changing the arrangement and interactions needed for the new state rather than increasing temperature at that moment.

Density

Density is calculated using density = mass ÷ volume. Particle spacing can help explain density differences, especially when comparing states of the same substance. For different materials, particle mass also matters, so “closer particles always means denser” is too simple as a universal rule.

Melting point

A substance that requires more energy to overcome the interactions holding its solid structure together generally has a higher melting point than one whose solid structure is easier to disrupt.

Compressibility

A gas can be compressed substantially because there is a great deal of empty space between particles. Liquids and solids already have particles close together, so there is much less space to remove.

5. Diffusion in liquids and gases — E7

Diffusion is the net spreading of particles from a region of higher concentration toward regions of lower concentration because particles are in constant random motion.

Diffusion is usually faster when particles move more quickly. This is why food colouring generally spreads faster through warm water than through cold water, and why gases often spread more quickly than dissolved particles in liquids.

6. Five worked numerical & application examples

Example 1 — Follow a state change

Problem: Ice at −5°C is heated until it becomes liquid water at 20°C. Describe the particle changes.

  1. While the solid warms, particles remain in fixed average positions but vibrate more strongly.
  2. During melting, added energy changes the solid arrangement so particles can move past one another.
  3. After melting, further heating increases liquid-particle motion.
  4. The particles remain water particles throughout this physical change.

Answer: Motion increases with heating; the arrangement changes from fixed solid positions to mobile liquid particles.

Check: Do not draw larger particles after heating; change motion/arrangement instead.

Example 2 — Calculate density

Problem: A liquid has mass 240 g and volume 300 mL. Find its density.

  1. Use density = mass ÷ volume.
  2. Substitute: density = 240 g ÷ 300 mL.
  3. Calculate 0.80 g/mL.
  4. Interpret density as mass packed into each millilitre of the liquid.

Answer: 0.80 g/mL.

Check: 0.80 × 300 = 240 g, recovering the given mass.

Example 3 — Explain a melting plateau

Problem: A heating curve is flat while a solid melts even though energy is still being supplied. Why?

  1. Recognise that the substance is changing state.
  2. The model treats the added energy as changing particle arrangement and interactions.
  3. Temperature therefore stays constant through the idealised plateau.
  4. After melting is complete, added energy again raises temperature.

Answer: Energy is transferred during melting without increasing temperature in the idealised plateau because the particle arrangement is changing.

Check: A flat graph does not mean “no energy transfer”.

Example 4 — Compare compressibility

Problem: Why can a sealed syringe of air be compressed much more than a syringe full of water?

  1. Gas particles are far apart, so there is substantial empty space.
  2. Pushing the plunger reduces the average spacing between gas particles.
  3. Liquid particles are already close together.
  4. Therefore the water volume changes very little under the same simple push.

Answer: Air is much more compressible because its particles have much larger spaces between them.

Check: The gas particles themselves are not being squashed smaller.

Example 5 — Predict diffusion rate

Problem: Food colouring spreads through warm water faster than cold water. Explain.

  1. Warmer water has greater average particle motion.
  2. Water and colouring particles move and collide more rapidly.
  3. Random motion mixes the colouring through the water more quickly.
  4. The final explanation must refer to particle motion, not just “heat makes it spread”.

Answer: Greater particle motion at the higher temperature increases the rate of diffusion.

Check: The colouring still diffuses in cold water; it simply does so more slowly.

Common misconceptions and model limits

  • “Particles stop moving in a solid.” They vibrate about fixed average positions.
  • “Particles get bigger when heated.” The particle model changes motion and average spacing, not particle size.
  • “The gaps in the model are air.” The gaps represent space between particles; they are not automatically filled with another substance.
  • “A change of state creates a new substance.” Melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation are physical changes of state.
  • “Boiling and evaporation are identical.” Evaporation occurs at a surface over a range of temperatures; boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point under the stated conditions.
  • “Flat temperature means no energy is being added.” During an idealised state-change plateau, energy changes particle arrangement rather than temperature.
  • “All dense materials simply have particles closer together.” Particle spacing matters, but particle mass also affects density when different materials are compared.
  • “Diffusion is directed movement.” Random particle motion produces a net spread from high to low concentration.

10 important problems to solve

  1. Particle model: Describe or draw solid, liquid and gas particles of the same substance, showing arrangement, motion and relative spacing.
    Check answer

    Solid: close particles in fixed average positions, vibrating. Liquid: close but disordered particles moving past one another. Gas: widely separated particles moving freely throughout the container.

  2. Heating and melting: A block of ice at −5°C is heated until it becomes water at 20°C. Explain what happens to particle motion and arrangement before, during and after melting.
    Check answer

    Solid particles vibrate more as the ice warms; during melting the fixed arrangement breaks down so particles can move past one another; after melting, further heating increases liquid-particle motion.

  3. Density: A liquid has mass 240 g and volume 300 mL. Calculate its density.
    Check answer

    Density = 240 ÷ 300 = 0.80 g/mL.

  4. Compressibility: Explain why a syringe of air compresses easily but a syringe of water changes volume very little.
    Check answer

    Gas particles have large spaces between them that can be reduced; liquid particles are already close together, so little space can be removed.

  5. Melting point: Substance A melts at 50°C and substance B at 900°C. What does the large difference suggest about the energy needed to disrupt their solid particle arrangements?
    Check answer

    Substance B requires much more energy to disrupt the interactions holding its solid structure together, consistent with its much higher melting point.

  6. Diffusion: Perfume spreads across a room faster than food colouring spreads through still water. Explain using particle motion and spacing.
    Check answer

    Gas particles are widely spaced and move rapidly, allowing faster mixing through the available space; particles in liquids are much closer and diffusion is typically slower.

  7. Heating curve: A graph has a flat section while a solid melts. Why does temperature remain constant even though heat energy is being added?
    Check answer

    In the idealised model, the added energy is used to change particle arrangement/interactions during the state change instead of increasing temperature until melting is complete.

  8. Gas in a rigid container: A sealed rigid container of gas is heated. Predict what happens to particle motion and pressure.
    Check answer

    Particles move faster and collide with the walls more frequently and/or more forcefully, so pressure increases while the rigid container keeps volume fixed.

  9. Temperature and diffusion: Food colouring spreads faster in warm water than cold water. Explain why.
    Check answer

    Higher temperature corresponds to greater average particle motion, so random mixing and diffusion occur more rapidly.

  10. Condensation: A gas is cooled until it becomes a liquid. Explain the changes in energy, motion and spacing.
    Check answer

    Particles lose energy and move less rapidly; they become much closer together as the liquid state forms, while the substance remains the same substance.
