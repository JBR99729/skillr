Describe the pattern, then check the exception

Learning intention: Use numerical evidence to describe trends and relationships, investigate anomalies and qualify predictions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

More shade and a higher moisture reading: what can we conclude?

Across these four sites, greater shade is associated with higher soil-moisture readings: from 18% at 20% shade to 34% at 80% shade. The moisture reading rises by 16 percentage points across those observations. This is a positive relationship in the supplied data. It does not establish that shade alone caused the difference: soil, recent watering, drainage or sampling conditions may also vary.

Learn the skill

1. State the pattern with evidence

A trend is an overall direction through ordered data. A relationship describes how variables vary together. A repeated rise and fall can form a cyclic pattern. Use values and units rather than only saying “it goes up”.

Try it · Write a supported comparison between sites B and D.

Site D has 80% shade and a 34% moisture reading, compared with 40% shade and 23% moisture at B: the moisture reading is 11 percentage points higher at D.

2. Flag an anomaly without inventing its cause

An unusual value may be a recording error, a method problem or a genuine event. Keep it in the raw record while checking units, transcription, instrument and sampling context.

Try it · Is 61% automatically a mistake, and can you delete it now?

No. It is a candidate anomaly relative to the other readings. Check the record and sampling conditions and flag it; do not invent a typo or remove it only to make the mean smaller.

3. Predict within the evidence

Interpolation estimates between observed values under a stated pattern assumption; extrapolation goes beyond the tested range. Neither guarantees an exact future reading. Observational association alone does not isolate a cause.

Prediction limit For another comparable site near 50% shade, a moisture reading between the supplied 23% and 29% may be a tentative estimate if the pattern and context continue.

Try it · Why can we not promise exactly 26% moisture at every 50%-shaded site?

Only four different sites were observed; the relationship may not be exactly linear and other conditions can differ. State the assumption and uncertainty.

Common mistakes

“The largest number is always an error.” Judge it against the pattern, raw record and context. An unusual observation can be real.

“Association proves the cause.” A relationship can suggest a question, but confounding factors and a suitable design must be considered before making a causal claim.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write an evidence paragraph

Using the four-site shade table, write the overall relationship, two supporting values and one plausible alternative explanation. Then flag the unusual value in 22, 23, 61, 22% and write the first check you would make.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “In these records… from… to…”. Extend: design a controlled shade comparison that would address one alternative explanation, without claiming it has already been performed.

Use the supplied data or supervised observations on an approved route. Do not enter planted areas, dig soil or disturb habitats without appropriate permission.

Three tricky questions

1. From 18% to 34%, is the increase 16 percentage points or necessarily 16 percent?

It is 16 percentage points. A relative percentage increase is a different calculation; do not use the phrases interchangeably.

2. Two variables fluctuate with no consistent relationship in a suitable dataset. Must you draw an increasing trend?

No. Report that the supplied data show no clear relationship rather than forcing a preferred pattern.

3. Could the mean 32% of 22, 23, 61, 22% hide an important feature?

Yes. Three readings cluster near 22–23%, while 61% is unusual. The mean is arithmetically correct but should be accompanied by the raw values and anomaly discussion.

FAQ

Does an anomaly make the whole investigation worthless?

Not automatically. Investigate its cause and explain how it affects the pattern and strength of the conclusion.

Can a monthly seasonal pattern predict one particular day exactly?

No. Monthly summaries and individual days are different; day-to-day variation and other evidence matter.

What students learn in AC9S7I05

This unit helps students build a clear, usable understanding of Analyse data and information to describe patterns, trends and relationships and identify anomalies. The goal is not to memorise one answer pattern. Students should be able to explain the idea, recognise it in a new example and apply it in a short practice or homework task.

  • Start with the main idea: analyse data and information to describe patterns, trends and relationships and identify anomalies.
  • Use concrete examples, pictures, oral explanation and short written responses before moving to independent practice.
  • analysing change in predator and prey numbers over time to identify predator-prey cycles identifying patterns and relationships in data sets such as identifying qualitative relationships between the mass of a planet and its gravity
  • Finish with a mixed activity so students must choose the correct strategy rather than copy the last example.
Key concept

Analysis turns data into evidence by identifying patterns and relationships

Data analysis describes what the evidence shows before explaining why. Look for trends, clusters, outliers and relationships, and use specific values or comparisons rather than vague phrases such as ‘it went up a lot’.

Worked example

If reaction time falls from 42 s at 20 °C to 19 s at 40 °C, describe the pattern quantitatively first. Then connect the trend to a scientific explanation; do not invent causation that the design did not test.

Common misconception

An outlier should not be deleted automatically. Check for recording or method errors and consider whether it may be genuine variation.

Exam tip

A high-quality trend statement includes direction, evidence from values and any important exception or uncertainty.

Predator–prey cycles: compare repeated peaks

In a supplied hypothetical model, observations at eight equal time intervals give prey counts 20, 40, 60, 40, 20, 40, 60, 40 and predator counts 10, 10, 20, 30, 20, 10, 20, 30. Prey peaks occur at intervals 3 and 7; predator peaks occur at 4 and 8, one interval later. Describe the repeated rise and fall and the lag. Food availability is a possible explanation, but the pattern alone does not prove predation is the only cause; habitat, disease and movement can also matter.

Planet mass and gravity: hold distance conditions fixed

For three idealised spherical model planets of the same radius, a fixed test object has surface weights 2, 4 and 6 N when planet masses are 1, 2 and 3 relative mass units. The supplied model has a positive proportional relationship: doubling the planet mass doubles the test object's weight when radius and test-object mass are fixed. Do not apply that simple comparison to arbitrary real planets without considering their radii and the measurement distance. Weight is force; the test object's mass has not changed.

Daylight proportions and seasons

A hypothetical southern-hemisphere site has 10 h daylight in June, 12 h in September, 14 h in December and 12 h in March, each out of 24 h. The daylight fractions are 10/24 = 5/12, 12/24 = 1/2, 14/24 = 7/12 and 12/24 = 1/2; darkness is the remainder. In this supplied cycle, southern winter has the smaller daylight proportion and summer the larger. Different latitudes have different patterns; these example records are not a local forecast or exact values for every place.

Represent seasonal knowledge through an authorised partnership

A proposed seasonal-calendar project should begin with the relevant local First Nations community and knowledge holders, shared purposes and appropriate permission. Collaborators decide which observations and relationships may be recorded, what categories and digital representation are useful, who checks the meaning and what may be shared. A local calendar need not copy four equal seasons or another community's categories. Respect restrictions, attribution and knowledge-holder review; public access and a citation are not blanket permission. This lesson supplies no community calendar, seasonal indicators or claimed consultation.

Common mistakes to watch for
  • Describing what is seen without connecting it to a question, pattern or prediction.
  • Using everyday words only and missing simple scientific vocabulary.
  • Changing more than one thing in an investigation, which makes the comparison unfair.
