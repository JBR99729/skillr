Forces, Motion, Gravity and Simple Machines

Use force-arrow diagrams and numerical models to explain how balanced and unbalanced forces, mass and gravity affect motion, then apply the ideas to levers, pulleys, orbits and First Nations engineering.

LearnPracticeTest

What you need to know

A force is a push or pull caused by an interaction. Forces have both magnitude, measured in newtons (N), and direction. The combined effect of all forces on one object is its net force.

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

  • identify forces acting on a chosen object and draw correctly scaled force arrows
  • calculate one-dimensional net force and determine whether forces are balanced
  • explain that balanced forces mean zero acceleration, not necessarily zero motion
  • relate a change in motion to force magnitude, force direction and object mass
  • distinguish mass from weight and calculate weight using W = mg
  • explain how ideal levers and pulleys trade force for distance
  • describe gravity as the inward force responsible for orbital motion
  • analyse forces in boomerangs, spearthrowers and bows with respectful, place-aware First Nations context

1. Representing forces with arrows — E3

A force diagram is a model of the external forces acting on one chosen object. Each arrow should:

  • start on the object;
  • point in the direction the force acts;
  • use relative length to represent magnitude;
  • include a label such as weight, support, friction, tension or applied force.

Friction opposes relative sliding, or the tendency to slide, between surfaces. A support force acts perpendicular to a contact surface. Weight acts toward Earth's centre.

Do not cancel action–reaction pairs on one diagram. Newton's third-law pairs act on different objects. A force diagram combines forces only when they act on the same chosen object.

2. Balanced and unbalanced forces — E1 & E2

For forces along one line, choose one direction as positive, give opposite forces negative signs and add them.

Net force = sum of all force vectors acting on the object

Balanced forces

When the net force is zero, acceleration is zero. An object at rest remains at rest, while a moving object continues at constant velocity unless another unbalanced force acts.

Example: 15 N forward and 15 N backward give 0 N net force.

Unbalanced forces

When the net force is not zero, the object's velocity changes. It may start, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.

Example: 45 N right and 30 N left give 15 N right.

3. Force, mass and acceleration — E1

For the same object, a larger net force produces a larger acceleration. For the same net force, an object with greater mass accelerates less because it has greater inertia.

Useful numerical model: a = Fnet ÷ m

This equation is a compact way to represent the curriculum relationship. Real investigations should control friction, use the same surface and measure both force and motion consistently.

4. Measuring force with a force meter — E3

A force meter contains a spring that stretches by an amount related to the force. Before measuring:

  1. select a suitable newton range;
  2. check and adjust the zero;
  3. pull in line with the meter;
  4. read the scale at eye level;
  5. record the value in newtons and repeat measurements.

A force meter can measure tension, a pulling force or an object's weight when the object hangs at rest. It measures force, not mass.

5. Gravity, mass and weight — E4

Earth's gravitational force attracts objects toward Earth's centre. Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is measured in kilograms. Weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass and is measured in newtons.

Weight W = mass m × gravitational field strength g

Near Earth's surface, use g ≈ 9.8 N/kg, or approximately 10 N/kg for a quick estimate. A greater mass has a greater weight in the same gravitational field. Ignoring air resistance, mass does not make one freely falling object accelerate faster than another.

Mass

Measured in kilograms; stays the same when the object moves to another planet or moon.

Weight

Measured in newtons; changes when gravitational field strength changes.

6. Levers and pulleys — E5

Simple machines do not create energy. In an ideal machine, they reduce the input force by increasing the distance over which the effort acts.

Lever

A lever turns around a pivot or fulcrum. Increasing the effort arm can reduce the effort force needed for the same turning effect.

effort force × effort arm = load force × load arm

Pulley

A fixed pulley can change force direction. A moving pulley can share the load across supporting rope sections. If two ideal rope sections support a 100 N load, each carries about 50 N.

7. Gravity and orbits — E6

Gravity acts across space. It keeps moons in orbit around planets, planets around stars and stars moving within galaxies. An orbiting object has forward velocity while gravity continuously accelerates it inward, changing the direction of its motion. The object is in continuous free fall around the body it orbits.

Black holes are not stronger simply because they are black. Their extreme nearby gravitational effects arise because a large mass is concentrated into a very compact region, allowing objects to approach much closer to the centre of mass.

8. First Nations engineering: boomerangs, spearthrowers and bows — E7 & E8

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander technologies are diverse and belong to particular Peoples, Countries/Places and cultural traditions. Specific designs, names, uses and knowledge should be attributed accurately rather than treated as one uniform national technology.

Boomerangs

First Peoples developed many boomerang forms for different purposes; not all are designed to return. In a returning boomerang, airfoil-shaped arms and spin produce lift. The advancing arm moves faster relative to the air than the retreating arm, creating unequal lift and a turning effect. The spinning object responds by gradually changing its flight orientation, helping produce a curved path. Gravity and drag also act throughout the flight.

Spearthrowers and bows

A spearthrower extends the effective throwing arm, allowing force to act through a greater distance and helping the spear reach a greater launch speed. Bow-and-arrow technologies used by Torres Strait Islander Peoples store elastic potential energy in the drawn bow and transfer it to the arrow when released.

5 worked numerical and application examples

Each example follows the same scientific sequence: identify the object and forces, represent the situation, calculate or reason, interpret the result and check it independently.

Example 1 — Net force

Problem: A box is pushed 45 N right while friction acts 30 N left.

  1. Choose right as positive.
  2. Write the forces as +45 N and −30 N.
  3. Calculate Fnet = 45 − 30 = 15 N.
  4. Interpret the direction from the positive sign.

Answer: 15 N to the right, so the box accelerates right.

Check: the larger arrow is rightward and exceeds the left force by 15 N.

Example 2 — Same force, different mass

Problem: A net force of 20 N acts on a 2 kg cart and a 10 kg cart.

  1. Use a = Fnet/m.
  2. Light cart: a = 20/2 = 10 m/s².
  3. Heavy cart: a = 20/10 = 2 m/s².
  4. Compare the motion changes.

Answer: the 2 kg cart accelerates five times as much.

Check: both calculations give F = ma = 20 N.

Example 3 — Weight and support

Problem: Find the weight of a 7 kg bag on Earth.

  1. Use W = mg.
  2. Substitute m = 7 kg and g = 9.8 N/kg.
  3. Calculate W = 7 × 9.8 = 68.6 N.
  4. State direction: toward Earth's centre.

Answer: the bag's weight is 68.6 N downward.

Check: estimate with 10 N/kg gives about 70 N. If the bag hangs at rest, tension is 68.6 N upward.

Example 4 — Ideal lever

Problem: A 60 N load is 0.4 m from the pivot. The effort acts 1.2 m from the pivot.

  1. Set effort × 1.2 = 60 × 0.4.
  2. Calculate the load turning effect: 24 N·m.
  3. Effort = 24/1.2 = 20 N.
  4. Interpret the trade-off.

Answer: an ideal 20 N effort balances the 60 N load.

Check: 20 × 1.2 and 60 × 0.4 both equal 24 N·m.

Example 5 — Ideal moving pulley

Problem: Two rope sections support a 100 N load.

  1. Assume an ideal rope and pulley, so both sections have equal tension.
  2. Share 100 N across two supporting sections.
  3. Calculate tension = 100/2 = 50 N.
  4. Recognise the distance trade-off.

Answer: the required ideal pull is 50 N.

Check: 50 N + 50 N = 100 N upward. To lift the load 1 m, about 2 m of rope must be pulled.

10 important problems to solve

Attempt each problem before opening its answer. These move from core force diagrams to numerical and unfamiliar applications.

  1. Two teams pull a rope with 120 N in opposite directions. Calculate the net force and explain what happens if the rope is initially stationary.

    Check answer

    Answer: 120 N − 120 N = 0 N. The forces are balanced, so there is no acceleration; an initially stationary rope remains stationary.

  2. An 11 kg crate is pulled 40 N right while friction is 18 N left. Find the net force and, using the simplified model, its acceleration.

    Check answer

    Answer: Fnet = 22 N right. a = 22/11 = 2 m/s² right.

  3. A cyclist travels at constant velocity. The driving force is 160 N forward. What total resistive force acts, and why?

    Check answer

    Answer: 160 N backward. Constant velocity means zero acceleration and therefore zero net force.

  4. A 5 kg box rests on a horizontal table. It is pushed 12 N right and experiences 4 N friction left. Describe all four main force arrows and calculate horizontal net force.

    Check answer

    Answer: weight ≈49 N down, support ≈49 N up, applied 12 N right and friction 4 N left. Vertical net force is 0 N; horizontal net force is 8 N right.

  5. Two carts of 2 kg and 10 kg each experience the same 20 N net force. Compare their accelerations and explain the role of mass.

    Check answer

    Answer: accelerations are 10 m/s² and 2 m/s². The heavier cart has greater inertia and therefore less acceleration under the same net force.

  6. A force meter holding a bag reads 35 N. Estimate the bag's mass using g = 9.8 N/kg.

    Check answer

    Answer: m = W/g = 35/9.8 ≈ 3.57 kg, about 3.6 kg. Using g≈10 N/kg gives the reasonableness estimate 3.5 kg.

  7. A lever has a 0.3 m load arm and 0.9 m effort arm. It lifts a 180 N load. Calculate the ideal effort force.

    Check answer

    Answer: effort ×0.9 = 180×0.3 =54 N·m, so effort =54/0.9 =60 N.

  8. An ideal moving pulley has two rope sections supporting a 240 N crate. Calculate the pull required and explain the distance trade-off.

    Check answer

    Answer: 240/2 =120 N. The pull is halved, but approximately twice as much rope must be pulled as the load rises.

  9. A satellite has forward velocity while gravity acts toward Earth. Explain why it follows a curved orbit rather than moving in a straight line.

    Check answer

    Answer: gravity continuously accelerates the satellite inward, changing the direction of its velocity. Its forward motion carries it around Earth while it continually falls toward Earth.

  10. A returning boomerang's advancing arm experiences greater lift than its retreating arm. Explain how this can help produce a curved path, and name two other forces acting.

    Check answer

    Answer: unequal lift creates a turning effect on the spinning boomerang; its gyroscopic response changes the orientation of the spinning plane and curves the path. Gravity and drag also act. The outcome depends on design, spin, release and wind.

Common misconceptions

  • “Balanced forces mean an object is not moving.” They mean zero acceleration; constant-velocity motion is possible.
  • “A moving object must have a forward net force.” A forward net force is needed to accelerate forward, not to maintain constant velocity.
  • “Gravity disappears when an object is supported.” Gravity remains; the support force can balance it.
  • “Mass and weight are the same.” Mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a force in newtons.
  • “A longer force arrow means the object moves farther.” Arrow length represents force magnitude, not travel distance.
  • “Action and reaction cancel on one object.” The paired forces act on different objects.
  • “A pulley creates energy.” It trades force for distance and real systems also lose energy through friction.
  • “All boomerangs are returning boomerangs.” First Peoples developed diverse forms for different purposes.
  • “Gravity in orbit is absent.” Gravity is the inward force producing the orbit.
  • “A black hole pulls more strongly than the same mass from every distance.” At the same distance from the centre, gravitational effect depends on mass; compactness allows much closer approach.

Apply and transfer

  1. Design an investigation comparing the acceleration of two carts of different mass under the same measured force. State controlled variables and repeated measurements.
  2. A lift is moving upward but slowing down. Draw a force diagram and explain which vertical force must be larger.
  3. Evaluate a force diagram in which the weight arrow is drawn upward because the ball is moving upward.
  4. Explain how a spearthrower can increase launch speed without creating energy.
  5. State one useful feature and one limitation of the simplified boomerang-force diagram on this page.
