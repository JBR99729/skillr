Year 7 Science · AC9S7H01

How scientific knowledge changes

Evidence, models and changing perspectives
Learning intentionStart here

Explain how reliable new evidence or a different perspective can lead scientists to refine, revise or replace scientific knowledge.

Success criteria

  • I can distinguish evidence from interpretation.
  • I can explain why one surprising result is not enough to overturn a well-supported explanation.
  • I can trace: earlier explanation → new evidence/perspective → checking → revised explanation.
  • I can explain why revision strengthens science rather than making it unreliable.
Explicit teaching sequenceI do
  1. Start with an accepted explanation that fits the evidence available at the time.
  2. Introduce new evidence, improved technology or a different perspective that reveals a gap.
  3. Ask whether the new result is reliable, repeatable and consistent with other evidence.
  4. Compare old and new explanations for explanatory power and prediction.
  5. Revise knowledge only as far as the evidence supports.

Key distinction: scientific knowledge is provisional but not arbitrary. Change must be justified by evidence and reasoning.

Modelled exampleThink aloud

Classification changes

Earlier classifications may group organisms mainly by visible similarities. Later DNA, fossil and anatomical evidence can reveal different relationships.

Reasoning: the earlier evidence was not useless; it was incomplete. Scientists compare all available evidence and revise the grouping if the new classification explains the relationships better.

Teacher prompt: “What evidence changed, and what exact part of the explanation changed because of it?”

Guided questioningWe do
  • Why should a blurry photograph not instantly change a species classification?
  • How can a better telescope change knowledge without changing the object being observed?
  • What makes peer review and replication useful when a claim is surprising?
  • How can a different perspective generate a useful question while evidence still decides whether the explanation is supported?
Misconception checkStop and fix
  • “Science changes, so it is unreliable.” Correction: evidence-based revision is a strength.
  • “One new result overturns a theory.” Correction: results must be checked against methods and wider evidence.
  • “Old evidence becomes useless.” Correction: old observations can remain valid while interpretation changes.
  • “Different perspectives mean all explanations are equally scientific.” Correction: claims still need evidence.
Quick understanding checkShow me
  1. Define evidence and interpretation in one sentence each.
  2. Why might a model be refined rather than completely discarded?
  3. What should scientists do with one result that conflicts with a strong body of evidence?
  4. Complete: “Scientific knowledge becomes stronger when…”
Independent releaseYou do

Give students a short unfamiliar case: an older explanation, a new observation made with improved technology and two possible revised models. Students identify the evidence, choose the better-supported revision and justify why it is a refinement of scientific knowledge rather than a change based on preference.
