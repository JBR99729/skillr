Classifying living things

Classification helps scientists organise the diversity of life so organisms can be identified, compared and communicated clearly.

Students should leave able to use features, groups and keys — not just recite vocabulary.

🌿
Observe featuresGroup by evidenceUse a keyExplain the reason

Why classify?

  • Classification makes biodiversity easier to study and communicate.
  • Groups are based on shared features and relationships.
  • Nested groups move from broad similarities to more specific similarities.
  • A good classification can be checked against evidence.

Using a dichotomous key

A dichotomous key uses paired choices. Each answer sends you to the next choice or to an identification.

1a. Has feathers → bird
1b. No feathers → go to 2
2a. Has fur → mammal
2b. No fur → reptile or amphibian check
Teacher prompt: Which feature must be observable, clear and not based on opinion?

First Nations perspectives

  • First Nations Australians have diverse systems for knowing and grouping living things.
  • Classifications may connect organisms with Country/Place, season, behaviour, habitat, use and relationships.
  • These systems should be treated respectfully and locally, not as one single uniform system.
Compare: Western classification often foregrounds structure and ancestry; cultural/ecological systems may foreground relationships and use.

Classification can change

  • New observations can reveal features people could not see before.
  • Microscopy changed how organisms and cells were compared.
  • Modern genetic evidence can refine relationships between organisms.
  • Changing a classification is a strength of science when evidence improves.
