Connect a method weakness to its consequence

Learning intention: Examine assumptions, errors, conflicting findings and unanswered questions before judging a conclusion.

WORKED EXAMPLE

“Warmer means five centimetres taller” goes beyond the evidence

Report A’s warm group ends 5 cm taller than its cool group, but the warm group also receives brighter light. The method cannot isolate temperature as the only explanation. Report B, with light matched, shows a 1 cm difference instead. Those findings challenge a universal “5 cm” claim; they do not require one group to have invented its data. Compare methods, samples and conditions, then propose a test that separates the competing explanations.

Both reports: six separate seedlings per group, same variety and starting mean height 5 cm, 7 days and 10 mL water per seedling daily. Assume other stated conditions match within each report.

Learn the skill

1. Identify the problem and its effect

A useful evaluation names a specific weakness, explains what it could change and proposes a matching repair. “Human error” gives no such explanation. An assumption should be checked or stated as a limit.

Ruler example The stem begins at the 1 cm ruler mark and ends at 15 cm. Its length is 15 − 1 = 14 cm. Recording 15 cm overestimates it by 1 cm.

Try it · Would ten repeats using the same wrong endpoint-only method remove that 1 cm overestimate?

No. The same method can preserve the same offset. Align zero correctly or subtract the starting reading, then remeasure as appropriate.

2. Match the conclusion to the method

Independent seedlings help examine biological variation, but they do not remove a shared confound. The relevant outcome is also important: final height and growth from baseline are different quantities.

Try it · How much did Report A’s warm group grow on average, and can temperature alone explain it?

Its mean height rose from 5 to 14 cm, a growth of 9 cm. Temperature alone cannot be isolated because light also differs between groups.

3. Use disagreement to ask a better question

Different findings can arise from methods, contexts, sampling or an incomplete model. Compare evidence quality before choosing a favourite. Identify a follow-up that could discriminate between explanations.

A discriminating follow-up Compare warm and cool groups under the same measured light conditions, with comparable starting seedlings, consistent water, repeated independent samples and a defined measurement method.

Try it · Why is “add more seedlings but keep warm/bright and cool/dim” an incomplete repair?

A larger sample can improve information about variability, but temperature and light still change together. The confound remains.

Common mistakes

“A supported hypothesis is finally proven.” The evidence supports a conclusion within its method, range and uncertainty; further questions and alternatives may remain.

“Conflicting results mean both studies are useless.” First compare what was actually measured and how. Differences can expose a limitation and guide a stronger test.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Repair a conclusion and its method

Rewrite “warming always makes seedlings 5 cm taller” using the two fictional reports. Identify one confound, its possible effect and a specific improvement. Add a follow-up question that could distinguish temperature from light.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “problem → possible effect → improvement”. Extend: explain the separate value of more independent samples, better controls and a checked measuring instrument.

This is a paper evaluation. Any plant study needs adult-approved conditions and care; do not harm living things to create an extreme result.

Three tricky questions

1. Do six seedlings in each group automatically control the light conditions?

No. Sample size and controlling a confounding variable solve different problems.

2. Why can repeated readings be close together yet inaccurate?

A shared offset or unsuitable setup can shift every reading similarly. Small spread does not by itself establish closeness to a reference value.

3. Does Report B’s 1 cm difference establish that Report A’s 5 cm difference was an arithmetic error?

No. Their light conditions differ and other contextual variation can matter. Check records and methods before assigning a cause to the disagreement.

FAQ

Is random variation the same as a data-entry blunder?

No. Check an apparent transcription error against the raw record; do not automatically classify every mistake as natural random variation.

Must every unanswered question invalidate current results?

No. A bounded result can be useful while leaving other causes, conditions or applications unresolved.

Key concept

Strong scientific evaluation follows the evidence backwards: claim → evidence → data processing → method. At each step, ask what was assumed, what could have introduced error, whether the data are consistent, and whether the conclusion goes beyond what was actually tested.

Exam structure: limitation → effect on evidence → specific improvement.

Topic guide

1. Data quality and unanswered questions (E1)

Useful indicators include consistency across repeated trials, spread, outliers, relevance to the aim, sufficient repeats and whether measurements directly answer the investigation question. A gap in these indicators often reveals an unanswered question.

2. Evaluating methods and assumptions (E2)

An assumption is something accepted as true without being directly tested—for example, that temperature stayed constant or a balance remained calibrated. Identify variables that should have been controlled, then explain how the method could be improved and why.

3. Conclusions, claims and premises (E3)

A conclusion should be supported by evidence, answer the aim and stay within the scope of the sample and method. Watch for premises taken for granted, such as assuming correlation proves causation or assuming all trials were fair without evidence.

4. Spread of repeated measurements (E4)

Spread describes how far repeated measurements are separated. A simple measure is range = maximum − minimum. Small spread supports high precision/consistency; it does not automatically prove accuracy.

5. Sources of error and improvements (E5)

Random variation changes unpredictably between trials, such as slight reaction-time differences. Repeats can reduce its influence. Systematic error shifts results consistently, such as a miscalibrated instrument; it must be corrected at the source.

6. Conflicting evidence (E6)

When evidence disagrees, compare sample size, controls, spread, relevance, method quality, possible bias and whether the studies tested the same conditions. Conflicting evidence should be evaluated, not ignored.

10 Important Questions & Answers
  1. What makes data high quality? Consistent, relevant repeated measurements with manageable spread, investigated outliers and a method that directly addresses the aim.
  2. Does small spread prove accuracy? No. It supports precision; measurements can be tightly clustered yet systematically wrong.
  3. What is an assumption? A condition treated as true without direct evidence or testing.
  4. Why do uncontrolled variables matter? They can provide another explanation for changes in the dependent variable, weakening validity.
  5. How do repeats help? They reveal variation and reduce the influence of random error when results are summarised appropriately.
  6. Can repeats remove systematic error? No. Calibration, replacement or method correction is needed.
  7. What makes a conclusion strong? It is supported by the data, matches the aim, stays within the evidence and acknowledges limitations.
  8. What should you do with an outlier? Investigate why it occurred and, where possible, repeat the measurement before deciding how to treat it.
  9. How should conflicting evidence be handled? Compare evidence quality and context rather than choosing the result you prefer.
  10. What is a good unanswered question? A follow-up question that targets a genuine gap in what the original investigation tested.
Common mistakes
  • Saying “the data are accurate” only because repeated values are close together.
  • Listing a limitation without explaining its effect on the conclusion.
  • Saying “more trials” fixes every problem, including systematic bias.
  • Deleting outliers automatically.
  • Accepting a conclusion simply because it matches the hypothesis.
  • Ignoring conflicting evidence.
Revision Notes

Data Quality

  • Check repeated-trial consistency.
  • Calculate or compare spread.
  • Identify and investigate outliers.
  • Ask whether the data actually answer the aim.

Method Quality

  • Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • Find assumptions and uncontrolled variables.
  • Check whether the steps are reproducible and measurements appropriate.
  • Suggest specific improvements with reasons.

Error

  • Random variation: unpredictable trial-to-trial differences; use repeats and standardised technique.
  • Systematic error: consistent bias; calibrate, replace or redesign.

Claims

  • Trace each claim to supporting evidence.
  • Check whether premises are supported and relevant.
  • Do not treat correlation as causation without an appropriate design.
  • Match the scope of the conclusion to the sample and conditions.

Conflicting Evidence

  • Compare sample size, controls, spread, bias, methods and relevance.
  • Acknowledge disagreement and explain why one evidence set may be stronger.
