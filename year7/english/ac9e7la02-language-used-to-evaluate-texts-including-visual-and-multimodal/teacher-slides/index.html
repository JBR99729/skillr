1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Students learn to recognise language used to evaluate texts including visual and multimodal texts, and how evaluations of a text can be substantiated by reference to the text and other sources. They identify precise evidence and explain or evaluate how a language, structural, literary or multimodal choice shapes meaning, representation, audience response or purpose. Key concepts include language, visual, multimodal, character, evidence.

Learning target: recognise language used to evaluate texts including visual and multimodal texts, and how evaluations of a text can be substantiated by reference to the text and other sources

2. Subject-Specific Content & Key Examples

Opinion plus reason

Move from “I liked the story” to “I found the ending effective because the repeated warning built suspense.” The second response gives a judgement and evidence.

Strength of judgement

Compare “might help”, “should help” and “will help”. The modal choice changes how certain or forceful the claim sounds.

Subjective and objective wording

“The brilliant new playground is exciting” evaluates; “the playground opened on Monday” reports a checkable fact. Discuss why a writer may choose either.

Transfer

Students underline one evaluative word in a fresh sentence, replace it with a weaker or stronger choice and explain the changed effect.

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: building knowledge about words of evaluation, including words to express emotional responses to texts, judgement of characters and their actions, and appreciation of the aesthetic qualities of text
  • E2: analysing how evaluative language can be used to assess the qualities of a narrative or persuasive text; for example, its impact on the reader or the author’s skill in the use of language
  • E3: recognising how evaluative language is used to critically assess the validity of evidence and the reliability of sources

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Feature spotting: Naming a camera angle, colour or sound is not analysis; explain its effect.
  • One fixed effect: Interpret the feature in context and alongside other modes.
  • Separate-mode lists: Explain how image, words and sound reinforce, contradict or qualify one another.
  • Retelling: Analyse how the representation was constructed, not only what it shows.
