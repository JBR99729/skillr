Repeated Chance Experiments and Simulations

Run repeated physical and digital chance experiments, compare observed relative frequencies with probability predictions, and explain what changes as the number of trials increases.

LearnHomeworkPracticeTest

What students need to master

This topic connects theoretical probability with experimental evidence. Students run many trials, calculate relative frequencies, compare observed and predicted results, and explain why random variation remains even when a probability model is sound.

Digital simulations can generate hundreds or thousands of trials quickly, but they are only as valid as their programmed probability model and recording process.

1. Repeated chance experiments

For each experiment record total trials, event count, relative frequency and model probability.

Relative frequency = observed event count ÷ total trials. Example: 22 heads in 50 tosses gives 22/50=0.44.

2. Predicted vs observed frequency

Expected frequency = probability × number of trials. A fair die rolled 120 times has expected sixes 120×1/6=20. If 18 occur, that is 2 below expected, but it does not by itself show the die is unfair.

Expected frequency is a long-run prediction, not an exact quota.

3. Large numbers of trials — E1

With repeated independent trials under the same model, relative frequency tends to become more stable around the underlying probability as trial count grows. This is the Year 7 idea behind the law of large numbers.

It does not mean every larger sample must be closer than every smaller sample or that the proportion improves after every additional trial. Random fluctuations continue.

4. Digital simulations — E2

A sound simulation should represent the intended sample space and probabilities, use an appropriate random process, record every valid outcome and produce counts that total the requested trials.

Model check: if a fair-coin program is accidentally set to P(heads)=0.6, more trials will stabilise near 0.6. More trials reveal the programmed model; they do not repair a wrong one.

5. Comparing experiments fairly

When trial totals differ, compare relative frequencies rather than raw counts. For example, 24 heads in 50 tosses is 0.48 while 241 heads in 500 is 0.482.

A larger sample generally provides a more stable estimate, but randomisation, recording and model validity also matter.

6. Physical vs digital experiment

Suppose 300 real coin tosses give 139 heads: RF≈0.4633. A 5000-trial simulation gives 2488 heads: RF=0.4976. The simulation is closer to 0.5 in this example, but it also used far more trials. This does not prove digital experiments are automatically more accurate.

7. First Nations Australian instructive games — E3

When investigating chance through a specific First Nations Australian instructive game, use reliable community or source information for its rules, attribution and cultural context before assigning probabilities.

Once the chance mechanism is accurately established, students can compare expected and observed outcomes across repeated play. Keep mathematical claims tied to the stated game mechanism; broader cultural claims require source support.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting observed and predicted frequency to match exactly.
  • Saying large trials remove randomness.
  • Comparing raw counts across different sample sizes.
  • Assuming every larger sample must be closer to theory.
  • Trusting simulation output without checking model/settings.
  • Editing outcomes to make them match expectation.
