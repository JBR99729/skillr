AC9M7M06 • Year 7 Maths

Mathematical Modelling with Ratios

Use ratios to model practical situations, connect part–part and part–whole representations, choose useful diagrams or equations, solve the model, and explain what the answer means in context.

What you need to know first

Before modelling with ratios, students should be comfortable simplifying ratios, multiplying and dividing whole numbers and decimals, working with fractions, and using units such as grams, kilograms, millilitres and litres.

  • Ratio: a comparison between quantities.
  • Equivalent ratios: ratios made by multiplying or dividing every term by the same non-zero factor.
  • Part–part: compares one component with another, such as red:blue = 3:2.
  • Part–whole: compares one component with the total. For 3:2, the first part is 3/(3+2) = 3/5 of the whole.
1. Ratio modelling using fractions — E1

A ratio such as 3:4 does not mean the first part is 3/4 of the whole. It means there are 3 parts of the first quantity and 4 parts of the second, so there are 7 total parts.

Worked example: boys:girls = 3:4. Boys are 3/7 of the group and girls are 4/7.

Key method: add the ratio terms to find the whole, then write the required part over that total.

Another example: paint red:blue = 2:5. Total parts = 7. Red fraction = 2/7; blue fraction = 5/7.

Common trap: confusing a part–part ratio with a part–whole fraction.

2. Solving practical ratio models — E2

Practical ratio problems usually follow the same modelling cycle:

  1. Identify the quantities and write the ratio in the correct order.
  2. Find the total number of ratio parts.
  3. Use the known total or known component to find the value of one part.
  4. Scale each ratio term.
  5. Check the units and whether the answer makes sense in the situation.
  6. Write a sentence interpreting the result.
Concrete example: cement:sand:gravel = 1:2:3 and total mass = 24 kg. Total parts = 6, so one part = 4 kg. Cement = 4 kg, sand = 8 kg, gravel = 12 kg.
Food example: oil:vinegar = 3:1 and total volume = 200 mL. Four parts make 200 mL, so one part = 50 mL. Oil = 150 mL and vinegar = 50 mL.
Reverse example: sugar:flour = 2:3 and flour = 600 g. Three parts equal 600 g, so one part = 200 g and sugar = 400 g.

Other useful contexts include scale and design, textiles, paint mixtures, recipes and construction materials.

3. Choosing a representation — E3

A strong mathematical model does more than calculate. It uses a representation that makes the structure of the problem clear.

  • Counters or blocks: useful for introducing ratios concretely.
  • Bar models: show equal-sized parts and make part–whole reasoning visible.
  • Ratio tables: show equivalent ratios and scale factors efficiently.
  • Equations: useful for multi-step problems. For a 3:4 ratio totalling 56, write 3x + 4x = 56.
Colour model: red:blue = 3:1 can be represented by 3 red counters and 1 blue counter. Red is 3/4 of the whole model.

The best representation is the one that helps explain the relationship, not simply the one that looks most complicated.

4. Interpreting and communicating the solution

AC9M7M06 is a modelling code, so the final step matters. A correct numerical answer is not enough if it is not connected back to the situation.

For example, instead of writing only x = 8, write: “Use 8 kg of sand so the mixture keeps the required ratio.”

Students should also check practical constraints. A model may give 2.4 kg, but if material is sold only in whole-kilogram bags, the practical decision may be to buy 3 kg and explain why.

5. First Nations Australians’ knowledges — E4

The Australian Curriculum elaboration asks students to investigate commercialised substances founded on First Nations Australians’ knowledges and understand how ratios are used in their development.

For classroom quality, use verified case studies and acknowledge the knowledge source. Do not invent a “traditional recipe”, assume all communities use the same practices, or imply that a mathematical ratio alone establishes safety or authenticity. The mathematics focus is how consistent proportions can be represented and scaled in a documented formulation while respecting attribution, cultural protocols and real-world quality requirements.

Worked problems

Example A — total given

A dye uses blue:red = 5:2 and totals 210 mL.

Total parts = 7; one part = 210 ÷ 7 = 30 mL. Blue = 150 mL; red = 60 mL.

Example B — one component given

Gravel:sand = 7:3 and sand = 12 kg.

Three parts = 12 kg, so one part = 4 kg. Gravel = 7 × 4 = 28 kg.

Example C — fraction from ratio

A:B = 4:1. B is 1/(4+1) = 1/5 of the whole.

Example D — modelling equation

A:B = 3:4 and total = 56. Let one ratio part be x. Then 3x + 4x = 56, so 7x = 56 and x = 8. Therefore A = 24 and B = 32.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Treating 3:4 as 3/4 of the whole instead of 3/7.
  • Changing only one term when scaling a ratio.
  • Reversing the ratio order asked in the question.
  • Using the total as the value of one part.
  • Dropping units in practical problems.
  • Giving a calculator result without interpreting it in context.
  • Choosing a diagram that does not match the quantities in the model.
  • Ignoring practical constraints such as packaging, measurement precision or instructions.
15 important questions to solve
  1. In 3:5, how many total parts are there?
    Answer

    8.

  2. In 3:5, what fraction of the whole is the first part?
    Answer

    3/8.

  3. Simplify 12:18.
    Answer

    2:3.

  4. Which ratio is equivalent to 4:6: 8:12 or 8:10?
    Answer

    8:12.

  5. A 1:4 drink mix totals 500 mL. How much is the first ingredient?
    Answer

    100 mL.

  6. Oil:vinegar = 3:1 and vinegar = 40 mL. How much oil?
    Answer

    120 mL.

  7. Concrete 1:2:3 totals 48 kg. How much gravel?
    Answer

    24 kg.

  8. A bar model has 6 blocks for A and 2 for B. Simplify A:B.
    Answer

    3:1.

  9. Six red and nine blue counters are used. What fraction are red?
    Answer

    6/15 = 2/5.

  10. A 2:3 ratio totals 25 L. Find the first quantity.
    Answer

    10 L.

  11. A ratio 5:3 is scaled until the first term is 45. Find the second quantity.
    Answer

    27.

  12. Write an equation for a 3:4 ratio with total 56.
    Answer

    3x + 4x = 56.

  13. A model gives 2.4 kg but bags are sold only in whole kilograms. What is the practical purchase?
    Answer

    3 kg, with the constraint explained.

  14. Why is 3:7 not the same as saying the first part is 3/7 of the whole?
    Answer

    Because the whole has 3 + 7 = 10 parts, so the first part is 3/10.

  15. Why must a modelling answer include a contextual conclusion?
    Answer

    Because the numerical result must be interpreted as a decision or quantity in the real situation.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations
  • Content description: use mathematical modelling to solve practical problems involving ratios; formulate problems, interpret and communicate solutions in terms of the situation, justifying choices made about the representation.
  • E1: use fractions to model ratio problems, including part–part and part–whole relations.
  • E2: model practical ratios of length, capacity or mass in contexts such as construction, design, food or textile production.
  • E3: use manipulatives, diagrams and mathematical discussion, including colour-mixing models.
  • E4: investigate commercialised substances founded on First Nations Australians’ knowledges and understand how ratios are used in their development.
