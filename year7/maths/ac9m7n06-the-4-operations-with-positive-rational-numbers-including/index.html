What students learn in AC9M7N06

We are learning to use the 4 operations with positive rational numbers including fractions, decimals and percentages to solve problems using efficient calculation strategies.

Positive rational numbers include positive fractions, decimals and percentages. Choosing an equivalent form can make one of the four operations more efficient without changing the value.

Addition and subtraction of fractions require common-sized parts, while multiplication and division can often be interpreted as scaling or sharing. Percentage problems require a clearly identified base quantity.

Multi-step calculations follow operation order and must be interpreted in context. An estimate, inverse operation or equivalent representation provides an independent reasonableness check.

  • I can apply the four operations to positive rational numbers accurately.

4 worked numerical & application examples

Follow each example from representation and setup through calculation/reasoning, interpretation and an independent check.

Example 1

Combine a percentage and fraction

  1. Find 25% of 64 as 1/4×64=16.
  2. Find 3/8 of 64 as 24.
  3. Add the two parts: 16+24=40.
  4. Check that 5/8×64=40.

Final answer: 25% of 64 plus 3/8 of 64 equals 40.

Check: The combined fraction is 1/4+3/8=5/8.

Example 2

Apply operation order

  1. Evaluate 3+2.5×4.
  2. Multiply first: 2.5×4=10.
  3. Add 3 to obtain 13.
  4. Check by repeated addition: 3+2.5+2.5+2.5+2.5=13.

Final answer: 3+2.5×4=13.

Check: Multiplication precedes addition.

Example 3

Application problem 1

Problem: A jacket costs $80. It is reduced by 15%, then a $6 delivery fee is added. Find the final cost and explain why adding 15 to 80 would be incorrect.

  1. Plan: Represent the information first, then calculate, interpret and independently check the result.
  2. Work: 15% of $80 is $12, so the discounted price is $68; adding $6 gives $74. A percentage is a proportion of the original amount, not a raw dollar amount.
  3. Interpret: This synthesis problem combines the chapter's core representation, calculation and reasoning skills.

Final answer: 15% of $80 is $12, so the discounted price is $68; adding $6 gives $74. A percentage is a proportion of the original amount, not a raw dollar amount.

Check: This synthesis problem combines the chapter's core representation, calculation and reasoning skills.

Example 4

Application problem 2

Problem: A 2.5 L drink is shared equally among 8 people after 10% spills. How much each?

  1. Plan: Find 90% of 2.5 L, then divide.
  2. Work: 2.25 L remains; 2.25÷8=0.28125 L, or 281.25 mL each.
  3. Interpret: Apply the percentage loss before division.

Final answer: 2.25 L remains; 2.25÷8=0.28125 L, or 281.25 mL each.

Check: Apply the percentage loss before division.

Common misconceptions

Common mistake: Fractions added by adding denominators.

Correction: Rename with common-sized parts.

Common mistake: Decimal division leaves divisor unchanged.

Correction: Scale both dividend and divisor equally.

Common mistake: Percentage change confused with percentage points.

Correction: Compare rates carefully.

Readiness check before AC9M7N06

Students are more likely to succeed with AC9M7N06 when these prerequisite ideas are secure. This is a teacher/parent planning check, not a formal assessment.

  • Recognise equivalent fractions and simplify common fractions.
  • Find common denominators for addition and subtraction.
  • Use decimal place value accurately when multiplying or dividing by powers of 10.
  • Convert familiar benchmark forms such as 1/2 = 0.5 = 50% and 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.
  • Estimate the size of an answer well enough to reject an unreasonable result.

If these foundations are weak, review AC9M7N04 equivalent rational-number representations or AC9M7N05 rounding and estimation before pushing harder AC9M7N06 practice.

Common errors and the shortest repair

Error: 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7.

Diagnosis: Numerators and denominators were added separately.

Repair: Rename both fractions using equal-sized parts before adding.

Error: Flip the first fraction when dividing.

Diagnosis: The reciprocal rule is being recalled without understanding which number is the divisor.

Repair: Ask “how many divisor-sized groups fit?” and multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction.

Error: Treat 25% as 25.

Diagnosis: Percent is being treated as a whole-number amount instead of “per hundred”.

Repair: Rewrite 25% as 25/100 = 1/4 or 0.25 before calculating.

Error: Decimal answer is ten or one hundred times too large.

Diagnosis: Place value was lost during multiplication or division.

Repair: Estimate the result first, then check whether the decimal placement matches the expected size.

Error: Convert every problem to decimals automatically.

Diagnosis: Representation is being changed by habit rather than for efficiency.

Repair: Compare fraction, decimal and percentage forms and choose the one that makes the arithmetic simplest.

Error: Correct calculation but no unit or contextual interpretation.

Diagnosis: The numerical result has not been connected back to the problem.

Repair: State what the answer measures and use an estimate or inverse operation to check it.

Direct answers to common AC9M7N06 questions

Why do fractions need a common denominator for addition and subtraction?

The denominator tells you the size of each part. You can only add or subtract fractions directly when the parts are the same size, so unlike fractions must first be rewritten with a common denominator.

Why does dividing by a fraction use the reciprocal?

Division asks how many divisor-sized groups fit into a quantity. Rewriting the divisor so it becomes 1 shows why multiplying by its reciprocal gives the same result. For example, 3/4 ÷ 1/8 asks how many eighths are in three quarters; 3/4 = 6/8, so the answer is 6.

When is a percentage easier as a fraction?

Use whichever equivalent form makes the calculation shortest. For example, 12.5% = 1/8, so 12.5% of 96 is often faster as 96 ÷ 8 = 12 than as 0.125 × 96.

Why can multiplying by a decimal make a number smaller?

If the multiplier is between 0 and 1, it represents only part of the original quantity. For example, multiplying by 0.25 finds one quarter of the number.

How should students check rational-number calculations?

Estimate first, use an equivalent representation or inverse operation where useful, and check that the final size and unit make sense in context.

Teacher mastery guidance

A student is ready to move on when they can do more than produce answers. They should be able to:

  • choose a sensible fraction, decimal or percentage representation without prompting;
  • apply all four operations accurately to positive rational numbers;
  • explain why a selected method is efficient;
  • identify and repair common errors;
  • estimate or use an inverse/equivalent form to check reasonableness;
  • interpret the answer in the context of the problem.

If a student repeatedly fails one of these areas, reteach that specific misconception before assigning another mixed set.

    Check answer

    Answer: 27.

    Hint: Multiply 90 by 0.3.

    Why: Thirty percent is 0.30 of the quantity.

  4. 4. Evaluate 3+2.5×4.

    Check answer

    Answer: 13.

    Hint: Calculate 2.5×4 first.

    Why: Operation order requires the multiplication to be completed before the addition.

    Hint: Double 3/4.

    Check answer

    Answer: Discount $18; sale price $102.
