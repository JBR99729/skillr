AC9M7N09 • Year 7 Maths • Number

Mathematical modelling with rational numbers and percentages

Formulate a practical problem, choose a useful representation, calculate efficiently, interpret the result in context, and justify the choices and assumptions used.

The modelling cycle

1. Understand

Identify quantities, units, constraints and what must be found.

2. Represent

Choose signed numbers, percentages, fractions, decimals, tables, equations or digital tools.

3. Calculate

Use an efficient strategy and keep units attached.

4. Interpret

Explain what the answer means in the real situation.

5. Evaluate

Check reasonableness, assumptions and limitations.

Core ideas

Signed change model: final = initial + changes

Percentage part = proportion × total

Profit = revenue − cost

Percentage profit/loss = profit or loss ÷ original cost × 100%

E1: model positive and negative quantities such as floors, altitude and depth. E2: model proportions and percentages. E3: model profit/loss, credits/debits and gains/losses. E4: investigate land-use proportions, including Australian First Nations contexts, using appropriate and reliable data.

Cheat sheet

Positive/negative

Choose a zero/reference point first. Direction determines the sign.

Percentage

p% = p/100. Find a part using decimal × total.

Reverse percentage

Whole = known part ÷ decimal proportion.

Profit/loss

Compare revenue and cost; percentage uses cost as the base unless told otherwise.

Reasonableness

Estimate using friendly numbers before accepting a calculator result.

Communication

Finish with a sentence explaining the answer, units, assumptions and limitations.

Important questions

  1. How do you choose a zero/reference point for an integer model?
  2. Why is −240 m useful for representing submarine depth?
  3. How do you calculate 55% of a cohort and interpret the answer?
  4. How do you work backwards when a part and percentage are known?
  5. What is the difference between profit and percentage profit?
  6. How should credits and debits be represented with signed numbers?
  7. Why can percentages be better than raw counts for comparing groups of different sizes?
  8. How can estimation reveal an unreasonable model output?
  9. What assumptions should be stated when using past data to predict future attendance?
  10. How should a model using First Nations land-use data distinguish verified evidence from hypothetical examples?

Worked examples

Attendance: 55% of 400 = 0.55×400 = 220. Interpretation: if the 55% model is appropriate, about 220 students would attend.

Fundraiser: revenue $1050, cost $780 → profit $270. Percentage profit = 270÷780×100% ≈ 34.6%.

Signed movement: submarine −240 + 85 − 120 + 60 − 30 = −245 m, so it finishes 245 m below the reference sea level.

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong sign for a rise, fall, credit or debit.
  • Calculating percentage profit using revenue instead of original cost.
  • Applying a second percentage to the wrong group.
  • Giving a bare number without interpreting it in context.
  • Treating assumptions or illustrative land-use percentages as verified real-world facts.
