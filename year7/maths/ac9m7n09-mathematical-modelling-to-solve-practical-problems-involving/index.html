Mathematical Modelling with Rational Numbers and Percentages

Turn a real situation into mathematics, choose an efficient representation, calculate, check the model and communicate what the result actually means.

Start lessonCheat sheetImportant questionsPracticeTestHomework

The big idea

Modelling is a cycle, not just a calculation

1. FormulateWhat is known? What must be found? What assumptions are needed?
2. RepresentChoose signed numbers, fractions, decimals, percentages, equations, tables or digital tools.
3. CalculateUse an efficient and accurate strategy.
4. ValidateCheck scale, units, constraints and reasonableness.
5. InterpretExplain the result in the original situation.

E1 — Additive situations with positive and negative quantities

Choose a reference level such as ground floor, sea level or a starting balance. Changes above/increase can be positive; changes below/decrease can be negative.

Start −240 m → +85 −120 +60 −30 = −245 m

The negative result means the final position is 245 m below the reference level.

Try before revealing

A lift starts at floor 0, rises 9 floors and descends 14. Model: 0+9−14=−5, so the final floor is −5.

Self-check:

A temperature is 4°C, falls 9°C and rises 3°C. Final temperature?

E2 — Proportion and percentage models

Ask: What is the whole? What proportion is being used? Which form makes the calculation easiest?

School event

55% of 320 = 0.55×320 = 176 students.

Green space

35% of 18,000 m² = 6300 m².

Efficient form

12.5% = 1/8, so 12.5% of 960 is 960÷8 = 120.

Interpretation matters: 176.4 students is not a sensible final count. A model output must be interpreted according to the quantity being modelled.

E3 — Financial modelling: profit, loss, credits and debits

Profit

profit = revenue − cost

Percentage profit

profit ÷ cost × 100%

Signed transactions

Credit +, debit −, then calculate the running balance.

Worked fundraiser — reveal steps

Revenue $1050, costs $780. Profit = $270. Percentage profit = 270÷780×100% ≈ 34.6%. The percentage is useful because it compares performance relative to the amount invested.

Self-check:

An event earns $900 and costs $750. Percentage profit?

E4 — Land-use proportion modelling

The curriculum includes investigation of proportions of Australian First Nations Peoples’ traditional grain belt and Australia’s current grain belt as a teaching context. When real data are used, students should cite and verify the source rather than treating invented classroom percentages as factual claims.

Safe modelling example

For a hypothetical 2.4 million km² region, 18% = 432,000 km² and 27% = 648,000 km². The difference is 9 percentage points, while 27% is 50% larger than 18% relative to the original 18%.

This is also a good place to distinguish percentage-point difference from relative percentage increase.

Choosing representations and digital tools

Signed numbers

Best for direction, gains/losses and balances.

Percentages

Best for comparing proportions of a whole.

Fractions

Best when benchmark forms such as 1/4 or 1/8 simplify a calculation.

Spreadsheet

Best for repeated scenarios, changing assumptions and comparing outputs.

A good modeller should be able to justify why a representation was chosen.

Validate before accepting an answer

  • Is the result within possible bounds?
  • Are the units correct?
  • Does the scale make sense?
  • Does a percentage part exceed the whole when it should not?
  • Did a count produce a fractional person?
  • Would changing an assumption materially change the conclusion?
Example: 35% of an 18,000 m² site cannot be 63,000 m² because the part cannot exceed the whole.

High-yield

Important questions

  1. Why is choosing a reference zero important in signed-number models?
  2. Model 23% of 1200 using two different representations.
  3. Find profit and percentage profit when revenue is $1050 and cost is $780.
  4. Explain the difference between 9 percentage points and a 50% relative increase.
  5. Give one example where a spreadsheet is more useful than a single hand calculation.
  6. Explain why a mathematically correct calculation may still give an invalid model result.
  7. State two assumptions in a simple fundraising model.
  8. Write a complete conclusion that includes result, units, context and limitation.

Extension

Higher-order modelling

  1. A fundraiser has fixed cost $300 and earns $8 per ticket. Build a profit model and determine the minimum whole-number break-even attendance.
  2. Compare two fundraising plans using percentage profit rather than raw dollars and explain why the ranking can change.
  3. Create a two-stage percentage model, such as survey participation followed by yes-vote proportion.
  4. Change one assumption in a land-use or financial model and explain how sensitive the conclusion is to that change.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating before defining what each number represents.
  • Confusing percentage-point difference with percentage increase.
  • Using revenue as the denominator when the task defines percentage profit on cost.
  • Reporting impossible fractional counts without interpretation.
  • Accepting calculator output without checking bounds or scale.
  • Presenting hypothetical percentages as real-world facts without verifying a source.
