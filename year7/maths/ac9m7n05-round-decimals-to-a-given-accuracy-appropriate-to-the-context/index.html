Rounding, Accuracy and Reasonableness

Round decimals accurately, place rational numbers between consecutive integers, choose sensible precision for real situations and use estimation to challenge unreasonable answers.

1. Locate the number before you round

Knowing the interval helps you understand size and prevents sign mistakes.

7 < 7.32 < 8    and    −4 < −3.89 < −3

Self-check

Which consecutive integers contain −8.0004?

2. Round to the requested accuracy

Keep the requested digits, inspect the next digit, then decide whether the final kept digit stays or increases.

12.4879 → 12.488

Try first: round 3.9996 to 2 decimal places

Hint 1

Keep 3.99 and inspect the third decimal digit.

Hint 2

The next digit is 9, so the hundredths digit rounds up.

Reveal answer

4.00. Rounding can carry into the whole-number part.

3. Context decides what “accurate enough” means

A mathematically correct rounded value can still be unsuitable for the situation.

Paint

Need 1.89 L, sold in 1 L tins → buy 2 L. Enough material matters more than ordinary nearest-integer rounding.

Budget

$12,847.32 for early planning → about $12,800 to the nearest $100 may be appropriate.

Tiles

Need 23.78 m², boxes cover 10 m² → purchase 30 m², because 20 m² is insufficient.

Self-check

A school needs seats for 238 people. Chairs come in stacks of 25. What is the minimum number to order?

4. Estimate to check reasonableness

Estimation is not only for getting an approximate answer. It is a powerful error detector.

Calculator check

A calculator displays 398 × 21 = 83,580.

Try an estimate

Round 398 to 400 and 21 to 20.

Reveal judgement

400 × 20 = about 8,000. Therefore 83,580 is unreasonable by roughly a factor of 10.

Self-check

Which estimate is best for 598 ÷ 29?

5. Rounding intervals

If a value rounds to 6.2 to the nearest tenth, the original number lies from halfway below 6.2 to halfway above it.

6.15 ≤ x < 6.25 → x rounds to 6.2 (nearest 0.1)

This is a useful extension because it explains exactly which original values produce the same rounded result.

High-yield

Important questions

  1. Round 18.0047 to 2 decimal places and explain the deciding digit.
  2. State the consecutive integers containing −3.89.
  3. Explain why 1.89 L of paint may require buying 2 L.
  4. Choose a sensible accuracy for an early renovation budget and justify it.
  5. Use estimation to check whether 82.1 × 3.9 = 3,201.9 is reasonable.
  6. Explain why ordinary rounding and purchasing-rounding can give different decisions.
  7. Find the interval of values that round to 4.36 to the nearest 0.01.
  8. Give two different reasonable estimates for 487 × 19.8 and compare them.

Extension

Challenge your reasoning

  1. A number rounds to 7.4 to the nearest tenth and to 7 to the nearest whole number. Describe all possible values.
  2. Create a real purchasing situation where rounding to the nearest whole number gives the wrong practical decision.
  3. Two students choose different estimates for the same product. Develop a rule for deciding which estimate is “better”.
  4. Investigate how repeated rounding during a long calculation can differ from rounding only the final answer.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding before identifying the exact interval.
  • Looking at the wrong digit when rounding to a specified decimal place.
  • Assuming every real-world answer should be rounded to the nearest value.
  • Keeping unnecessary digits and calling that “more accurate” even when the context does not support them.
  • Trusting a calculator answer without checking its magnitude.
