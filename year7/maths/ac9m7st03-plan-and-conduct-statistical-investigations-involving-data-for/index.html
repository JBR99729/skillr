Planning and Conducting Statistical Investigations

Plan a question, collect or source data, analyse centre/spread/shape, interpret what the distribution means, and report conclusions with appropriate limitations.

LearnHomeworkPracticeTest

What AC9M7ST03 is about

This topic moves beyond calculating statistics in isolation. Students run the complete investigation cycle: ask a statistical question, identify variables, collect or source data, choose suitable summaries/displays, interpret the distribution and write a conclusion that matches the evidence.

A strong investigation is only as good as its weakest stage. A careful mean does not rescue a biased sample, and a good graph does not justify a conclusion that goes beyond the data.

1. Plan the investigation

A useful statistical question identifies what will vary and what will be compared. For example: “Does a design modification reduce completion time?” is stronger than “Is the new design better?” because completion time is measurable.

Before collecting data, identify:

  • the question
  • the population or groups of interest
  • the variable being measured
  • whether the variable is discrete, continuous or ordinal
  • how data will be collected or sourced
  • what conditions should be kept consistent

2. Discrete, continuous and secondary data

Discrete data are counts such as number of successful robot lifts. Continuous data are measurements such as reaction time, temperature or height. Ordered survey categories such as 1–5 agreement ratings are ordinal.

Primary data are collected directly for your investigation. Secondary data come from an existing source such as a published survey or report. Secondary data can be valuable, but you must check who collected it, when, how, the sample size, response rate, question wording and stated limitations.

3. Collect data fairly

Comparisons are stronger when measurement conditions are consistent. In a reaction-time investigation, for example, use the same device, instructions and procedure for each group. In a STEM design test, use similar loads, operators and testing conditions before and after modification.

Sampling also matters. A large convenience sample can still be biased. If you want to generalise beyond the observed sample, explain how the sample relates to the target population.

4. Analyse centre, spread and shape

Important: mean and median can disagree because of skewness or extreme values. A difference in centre should therefore be interpreted alongside spread and shape.

5. Choose suitable displays

  • Dot plot: individual values, clusters, gaps and outliers.
  • Stem-and-leaf: retains raw values while showing shape.
  • Histogram: frequencies across numerical intervals; useful for distribution shape.
  • Side-by-side box plots: compact comparison of centre and spread.

Display choice should match the investigation purpose. A graph is evidence, not decoration.

6. Interpret in context

Suppose a modified robot design has median completion time 15 s and range 4 s, compared with 18 s and range 7 s before modification. If lower time is better, the modification appears faster typically and more consistent.

But a complete conclusion also asks whether other factors could explain the change: practice effect, different testing conditions, small sample size or measurement error.

7. Write evidence-based conclusions

A strong conclusion answers the original question and cites the data. Prefer:

“In this sample, the modified design had a lower median completion time and smaller range, suggesting faster and more consistent performance.”

Avoid claims such as “the modification always works better” unless the investigation design and evidence genuinely support that level of generalisation.

8. Report limitations

Common limitations include:

  • small or unrepresentative sample
  • measurement error or limited precision
  • different testing conditions
  • nonresponse in surveys
  • leading question wording
  • practice or order effects
  • secondary data collected for a different purpose

Limitations do not make an investigation useless. They tell the reader how cautiously to interpret the findings.

Common mistakes

  • Collecting data before writing a clear question.
  • Confusing counts with measurements.
  • Comparing groups tested under different conditions.
  • Using only the mean when skewness or outliers matter.
  • Using only range without discussing centre.
  • Deleting outliers automatically.
  • Making causal claims from a simple observational comparison.
  • Generalising from an unrepresentative sample without qualification.
  • Reporting group averages as if they describe every individual.
  • Writing a conclusion without limitations.

Answer guide

Question → plan/collect → analyse → interpret → report. A good question identifies a variable and comparison. Discrete=count, continuous=measurement, ordinal=ordered categories. Primary is collected directly; secondary already exists. Consistent conditions improve fairness. Population=full group of interest; sample=subset observed. Large biased samples remain biased. Median describes ordered centre; range describes simple spread. Skew/outliers can separate mean and median. Outliers may be errors or genuine cases. Dot plots, histograms, stem-and-leaf and box plots can support different comparisons. Conclusions should answer the question with evidence and stay within the scope of the study. Limitations communicate uncertainty. Association does not automatically prove causation.

Australian Curriculum coverage

AC9M7ST03: plan and conduct statistical investigations involving data for discrete and continuous numerical variables; analyse distributions and report findings.

The supplied topic examples emphasise reaction-time investigations, before/after STEM performance and the use of published secondary data. These are represented here as investigation contexts while keeping the mathematical focus on data quality, distribution comparison, justified conclusions and limitations.

International curriculum connections

Comparable middle-years statistics internationally includes posing statistical questions, collecting or sourcing data, selecting displays and summary measures, comparing distributions, considering sampling/measurement limitations and communicating conclusions. Exact terminology and expectations vary; AC9M7ST03 remains the authoritative alignment for this resource.
