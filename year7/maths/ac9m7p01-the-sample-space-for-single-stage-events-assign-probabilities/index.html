Sample Spaces, Probability and Relative Frequency

List all outcomes for single-stage chance experiments, assign probabilities, compare theoretical probability with experimental relative frequency and make sensible predictions.

LearnHomeworkPracticeTest

What students need to master

Probability describes uncertainty with numbers from 0 to 1. This topic builds the foundation: identify every possible outcome, define the event of interest, decide whether outcomes are equally likely, calculate probability and connect the model with results from repeated trials.

Important: expected frequency is a prediction, not a quota. Random experiments can produce different short-run results even when the probability model is correct.

1. Probability language — E1

  • Experiment: the chance process being performed.
  • Trial: one performance of the experiment.
  • Outcome: a possible result.
  • Sample space: all possible outcomes.
  • Event: one outcome or a set of outcomes of interest.
  • Favourable outcome: an outcome belonging to that event.

For one coin toss, S={H,T}. For event “heads”, H is the favourable outcome.

2. Listing sample spaces — E2

For a single fair die roll, S={1,2,3,4,5,6}. For a spinner labelled Red, Blue, Green and Yellow, S={R,B,G,Y}.

The sample space must match the question. If a die problem asks for face outcomes, writing only {even,odd} hides the elementary outcomes needed for many probability calculations.

This curriculum code focuses on single-stage events. Multi-stage examples can be useful extension material, but they should not replace mastery of the one-action sample space.

3. Assigning probabilities

For equally likely outcomes:

P(event) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total number of outcomes.

For a fair die, P(even)=3/6=1/2 and P(greater than 4)=2/6=1/3.

All elementary outcome probabilities must total 1. The complement rule is P(not A)=1−P(A).

Common trap: labels do not guarantee equal probability. If one red spinner sector covers half the circle while blue and green each cover a quarter, P(red)=1/2, not 1/3.

4. Expected frequency — E4

Expected frequency = number of trials × probability.

If P(6)=1/6, then in 60 fair-die rolls the expected number of sixes is 60×1/6=10.

You should say “about 10” or “expected 10”, not “there will be exactly 10”. Chance variation remains.

5. Relative frequency — E3

Relative frequency = observed event frequency ÷ total trials.

If 8 sixes occur in 60 rolls, RF=8/60≈0.133. The theoretical probability is 1/6≈0.167. The values differ because experimental results fluctuate.

With many relevant repeated trials, relative frequency often becomes more stable around the underlying probability. This does not mean it must ever equal the theoretical value exactly.

6. Theoretical vs experimental probability

TypeComes fromExample
Theoretical probabilitya mathematical modelfair die: P(6)=1/6
Experimental probabilityobserved relative frequency8 sixes in 60: 8/60

Experimental evidence can be used to estimate or check a probability model, but a small difference does not automatically prove the model is wrong.

7. Frequency-based prediction in real contexts

Observed relative frequencies can inform predictions. For example, if a word appears 52 times in 1000 observed tokens, its relative frequency is 0.052. A prediction system could use this as one source of evidence about likelihood.

Frequency alone does not guarantee the next outcome, and realistic predictive text also depends on context. The mathematical connection is the same core idea: repeated observations can provide probability estimates.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving possible outcomes out of the sample space.
  • Assuming differently sized spinner sectors are equally likely because they have different labels.
  • Using favourable/total when elementary outcomes are not equally likely.
  • Forgetting probabilities across the complete sample space must total 1.
  • Treating expected frequency as guaranteed.
  • Confusing theoretical probability with observed relative frequency.
  • Thinking previous independent failures make success “due”.
