AC9M7M04 • Year 7 Maths

Parallel lines, transversals and angle relationships

Identify corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles, use their properties to solve problems, and explain each step with geometric reasons.

Topic guideHomeworkPracticeTest
What this skill means

When a line crosses two parallel lines, it creates predictable angle relationships. The crossing line is called a transversal. The three relationships students must recognise are:

  • Corresponding angles: matching positions at the two intersections; they are equal.
  • Alternate interior angles: inside the parallel lines on opposite sides of the transversal; they are equal.
  • Co-interior angles: inside the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal; they sum to 180°.

Students also use vertically opposite angles (equal) and angles on a straight line (sum to 180°) to complete multi-step reasoning.

Prerequisite knowledge
  • Recognise acute, obtuse, right and straight angles.
  • Know that angles on a straight line sum to 180°.
  • Know that vertically opposite angles are equal.
  • Understand that parallel lines remain the same distance apart and never meet.
How to identify the three main angle pairs

Do not memorise one diagram orientation. Use the position of the angles.

  • Corresponding: same relative corner at each intersection.
  • Alternate interior: between the parallel lines, opposite sides of the transversal.
  • Co-interior: between the parallel lines, same side of the transversal.

A diagram can be tilted or rotated and the relationships are unchanged.

Worked examples

Example 1 — corresponding angles

One corresponding angle is 72°. Because corresponding angles are equal, the matching angle is 72°.

Example 2 — co-interior angles

One co-interior angle is 118°. Co-interior angles sum to 180°, so the other angle is 180° − 118° = 62°.

Example 3 — two-step reasoning

An angle is 146°. Its adjacent angle is 34° because angles on a straight line sum to 180°. If that 34° angle is alternate interior to another angle between parallel lines, the second angle is also 34°.

Example 4 — algebraic reasoning

Two co-interior angles are x° and 3x°. Since they sum to 180°: x + 3x = 180, so 4x = 180 and x = 45°. The other angle is 135°.

Why the rules work

Parallel lines have the same direction. When the same transversal crosses each line, the repeated geometry creates equal corresponding and alternate angles. The same-side interior pair fills a straight-angle relationship, so the pair is supplementary. Dynamic geometry software is useful because students can drag the transversal and observe that the numerical measures change while the relationships themselves stay true.

Choose the right strategy
  1. Mark which lines are parallel.
  2. Identify the transversal.
  3. Name the relationship before calculating.
  4. If the pair is corresponding or alternate, copy the angle value.
  5. If the pair is co-interior or a straight-line pair, subtract from 180°.
  6. If the required angle is not directly linked, use a chain of valid steps and state a reason for each one.
Triangle connections

The same geometric reasoning connects to triangles. The interior angles of a triangle sum to 180°. An exterior angle equals the sum of the two opposite, non-adjacent interior angles.

Example: a triangle has interior angles 40° and 65°. The third angle is 180° − 105° = 75°. The exterior angle beside the 75° angle is 105°, which also equals 40° + 65°.

Construction and dynamic geometry

Parallel lines with a ruler and set square: draw one line along a set square, hold a ruler as a guide, slide the set square without rotating it, then draw the second line. The lines have the same direction and are parallel.

Perpendicular lines: use the 90° corner of a set square, or construct a perpendicular bisector using equal-radius compass arcs.

Dynamic geometry: constrain two lines to be parallel, add a transversal, measure angle pairs and drag the transversal. Corresponding and alternate pairs remain equal; co-interior pairs remain supplementary.

Applications

Parallel-line and angle relationships appear in linkages such as scissor lifts, folding umbrellas, toolboxes and cherry pickers. The geometry does not make the mechanism work by itself, but it helps describe and predict how linked bars and supports move relative to one another.

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Calling co-interior angles equal: they normally sum to 180°.
  • Using corresponding or alternate rules without parallel lines: those equality rules depend on parallelism.
  • Trusting the picture: a diagram that looks parallel is not proof that it is parallel.
  • Memorising shapes such as “F”, “Z” and “C” only: these can help initially, but structural position is more reliable when the diagram is rotated.
  • Giving a number without a reason: Year 7 geometric reasoning should state the rule used.
Quick check before Practice
  1. A corresponding angle is 83°. What is its match?
    Answer

    83°.

  2. A co-interior angle is 127°. Find its partner.
    Answer

    53°.

  3. An alternate interior angle is 41°. Find the alternate partner.
    Answer

    41°.

  4. Two corresponding angles are equal. What can you conclude about the two lines?
    Answer

    They are parallel, by the converse corresponding-angle result.

  5. A triangle has angles 50° and 60°. Find the third angle.
    Answer

    70°.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

AC9M7M04: identify corresponding, alternate and co-interior relationships between angles formed when parallel lines are crossed by a transversal; use them to solve problems and explain reasons.

  • E1: construct parallel and perpendicular lines using their properties, compasses and ruler, set squares or dynamic geometry software.
  • E2: use dynamic geometry to identify alternate, corresponding and co-interior relationships.
  • E3: investigate angle properties in scissor lifts, folding umbrellas, toolboxes and cherry pickers.
  • E4: generalise angle relationships using geometric reasoning, including triangle interior-angle sum and the exterior-angle theorem.
