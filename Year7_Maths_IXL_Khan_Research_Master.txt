AC9M7N01 — PERFECT SQUARES AND SQUARE ROOTS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Describe the relationship between perfect square numbers and square roots, and use squares of numbers and square roots of perfect square numbers to solve problems.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Teach squares of whole numbers, recognition of perfect squares, the principal square root and inverse reasoning in meaningful problems. Do not drift into simplifying surds, irrational-number approximation, quadratic equations or difference-of-squares algebra. The radical symbol denotes the non-negative principal root; x² = a may have two solutions, but √a names the principal root.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Primary mapping: J.7 “Square roots of perfect squares” (skill code JKM), https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/square-roots-of-perfect-squares
Supporting mapping: J.1 “Evaluate powers”, https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/evaluate-powers
Live question observed: “What is the square root of 0?” This verifies direct retrieval includes boundary cases, not only 1–144.
Learn-with-an-example observed: “What is the square root of 100?” IXL teaches the learner to find the number which, multiplied by itself, equals the radicand. It displays 10² = 10 × 10 = 100, then reverses the relationship to √100 = 10. The explanation explicitly frames squaring and square root as opposite operations.
IXL progression inferred from the live skill: direct perfect-square roots; zero and one; fluency with square notation; inverse-operation checks; later items can place the same knowledge in area or missing-side settings.
High-value IXL misconception evidence: confusing √100 with 50 or with ±10; reading the radical as division by two; treating n² as 2n; failing to check by squaring.

KHAN ACADEMY EVIDENCE
Authenticated page inspected: “Intro to square roots”, https://www.khanacademy.org/math/algebra/x2f8bb11595b61c86:rational-exponents-radicals/x2f8bb11595b61c86:radicals/v/introduction-to-square-roots
Khan develops the inverse relationship from 3² = 9 to √9 = 3 and repeats with 4² = 16 and 5² = 25. It distinguishes the principal root √9 = 3 from solving x² = 9, where x can be 3 or −3. This distinction belongs in teacher notes and feedback, but test questions must remain inside the Year 7 perfect-square boundary.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: begin with square arrays and the vocabulary factor, square, perfect square, radical, square root and principal root. Build a table n, n × n, n² for n = 0–20. State the inverse test: if a² = b, then √b = a for a ≥ 0.
Classroom View: slide 1 visual square array; slide 2 notation connection; slide 3 worked example “A square garden has area 196 m². Side = √196 = 14 m”; slide 4 misconception contrast √81 = 9, not ±9; exit ticket combines recognition and context.
Homework: mix fact fluency, missing entries in square/root tables, area-to-side, side-to-area, perimeter after finding a side and explain-your-reasoning items.
Feedback pattern: name the inverse relation, show the matching multiplication fact, then verify by squaring the answer.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 identify perfect squares and match n² to values.
P09–P16 evaluate squares including 0, 1 and two-digit bases within fluent range.
P17–P24 evaluate principal square roots of perfect squares.
P25–P32 complete inverse-operation and missing-number statements.
P33–P40 solve square-area, tile-array and side-length problems.
P41–P48 multi-step perimeter, scaling, error analysis and justification.
Difficulty: 16 accessible, 16 developing, 12 challenging, 4 stretch. Use original values/contexts.

16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 direct square/root fluency; T05–T08 inverse statements and perfect-square classification; T09–T12 original area/perimeter applications; T13–T16 independent multi-step reasoning and misconception diagnosis. No Practice stem may be reused with cosmetic number changes.

AC9M7N02 — PRIME FACTORISATION WITH EXPONENT NOTATION
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Represent natural numbers as products of powers of prime numbers using exponent notation.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
The required endpoint is a unique prime factorisation written as ascending prime powers. HCF and LCM are useful applications/elaborations but should support, not replace, the representation requirement. Distinguish factors from prime factors and exponents from multiplication coefficients.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Primary mapping: A.2 “Prime factorisation” (skill code RLB), https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/prime-factorisation
Supporting mappings: A.1 “Prime or composite”; A.5 “Highest common factor”; A.6 “Lowest common multiple”; A.7 “HCF and LCM: word problems”; Year 6 prerequisite “Write multiplication expressions using exponents”.
Learn-with-an-example observed: write the prime factorisation of 12, use exponents when appropriate and order factors least to greatest. IXL’s method repeatedly divides by prime factors until the quotient is 1: 12 ÷ 2 = 6, 6 ÷ 2 = 3, 3 ÷ 3 = 1; then writes 2 × 2 × 3 and compresses the repeated 2 as 2² × 3.
IXL design evidence: directions explicitly constrain canonical answer form; answer checking must accept mathematically equivalent ordering internally while feedback teaches least-to-greatest convention.
Common traps: stopping at a composite factor (for example 4 × 6); writing 2 × 6; using 2 × 3² for 12; exponent counts the repeated factors, not their sum; treating 1 as prime.

KHAN ACADEMY EVIDENCE
Mapped Khan resource: “Prime factorization”, which teaches factor trees/repeated decomposition and writing repeated prime factors with exponent notation: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/class-6-tg/x47164056520f4c0a%3Aplaying-with-numbers/x47164056520f4c0a%3Aprime-factorisation/v/prime-factorization-indian-accent
Supporting Khan concept: “Exponent example 2” explains that an exponent gives the number of repeated factors, not a multiplier.
Khan page access was checked in the single authenticated tab. Public metadata confirms prime definition and exponential-notation endpoint.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: define prime/composite; show divisibility checks; model both a factor tree and repeated division; circle prime leaves; regroup equal primes into powers; verify by expanding and multiplying.
Classroom View: use 180 as a teacher model, deliberately show two different factor trees converging on the same prime-power product; include “Is 4 × 45 finished?” hinge question; finish with reverse reconstruction from 2³ × 3².
Homework: prime/composite warm-up, factor trees, repeated-division ladders, exponent compression, expansion checks, HCF/LCM applications and explain why factor-tree shape does not change the final product.
Feedback pattern: identify the first composite factor remaining, decompose it, regroup repeated primes, expand to verify the original number.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 prime/composite and factor vocabulary prerequisites.
P09–P16 complete factor trees.
P17–P24 repeated division by primes.
P25–P32 write canonical prime-power products.
P33–P36 reconstruct numbers from prime powers.
P37–P40 compare two decompositions and detect errors.
P41–P44 use prime powers for HCF.
P45–P48 use prime powers for LCM and contextual cycles/grouping.
Ensure products vary materially and include repeated powers of 2, 3 and 5 plus at least one larger prime.

16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 prerequisite discrimination and direct factorisation; T05–T08 exponent-form conversion/reconstruction; T09–T12 error analysis and uniqueness reasoning; T13–T16 original HCF/LCM or multi-step applications. Test contexts, values and reasoning structures must be independent from Practice.

AC9M7N03 — EXPANDED NOTATION USING PLACE VALUE AND POWERS OF 10
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Represent natural numbers in expanded notation using place value and powers of 10.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Use natural numbers and non-negative whole-number powers of 10. Connect standard form, place-value expansion, multiplication form and exponent form. Scientific notation with non-integer coefficients and negative exponents belongs later and is out of scope.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Primary mapping: J.4 “Powers of ten” (skill code FQ6), https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/powers-of-ten
Supporting mapping: J.1 “Evaluate powers”; relevant place-value review skills on the Year 7 list.
Learn-with-an-example observed: find the missing exponent in 10^? = 100 000. IXL defines an exponent as the number of times the base is used as a factor, then tabulates 10¹ = 10 through 10⁴ = 10 000. For powers of 10, the exponent equals the number of zeros after the leading 1; therefore 100 000 = 10⁵.
This shortcut must be taught after the repeated-factor meaning, not as an unexplained trick.
Question families required beyond the observed missing exponent: value ↔ exponent matching; digit value; full expanded form; missing place; standard-form reconstruction; zero internal digits.

KHAN ACADEMY EVIDENCE
Authenticated Khan search for “powers of 10 expanded form” did not yield a clean age-matched lesson in its first results: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for a single exact Year 7 lesson.
Relevant Khan foundation remains exponent meaning (“Exponent example 2”) and place-value/expanded-form material in elementary arithmetic. Use it only for conceptual cross-checking; IXL and AC v9/QCAA provide the direct mapping.
Public Khan evidence reinforces: b^n means n factors of b. Therefore 10⁵ means five factors of 10 and has place value one hundred thousand.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: place-value chart from ones to millions; align each column with 10⁰, 10¹, …; demonstrate 604 032 = 6×10⁵ + 0×10⁴ + 4×10³ + 0×10² + 3×10¹ + 2×10⁰, then show the conventional omission of zero terms.
Classroom View: physically shift a digit one place left and multiply its value by 10; compare 10³ and 3¹⁰ to prevent base/exponent reversal; reconstruct a number from unordered place-value terms; include zeros as placeholders.
Homework: read/write powers of ten, value-of-digit questions, expansions, reconstructions, missing exponent/coefficients, error correction and large-number contexts.
Feedback pattern: locate the digit’s column, name its value, write digit × matching power of 10, and verify by evaluating the sum.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 evaluate powers of 10 and identify exponents.
P09–P16 match place names with powers of 10.
P17–P24 expand natural numbers with multiplication by powers.
P25–P32 reconstruct standard form, including internal/trailing zeros.
P33–P40 find missing digits, coefficients, exponents or terms.
P41–P48 compare representations, order terms, analyse errors and apply to populations/distances/data counts.
Use 10⁰ explicitly in some items and accept omission of zero-coefficient terms where appropriate.

16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 powers/place-value fluency; T05–T08 two-way conversion; T09–T12 missing-part and zero-placeholder reasoning; T13–T16 independent contextual and error-analysis items. No scientific notation or negative exponents.

AC9M7N04 — EQUIVALENT REPRESENTATIONS OF RATIONAL NUMBERS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Find equivalent representations of rational numbers and represent rational numbers on a number line.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Cover equivalent fractions, simplest form, improper fractions/mixed numbers and decimal/fraction equivalence where denominators permit terminating decimals; locate and compare positive and negative rational numbers on number lines as demanded by the descriptor. Do not restrict the bank to part-whole diagrams between 0 and 1.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Primary mappings:
- F.2 “Graph equivalent fractions on number lines”, https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/graph-equivalent-fractions-on-number-lines
- F.3 “Equivalent fractions”, https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/equivalent-fractions
- F.4 “Write fractions in lowest terms”, https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/write-fractions-in-lowest-terms
Supporting: F.7 compare/order fractions, F.9 mixed/improper conversion, and decimal number-line skills.

IXL Learn-with-an-example evidence inspected sequentially:
1. Number-line equivalence: top line partitioned into fourths and bottom into eighths; the same point shows 1/4 = 2/8. The method is positional: equal rational numbers occupy exactly the same point even when partitions differ.
2. Missing equivalent numerator: 1/4 = ?/8. Parallel number lines show that the denominator doubled and the numerator also doubled, producing 2/8.
3. Lowest terms: simplify 2/6. IXL defines lowest terms as the smallest possible numerator and denominator, finds the largest common divisor 2, then divides both by 2 to obtain 1/3.
Critical authoring rule: representational equivalence must be justified by scaling both numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number or by coincidence at the same number-line point. “Make denominators the same” is not itself a proof.
Common traps: changing only numerator or denominator; counting marks rather than equal intervals; assuming larger denominator means larger fraction; plotting negative rationals in the wrong direction; mistaking simplest form for a decimal approximation.

KHAN ACADEMY EVIDENCE
Mapped resource: “Creating equivalent fractions”, https://www.khanacademy.org/math/arithmetic/fraction-arithmetic/x18ca194a%3Aequivalent-fractions/v/generating-equivalent-fractions
Public metadata states the lesson uses 2/3, 4/6 and 6/9 and represents equivalence on a number line.
Supporting Khan resource: “Equivalent fractions with models”, which uses area/tape models; authenticated page access was checked in the single Khan tab.
Khan’s useful teaching move is to connect visual subdivision to multiplying numerator and denominator by the same factor, rather than presenting a rule alone.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: open with one point represented as 1/2, 2/4, 0.5 and 50%; show fraction strips and aligned number lines; formalise k·a/k·b = a/b for non-zero k; simplify via greatest common divisor; extend the number line left of zero and beyond one.
Classroom View: compare aligned fourth/eighth number lines; jointly construct an equivalence chain; model −3/4 between −1 and 0; use a double number line or hundred grid for fraction-decimal-percent links; exit ticket must require both a representation and a justification.
Homework: fraction-wall/number-line interpretation, missing equivalent terms, simplest form, mixed/improper conversions, terminating decimal equivalents, positive/negative plotting and contextual comparisons.
Feedback pattern: identify the scale factor or common divisor, apply it to both terms, then verify with cross-products or the same number-line location.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 read fraction strips and aligned number lines.
P09–P16 generate equivalent fractions and fill missing terms.
P17–P24 simplify using common divisors/GCD.
P25–P30 convert improper fractions and mixed numbers.
P31–P36 convert familiar fractions, decimals and percentages.
P37–P42 plot/order positive rational numbers including values >1.
P43–P46 plot/order negative rational numbers.
P47–P48 multi-representation error analysis and contextual justification.
Models must have mathematically correct partition counts and labelled endpoints.

16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 direct equivalence/simplest form; T05–T08 conversion among forms; T09–T12 number-line placement including negative and >1 values; T13–T16 independent comparison, error analysis and contextual reasoning. Avoid reusing Practice visuals, numbers or story structures.

AC9M7N05 — ROUNDING, ACCURACY AND REASONABLENESS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Round decimals to a given accuracy appropriate to the context and use appropriate rounding and estimation to check the reasonableness of solutions.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must round to specified decimal places or context-appropriate units, estimate results of all four operations, and decide whether an exact answer is plausible. Accuracy is a decision tied to context: money commonly uses cents, people/items may require whole numbers, and measurements depend on the instrument or purpose. Do not turn the unit into mechanical rounding only.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Primary mappings: D.4 “Round decimals”; E.3 “Estimate sums and differences of decimals”; E.8 “Estimate products of decimals”; E.12 “Estimate quotients of decimals”.
IXL Learn-with-an-example evidence:
- Round 41.4 to the nearest whole number. IXL identifies the ones digit, inspects the digit one place right, applies “less than 5 down; 5 or higher up”, and removes digits to the right: 41.4 → 41.
- Estimate 8.9 + 4.51 by rounding each addend to a whole number: 9 + 5 = 14.
- Estimate 2.7 × 6.8 by rounding each factor to a whole number: 3 × 7 = 21.
Direct URLs: https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/round-decimals ; https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/estimate-sums-and-differences-of-decimals ; https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/estimate-products-of-decimals
Question progression: identify rounding place → inspect deciding digit → calculate rounded value → estimate a result → compare estimate and exact value → judge a contextual claim.
Misconceptions: rounding every digit independently; looking left instead of right; changing unaffected digits; always rounding to whole numbers; confusing truncation with rounding; accepting calculator output without a magnitude check.

KHAN / PUBLIC EVIDENCE
Khan mapped support: estimating multi-digit division by choosing compatible rounded numbers, https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-fifth-grade-math/multi-digit-multiplication-and-division/multi-digit-division-estimation/v/approximating-multi-digit-division
The exact Year 7 AC v9 combination of decimal accuracy plus reasonableness has no single Khan lesson: use Khan as method support and retain the Australian Curriculum/QCAA descriptor as scope authority.
Trusted cross-checks: Australian Curriculum v9 Mathematics and QCAA Year 7 alignment listed in the source register.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: teach a place-value “target digit / deciding digit” routine, a number-line midpoint model, then distinguish stated accuracy from context-selected accuracy. Add front-end rounding and compatible-number estimation; close with estimate–calculate–compare.
Classroom View: model 6.475 to 2 decimal places, discuss the midpoint; estimate a shopping total; reveal an implausible calculator answer and diagnose decimal-place error. Include language: approximately, nearest, decimal place, significant context, upper/lower neighbour, reasonableness.
Homework: direct rounding, interval/midpoint items, context-choice items, all-four-operation estimates, exact-versus-estimate comparisons and written justification.
Feedback: name the target place, underline the deciding digit, state the rounding decision, preserve other place values, then compare the result with nearby benchmarks.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 round to whole numbers/tenths; P09–P16 hundredths/thousandths and midpoint cases; P17–P24 choose sensible contextual accuracy; P25–P32 estimate sums/differences; P33–P40 estimate products/quotients with compatible numbers; P41–P48 calculator reasonableness, bounds, error analysis and multi-step contexts.
16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 direct accuracy; T05–T08 context selection; T09–T12 independent estimation; T13–T16 reasonableness/error diagnosis. Use original numbers and contexts, with no Practice clones.

AC9M7N06 — OPERATIONS WITH POSITIVE RATIONAL NUMBERS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Apply the four operations to solve problems involving positive rational numbers, including fractions, decimals and percentages, and use efficient calculation strategies.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Positive rational numbers only for this code. Include fractions, mixed numbers, decimals and percentages; select a convenient representation and operation; simplify and interpret results. Negative-integer addition/subtraction belongs to AC9M7N07. Percentage modelling in practical/financial contexts is extended in AC9M7N09.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Mapped IXL families:
- Fractions: G.1–G.11 add/subtract; H.1–H.24 multiply/divide; “Evaluate numerical expressions involving fractions”.
- Decimals: E.1–E.17 covering four operations, estimation and word problems.
- Rational-number integration: “Convert between decimals and fractions or mixed numbers”; “Add and subtract rational numbers”; “Multiply and divide rational numbers”.
- Percentages: conversion and percent-of-number skills.
Worked example captured for 1/3 + 1/6 using fraction bars. IXL renames 1/3 as 2/6, combines 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6, then expects simplification. This is strong evidence for a model-before-algorithm sequence.
Several multiplication/division examples were visually rendered but their mathematical text was not exposed in the accessibility snapshot; mappings were recorded without inventing example details.
High-value methods: common denominators for addition/subtraction; simplify before/after multiplication; division as multiplying by the reciprocal, supported by measurement/grouping interpretations; align decimal places; convert percentages to fraction/decimal form when efficient.
Misconceptions: adding denominators; reciprocal of the first instead of second fraction; treating a percentage as a whole number; losing decimal place; failing to simplify or attach units; using one representation when another is much easier.

KHAN / PUBLIC EVIDENCE
Khan mapped resource: “Adding fractions, decimals and percentages” uses a mixed expression such as 0.79 − 4/3 − 1/2 + 150%, showing the need to convert to compatible forms: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-seventh-grade-math-essentials/x220f497c584d1b35%3Arational-numbers-addition-and-subtraction/x220f497c584d1b35%3Aadding-subtracting-rational-numbers/v/adding-fractions-decimals-and-percentages
Use Khan’s representation-conversion reasoning, not its negative-number extension, for this positive-only descriptor.
Trusted scope cross-checks: Australian Curriculum v9 and QCAA Year 7 alignment.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: a decision tree—identify operation, inspect forms, choose common form, calculate, simplify, interpret/check. Provide visual fraction addition, decimal area/grid multiplication, fraction division as “how many groups?”, and percent benchmarks 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%.
Classroom View: compare two valid strategies for the same problem and ask which is more efficient; include a worked recipe, length, and percentage-allocation example.
Homework: separated fluency sections followed by mixed-representation and contextual problems; require strategy labels and estimates for selected items.
Feedback: diagnose representation or operation first, then show the shortest correction and an estimate/check.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 fraction addition/subtraction; P09–P16 fraction multiplication/division; P17–P24 decimal operations; P25–P32 percentage operations and conversion; P33–P40 mixed-representation choice; P41–P48 original multi-step recipe, measurement, allocation and error-analysis problems.

AC9M7N07 — COMPARE, ORDER, ADD AND SUBTRACT INTEGERS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Compare, order and solve problems involving addition and subtraction of integers.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Include integer meaning, horizontal/vertical number lines, < > =, magnitude/absolute distance, addition and subtraction, missing values and authentic contexts. Multiplication and division of integers are not required by this descriptor.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE
Mappings: B.1–B.5 understanding, graphing, comparing and ordering integers; C.1–C.13 integer addition/subtraction with rules, counters, number lines, equations and word problems.
Learn-with-an-example evidence:
- Compare −2 and −6: IXL places both on a −10 to 10 number line; −2 lies to the right, so −2 > −6.
- Model −2 − 7 with counters: start with two negative counters; add seven zero pairs to create enough positive counters to remove; after removing seven positives, nine negatives remain, so −2 − 7 = −9.
- Word problem: a score of 700 followed by a loss of 400 gives 300, explicitly interpreting the integer in context.
Direct examples: https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/compare-integers ; https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/subtract-integers-using-counters ; https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/add-and-subtract-integers-word-problems
Misconceptions: “larger absolute value means greater”; reversing < and > for negatives; treating subtraction as always making smaller; dropping a negative sign; confusing −(−a) with −a; counting tick marks rather than intervals.

KHAN / PUBLIC EVIDENCE
Khan “Adding the opposite with number lines” teaches: adding positive moves right, subtracting positive moves left, and subtraction can be rewritten as adding the opposite: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-seventh-grade-math/cc-7th-negative-numbers-add-and-subtract/x6b17ba59%3Asubtracting-negative-numbers-fluently/v/adding-the-opposite-with-number-lines
Supporting Khan examples cover 2−3, −2−3, −2+3 and 2−(−3), reinforcing direction and sign distinctions.
Trusted scope cross-checks: Australian Curriculum v9 and QCAA.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: ground integers in temperature, elevation, credit/debt and score change; use horizontal and vertical number lines; teach “right/up is greater”; connect counters and zero pairs to addition/subtraction; only then introduce add-the-opposite notation.
Classroom View: human number-line activity; compare −2/−6; counter model of −2−7; context translation where direction words determine signed change; exit ticket includes explanation.
Homework: placement/comparison/order, counter drawings, number-line moves, symbolic calculations, missing-number sentences and multi-step contexts.
Feedback: translate each sign into starting value, operation and direction; show a model; then verify with the inverse operation.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 identify/locate integers; P09–P16 compare/order; P17–P24 add using number lines/counters; P25–P32 subtract including zero-pair cases; P33–P40 mixed addition/subtraction and missing values; P41–P48 temperature, elevation, finance, scoring and error analysis.
16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 compare/order; T05–T08 addition/subtraction fluency; T09–T12 independent contexts; T13–T16 missing values, multi-step reasoning and misconception diagnosis.

AC9M7N08 — RATIOS AND EQUIVALENT RELATIONSHIPS
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Recognise, represent and solve problems involving ratios.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Include part-to-part and part-to-whole interpretations, order-sensitive notation, equivalent ratios, scaling, tables/double number lines and division of a quantity in a ratio. Keep ratio distinct from a fraction while explaining their connection. Formal linear proportional equations are supportive, not the sole representation.

IXL MAPPING AND LIVE EVIDENCE

The worked-example method family is clear across these skills: order data before finding the median; add all values and divide by the number of values for mean; find the most frequent value for mode; subtract minimum from maximum for range; then compare how an outlier changes each statistic.
Required authoring extension beyond IXL calculation: learners acquire their own discrete and continuous data and justify a statistic in context.
Misconceptions: calling measured data discrete because readings are written as whole numbers; forgetting to order for median; dividing by the sum rather than count; assuming every set has one mode; including range as a measure of centre; automatically choosing mean despite an extreme outlier; giving a statistic without units/context.

KHAN / TRUSTED EVIDENCE
Khan’s statistics lessons support mean, median, mode, range and the effect of outliers; its distribution lesson distinguishes peaks, gaps, clusters and outliers: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-sixth-grade-math/cc-6th-data-statistics/cc-6-shape-of-data/v/examples-analyzing-clusters-gaps-peaks-and-outliers-for-distributions
Australian Bureau of Statistics education resources provide trustworthy real datasets and guidance on statistical investigations: https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/education
Australian Curriculum/QCAA control the discrete/continuous acquisition and justification requirements.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: discrete-vs-continuous sorting; ethical/repeatable measurement notes; worked ordered data set; visual “balance point” for mean; median as middle; mode as frequency peak; outlier comparison table; contextual statistic-choice sentence frames.
Classroom View: collect class measurements and counts, calculate with spreadsheet and by hand, insert/remove an outlier, compare centre/range, and defend the most useful measure.
Homework: classify variables, design collection methods, calculate statistics, repair errors, compare outlier effects and justify a measure for wages, travel times and survey data.
Feedback: classify how the value was generated, order/check the data, state the correct calculation, attach units and justify the measure using shape/outlier evidence.

16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 variable/data acquisition; T05–T08 calculation; T09–T12 independent outlier/comparison questions; T13–T16 contextual measure choice and justification.

STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Plan and conduct statistical investigations involving data for discrete and continuous numerical variables; analyse and interpret distributions of data and report findings in terms of shape and summary statistics.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Complete the inquiry cycle: pose a clear statistical question anticipating variability; identify population/variables; choose a fair collection or sampling method; collect/clean data; select displays and summaries; analyse shape, centre, spread and outliers; report a contextual conclusion with limitations. IXL MAPPING AND EVIDENCE

- “Create/interpret dot plots”

- “Measures of centre and spread”
The mapped sequence supports component evidence: recognise biased convenience/voluntary samples; organise data; choose displays; calculate summaries; identify outliers. The authoring must explicitly connect these components into planning, conducting and reporting.

AC9M7P01 — SAMPLE SPACES AND SINGLE-STAGE PROBABILITY
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY

CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Identify the sample space for single-stage events; assign probabilities to the outcomes of these events and predict relative frequencies for related events.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
List complete sample spaces for one-stage experiments; distinguish outcomes from events; assign probabilities between 0 and 1 for equally likely or explicitly weighted outcomes; calculate event probabilities; and predict expected/relative frequencies over a stated number of trials. Repeated experimental comparison belongs mainly to AC9M7P02, though reasonableness checks are useful.

IXL MAPPING AND EVIDENCE
Direct Year 7 mappings:
- “More, less and equally likely”
- “Sample space of simple events”: https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/sample-space-of-simple-events
- “Probability of simple events”: https://au.ixl.com/maths/year-7/probability-of-simple-events
- “Probability of opposite, mutually exclusive and overlapping events”
- “Make predictions”
Supporting “Experimental probability” is reserved mainly for the next code.
IXL progression: enumerate outcomes of a spinner/die/bag; count favourable and total equally likely outcomes; write P(event)=favourable/total; use complements or event language; multiply probability by the number of trials to predict a frequency.
Authoring must distinguish equally likely outcomes from equally named categories: a spinner colour covering three equal sectors has three elementary outcomes, not one.
Misconceptions: incomplete or duplicated sample space; confusing event with outcome; denominator equals number of favourable outcomes; treating physical outcomes as equally likely without evidence; probabilities outside [0,1]; forgetting to simplify or give expected frequency in context; promising an exact future count rather than a prediction.

KHAN / TRUSTED EVIDENCE
Khan “Theoretical probability” introduces probability through a fair coin and die and uses favourable outcomes over all equally likely outcomes: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/statistics-probability/probability-library/basic-theoretical-probability/v/basic-probability
Khan’s theoretical-versus-experimental lesson emphasises that observed relative frequency need not exactly equal theoretical probability in a finite run: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/cc-seventh-grade-math/cc-7th-probability-statistics/cc-7th-theoretical-and-experimental-probability/v/comparing-theoretical-to-experimental-probabilites
NRICH probability tasks provide trusted sample-space and fairness reasoning: https://nrich.maths.org/primary/geometry-and-measure/probability
Australian Curriculum/QCAA control single-stage scope.

AUTHORING TRANSFER
Topic Page: vocabulary and 0–1 probability scale; systematic sample-space lists/tables; fair die, unequal-colour spinner and bag examples; event probability; complement check; predicted frequency n×p; distinguish prediction from guarantee.
Classroom View: list outcomes before calculating, physical spinner fairness discussion, human probability line, card/die stations and compare two representations of the same sample space.
Homework: complete sample spaces, assign outcome/event probabilities, diagnose unequal-likelihood traps, use complements, predict frequencies and explain uncertainty.
Feedback: list elementary outcomes once, decide whether they are equally likely, count favourable/total or use given weights, check total probability is 1 and phrase frequency as an expectation.

48-PRACTICE BLUEPRINT
P01–P08 vocabulary/probability scale; P09–P16 sample spaces; P17–P24 equally likely event probabilities; P25–P32 unequal representations/complements; P33–P40 predicted frequencies; P41–P48 fairness, error analysis and contextual reasoning.
16-TEST BLUEPRINT
T01–T04 sample-space fluency; T05–T08 event probabilities; T09–T12 independent weighted/complement/frequency questions; T13–T16 fairness and justification. Keep all events single-stage.
