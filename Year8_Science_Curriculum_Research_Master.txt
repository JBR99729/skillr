AC9S8U01
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Recognise cells as the basic units of living things, compare plant and animal cells, and describe the functions of specialised cell structures and organelles.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students need a functional Year 8 model of cells: cells as the basic structural and functional unit; common plant/animal structures; features distinctive to typical plant cells; and structure–function links. Detailed biochemical pathways, membrane transport calculations and cell-cycle regulation are outside this code. Avoid the false absolute that every plant cell has chloroplasts.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact content description above. The Year 8 level framing introduces microscopic cell structures as explanations for macroscopic living systems.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 8 Science, section P “Cells”. Exact mapped skills observed:
   - P.1 Understanding cells
   - P.2 Identify functions of plant cell parts
   - P.3 Identify functions of animal cell parts
   - P.4 Plant cell diagrams: label parts
   - P.5 Animal cell diagrams: label parts
   - P.6 Compare cells and cell parts
   - P.7 Mitosis and the cell cycle (extension/partial mapping only)
   Interactive evidence: P.6 begins with staged mastery (“3 questions correctly in a row”). A sampled item asks learners to select the one true statement from function/structure claims, including that vacuoles store nutrients, water and waste, contrasted with distractors assigning cell walls and chloroplasts to animal cells or protein-building to cell walls. Correct response receives immediate positive feedback. This verifies a progression from recall to comparison and misconception diagnosis.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated: Middle school biology “Cells and organisms” provides video, “Learn and try: Cell theory”, “Understand: Cells and organisms” and “Apply: Cell theory”. The article develops the evidence base from microscopy and states the three core claims: organisms consist of one or more cells; cells are the basic unit of structure and function; cells arise from pre-existing cells. Khan’s Cambridge cellular-organisation sequence also includes cell parts/functions, plant versus animal cells, and specialised plant/animal cells. Useful as supplementary practice, not an Australian-code mapping.
4. OpenStax Biology/Anatomy public resources: eukaryotic cells contain specialised organelles; organelle descriptions support accurate structure–function explanations and diagrams.
5. ABC Education search: no stable, direct Year 8 resource mapping was located in the current search. Record SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED; do not fabricate an ABC alignment.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Cell membrane: selectively controls movement between cell and surroundings.
- Cytoplasm: site/medium for many cell reactions.
- Nucleus: contains genetic material and coordinates cell activities at this level.
- Mitochondria: release usable energy through cellular respiration.
- Ribosomes: build proteins.
- Plant cell wall: rigid support/protection; outside the membrane.
- Chloroplasts: photosynthesis in photosynthetic plant cells.
- Large central vacuole: water/solute storage and support through turgor.
- Typical animal cells lack cell walls and chloroplasts and usually do not have one large central vacuole.
- Specialised cell shape/structures suit function: root-hair cell surface area, palisade-cell chloroplast abundance, red-blood-cell form, neuron length/branching, muscle-cell energy demand.

QUESTION/CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: cell theory; scale; labelled plant and animal models; comparison table; organelle-function reasoning; specialised-cell case studies; model limitations.
Classroom View: retrieval warm-up, microscopy/model comparison, structure–function discussion, misconception checks.
Homework/Worksheet: labels, compare/contrast, explain functions, apply to unfamiliar specialised cells, critique a faulty diagram.
Practice bank coverage (48 later): 8 cell-theory/basic-unit items; 12 organelle/function items; 8 diagram/position items; 8 plant-animal comparisons; 8 specialised-cell applications; 4 model/evidence items.
Test bank coverage (16 later): independent contexts with balanced recall, diagram interpretation and structure–function reasoning; no duplicated practice stems.

MISCONCEPTIONS AND REQUIRED FEEDBACK
- “Cell wall replaces membrane”: plant cells have both.
- “All plant cells have chloroplasts”: only photosynthetic cells typically do.
- “Vacuoles are exclusive to plants”: emphasis is the large central plant vacuole.
- “Mitochondria make energy from nothing”: energy is transformed/released from chemical stores.
- “Bigger organisms have bigger cells”: commonly they have more cells.
Feedback must name the mistaken structure/function and state the corrected relationship.

AC9S8U02
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Analyse the relationship between structure and function of cells, tissues and organs in a plant and an animal organ system and explain how these systems enable survival of the individual.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
This code requires analysis across levels, not a list of body parts. Authoring must include both a plant system and an animal organ system, explicitly linking cell/tissue/organ structure to function and then to survival. Advanced histology and full medical physiology are not required.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact content description above. ACARA teacher background material for AC9S8I08 explicitly cross-references AC9S8U02 and suggests scientific drawings/representations of internal systems, including circulatory, digestive or reproductive systems.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 8 Science, section Q “Anatomy and physiology”. Exact relevant skills:
   - Q.1 Organisation in the human body
   - Q.2 Body systems: circulation and respiration
   - Q.3 Body systems: digestion
   - Q.4 Body systems: removing waste
   - Q.5 Body systems: perception and motion
   - Q.6 Science literacy: phantom pain (extension)
   Interactive evidence: Q.1 uses an orchestra analogy and explicitly teaches cell → tissue → organ → organ system. The sampled multiple-select item tests the truths that organs are made of tissues and the human body consists of smaller parts working together, while rejecting reversed hierarchy and “all cells do the same job”. Stage 1 requires 5 of 6 correct. IXL is strong for animal organisation; plant organ-system coverage is limited, so this code is only a partial IXL mapping overall.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated/public: “Tissues, organs, and organ systems” presents nested organisation, specialised cells forming tissues, tissues forming organs and organs forming systems. It stresses that structure relates to function and that integrated organ-system activity enables survival. Khan’s Cambridge cellular-organisation course additionally includes dermal tissue, xylem, root systems, stems, human skin, neurons and animal organ-system reviews. Supplementary, not ACARA mapped.
4. OpenStax Biology 2e: plant bodies contain specialised cells, organs and tissue systems; dermal, ground and vascular tissues provide protection, photosynthesis/storage and transport. OpenStax anatomy resources provide accurate animal examples.
5. ABC Education search: no stable direct resource mapping was located in the current search; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Hierarchy: specialised cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism.
- Structure–function is causal: features enable processes; coordinated processes support survival.
- Plant example: root hair cells increase absorption area; roots anchor/absorb; xylem conducts water/minerals and supports; phloem transports sugars; leaves use palisade tissue/stomata/veins for photosynthesis and gas/material exchange; root and shoot systems coordinate resource acquisition and transport.
- Animal example can use digestive + circulatory coordination, or respiratory + circulatory: thin exchange surfaces, branching, muscular tissues, blood vessels and pumping structures enable uptake and distribution of materials.
- Survival explanation should trace matter: entry/exchange → transport → use by cells → waste removal.

QUESTION/CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: organisation hierarchy; one detailed plant pathway; one detailed animal pathway; side-by-side structure–function chains; survival explanation template.
Classroom View: card-sort hierarchy, annotate diagrams, trace a water molecule through a plant and oxygen/nutrient through an animal.
Homework: classify examples by level, construct causal chains, compare plant and animal systems, diagnose system failure.
Practice bank (48 later): 8 hierarchy; 12 animal structures/functions; 12 plant structures/functions; 8 material-flow/system coordination; 8 unfamiliar-case reasoning.
Test bank (16 later): 4 hierarchy/diagram; 4 plant; 4 animal; 4 comparative causal explanations.

MISCONCEPTIONS AND REQUIRED FEEDBACK
- Tissue and organ treated as synonyms.
- Hierarchy reversed.
- Structure merely named without explaining how it enables function.
- Plant systems reduced to “roots drink water”.
- Systems described independently when survival depends on coordination.
Feedback must complete the causal chain and identify the missing organisational level or transport step.

AC9S8U03
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Investigate tectonic activity including the formation of geological features at divergent, convergent and transform plate boundaries and describe the scientific evidence for the theory of plate tectonics.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students must connect relative plate motion to features/processes and evaluate multiple evidence lines. They should distinguish Wegener’s continental-drift proposal from the modern plate-tectonics theory. Mantle convection detail can support but should not become an unsupported single-cause account.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact content description above.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 8 Science, section Z “Earth’s features”. Exact mappings:
   - Evidence of continental drift and plate tectonics
   - Z.2 Label Earth features at tectonic plate boundaries
   - Z.3 Describe tectonic plate boundaries around the world
   Interactive evidence: the evidence skill opens with Wegener’s 1912 theory, the apparent fit of Africa and South America, and a multiple-select task distinguishing “continents have always occupied present locations” from “connected long ago then moved apart” and recognising theory formation from repeated observations. Stage 1 requires 5 of 7 correct. The boundary skills add model/diagram and global-location application.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated/public: “Introduction to plate tectonics” defines lithospheric plates moving over softer asthenosphere, and compares:
   - divergent: move apart; rifts and mid-ocean ridges
   - convergent: move together; mountains and trenches (with subduction/collision distinctions)
   - transform: slide past; earthquakes
   It uses complementary continental coastlines as one evidence line. Relevant course practice is supplementary, not ACARA mapped.
4. Geoscience Australia: classroom resources explicitly filter for Year 7/8, Science, Plate tectonics, Rocks and Natural hazards. GA earthquake material explains Australian-plate motion and intraplate stress release; teacher programs specifically address plate-tectonics misconceptions. This is the preferred Australian geoscience authority.
5. USGS: “This Dynamic Earth” and plate-tectonics education resources connect subduction with volcanic arcs and use fossil/continental evidence, boundary diagrams, seafloor spreading and global earthquake/volcano distributions.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct mapping found in the current search; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Lithosphere is divided into moving plates; boundaries are zones of interaction.
- Divergent: plates separate; magma rises/cools; mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, new oceanic crust, shallow earthquakes.
- Convergent oceanic–continental/oceanic–oceanic: denser oceanic plate may subduct; trench, earthquakes at varied depths, melting-related volcanism/arc.
- Convergent continental–continental: collision, crustal shortening and major mountain building; little/no classic subduction volcanism in the collision zone.
- Transform: lateral motion along faults; earthquakes; crust neither created nor destroyed overall.
- Evidence set: continental-fit pattern, matching fossils/rock belts/past-climate indicators, symmetric magnetic stripes, youngest seafloor near ridges and increasing age away, concentration/depth patterns of earthquakes and volcanoes, modern GPS motion.
- Investigation should compare predictions from theory with maps/data, not merely recite facts.

QUESTION/CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: Earth structure model; three boundary types; convergent subtypes; feature matrix; evidence timeline/data; Australian context.
Classroom View: motion models, boundary-feature matching, seafloor-age/magnetic-stripe interpretation, earthquake-map reasoning.
Homework: annotate cross-sections, infer boundary from data, explain multiple evidence lines, critique claims.
Practice bank (48 later): 8 motion/type; 12 cross-sections/features; 12 evidence/data; 8 global/Australian contexts; 8 investigation/CER.
Test bank (16 later): independent diagrams/maps and novel evidence; minimum 4 evidence-inference items.

MISCONCEPTIONS AND REQUIRED FEEDBACK
- Plates are continents only.
- Continents float directly on liquid magma.
- Every convergent boundary produces identical features.
- Transform motion opens a gap or destroys crust.
- Earthquakes occur only at plate edges (Australia provides counterexample).
- Continental fit alone “proves” the whole theory.
Feedback must link direction, process and predicted feature/evidence.

AC9S8U04
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Describe the key processes of the rock cycle, including the timescales over which they occur, and examine how the properties of sedimentary, igneous and metamorphic rocks reflect their formation and influence their use.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
The rock cycle is a branching, repeating system across variable timescales, not a fixed sequence. Students must connect formation process to observable property and then to use. Mineral identification detail and quantitative radiometric dating are extension only.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact content description above. The Year 8 framing links heat and kinetic energy to rock-cycle change and requires attention to different timescales.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 8 Science, section Y “Rocks”. Exact mappings:
   - Y.1 Introduction to the rock cycle
   - Y.2 Classify rocks as igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic
   - Y.3 Label parts of rock cycle diagrams
   - Y.4 Select parts of rock cycle diagrams
   Interactive evidence: Y.1 explains that rock is constantly changing and that energy flows drive creation, destruction and transformation. Its diagram labels sediment, sedimentary, metamorphic and igneous rock and magma, connected by weathering/erosion, lithification, melting, crystallisation and metamorphism. The sampled multiple-select checks that sedimentary rock can become metamorphic and processes occur underground, while rejecting “rock moves through the cycle only once”. Challenge requires 7 of 9 correct. This directly exposes the linear-cycle misconception.
3. Khan Academy: no stable dedicated Year 8 Australian mapping was located. Khan Earth-science material can supplement weathering, plate processes and geologic time, but this code is recorded as SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for direct Khan skill alignment.
4. Geoscience Australia: Year 7/8 classroom resources include Rocks, Minerals, Geological time and Plate tectonics; its Education Centre program explicitly covers rocks/minerals and the rock cycle. Preferred Australian authority for rock samples, use contexts and geological timescale.
5. USGS/National Park Service public geology resources: support rock classification, plate-driven processes and the principle that textures/compositions record formation conditions.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct resource mapping located in the current search; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Weathering breaks rock down; erosion transports; deposition accumulates sediment; compaction and cementation/lithification form sedimentary rock.
- Cooling/crystallisation of magma/lava forms igneous rock; slower underground cooling generally permits larger crystals, faster surface cooling smaller/glassy textures.
- Heat and pressure without melting alter existing rock to metamorphic rock; directed pressure may form foliation.
- Melting can produce magma; uplift/exposure enables renewed weathering.
- Any rock type can follow multiple pathways given conditions; paths and rates differ.
- Timescales range from rapid eruptions/cooling events to burial, uplift, metamorphism and plate cycling over thousands to millions of years.
- Properties reflect origin: layering/fossils/porosity in many sedimentary rocks; interlocking crystals and durability in many igneous rocks; banding/foliation or recrystallisation in metamorphic rocks.
- Use must be justified by property: granite durability for construction/benchtops; slate cleavage for tiles; limestone workability/raw material; sandstone appearance/porosity with limitations; pumice low density/abrasive uses. Avoid absolute claims because properties vary within rock groups.

QUESTION/CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: non-linear cycle diagram; process glossary; timescale spectrum; formation→property→use case studies; exceptions.
Classroom View: sample observation, pathway sequencing, property testing, design/use recommendation.
Homework: complete/critique diagrams, classify from evidence, compare rates, recommend material with justification.
Practice bank (48 later): 12 cycle processes/pathways; 8 rock classification; 8 textures/properties; 8 timescales; 8 use decisions; 4 model critique.
Test bank (16 later): independent cycle/data/sample contexts, with no recycled practice stems.

MISCONCEPTIONS AND REQUIRED FEEDBACK
- The cycle has one starting point/order.
- Rocks change only when melted.
- Weathering and erosion are identical.
- Metamorphic rock has melted.
- All rocks of one class share identical properties.
- Geological processes all happen slowly.
Feedback must state the missing process/condition and connect formation to the observed property or suitable use.

AC9S8U05
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Classify different types of energy as kinetic or potential and investigate energy transfer and transformations in simple systems.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students classify energy using observable system features, trace transfers between objects and transformations between forms, and investigate simple systems. Quantitative kinetic/potential-energy formulae are useful extension but are not the core demand. Authoring must distinguish an energy store/form from a transfer pathway and must preserve conservation of energy.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; elaborations include roller-coasters/Newton’s cradles and classifying movement, heat/electricity and chemical, elastic or gravitational energy.
2. IXL Australia, Year 8 H “Kinetic and potential energy”: H.1 Identify changes in gravitational potential energy; H.2 Use tables and graphs to identify patterns about kinetic energy; H.3 Explore energy transformations: roller coaster ride; H.4 Explore energy transformations: bike ride. The live H.3 item uses a staged 7-of-9 challenge and a narrative system. Its first evidence question asks which observation better shows kinetic-energy change: the train changing from rest to motion, rather than merely electricity beginning to flow. This verifies evidence-based interpretation, not vocabulary recall. Supporting links explicitly bridge gravitational potential, kinetic patterns and earlier energy-transformation knowledge.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated Middle School Physics: kinetic-energy, potential-energy and conservation-of-energy resources define energy through motion/configuration, use proportional patterns and trace transformations. Strong supplementary conceptual sequence; no direct Australian-code map.
4. U.S. Department of Energy/PhET public education resources support energy-system diagrams, conservation, transfers and transformations through manipulable models.
5. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 8 mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Kinetic energy is associated with motion; thermal energy is microscopic kinetic energy.
- Potential energy is stored because of position/configuration or chemical arrangement: gravitational, elastic, chemical.
- Transfer means energy crosses between objects or parts of a system; transformation means the form/store changes.
- In a roller-coaster, gravitational potential decreases as kinetic increases; friction transfers energy to thermal stores and sound.
- In a battery–lamp system, chemical energy becomes electrical transfer, then light and thermal energy.
- “Wasted” energy is dissipated to less useful surroundings, not destroyed.
- Investigations should define a system boundary, identify variables/evidence, and compare predicted with observed changes.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: classification matrix; stores versus pathways; system-boundary diagram; roller-coaster, bicycle and electrical examples; conservation account.
Classroom View: card sort, Sankey/flow representations, evidence discussion and a ramp investigation.
Homework: classify, trace, draw flow diagrams, interpret tables/graphs, correct “energy used up”.
Practice 48: 10 classification; 10 transfer/transformation; 10 system diagrams; 8 data/evidence; 6 conservation/dissipation; 4 investigation.
Test 16: independent everyday systems, at least 5 diagram/data items and no repeated practice stems.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Energy is a material; energy disappears; stationary objects have no energy; electricity is stored in wires; heat is always “waste”; transfer and transformation mean the same. Feedback must identify the initial store, pathway, final stores and surroundings.

AC9S8U06
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Classify matter as elements, compounds or mixtures and compare different representations of these, including 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional models, symbols for elements and formulas for molecules and compounds.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
This is particle-level classification and representation. Students translate among words, symbols/formulae, 2-D particle diagrams and 3-D models. They do not need advanced bonding theory, mole calculations or exhaustive periodic-table trends.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; elaborations include physical/virtual models and Mendeleev’s early periodic table compared with the modern table.
2. IXL Australia Year 8 E “Atoms and molecules”: E.1 What are atoms and chemical elements?; E.2 How are substances represented by chemical formulae and models?; E.3 Identify chemical formulae for ball-and-stick models; E.4 Describe atomic composition of molecules; E.5 Classify elements and compounds using formulae; E.6 Classify elements and compounds using models. E.6 uses three stages beginning with five correct in a row and links model reading to formula classification. IXL directly covers elements/compounds and representations; mixtures require supplementation.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated Middle School Chemistry: Classifying matter includes pure substances/mixtures, elements and compounds. “Compounds and chemical formulas” explains that a compound has a fixed formula representing specific atom types and ratios. Exercises progress from identification to applying particle representations. Supplementary, not ACARA mapped.
4. Royal Society of Chemistry and OpenStax public chemistry materials distinguish pure substances from mixtures and provide particle-diagram and separation contexts.
5. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 8 code mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Element: one type of atom; represented by a chemical symbol.
- Compound: two or more element types chemically bonded in a fixed ratio; represented by a formula.
- Mixture: two or more substances physically combined in variable proportions; components retain identity and may be separated physically.
- A molecule can be an element (O2) or a compound (H2O); “molecule” is not a fourth classification.
- Formula subscripts show atom ratios/numbers in one represented particle, not macroscopic sample amount.
- Particle diagrams must use colour/shape keys consistently; touching circles imply bonding only if the key/model convention says so.
- Models highlight selected features and have limitations: scale, spacing, motion, electron structure and three-dimensional shape.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: classification decision tree; symbol/formula/model translations; mixture particle diagrams; model limitations; Mendeleev evidence as extension.
Classroom View: sort samples, build models, gallery critique and translate across representations.
Homework: classify familiar/unfamiliar samples, count atoms, write/interpret formulae, compare 2-D/3-D models, choose separation evidence.
Practice 48: 8 definitions; 10 particle diagrams; 10 symbols/formulae; 8 atom counting; 8 translation/model limits; 4 mixture/separation reasoning.
Test 16: balanced novel diagrams and representations, including molecule-versus-compound traps.

MISCONCEPTIONS
All molecules are compounds; mixtures have chemical formulae; a compound keeps component properties; subscripts are coefficients; differently coloured atoms automatically mean a mixture. Feedback must identify atom types, bonding groups and whether more than one particle type is present.

AC9S8U07
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Compare physical and chemical changes and identify indicators of energy change in chemical reactions.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students decide whether new substances formed using multiple observations and particle reasoning, and identify energy changes. “Reversible means physical” and any single indicator as proof are unsafe rules. Detailed thermochemistry and equation balancing are outside the core.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; elaborations include gas/solid production, colour and temperature change, and comparing properties before/after interaction.
2. IXL Australia Year 8 F “Chemical reactions”: F.1 Identify reactants and products; F.2 Count atoms and molecules in reactions; F.3 Calculate amounts in reactions; F.4 Describe energy changes in chemical reactions; F.5 Compare physical and chemical changes; plus applied materials explorations. In the live F.5 stage-one item, learners compare tarnish removal from a penny with pizza rotting and select that both are chemical changes, rejecting heating/cooling and “only physical” distractors. The skill requires five consecutive correct answers then advances to a second stage. Supporting prerequisites connect changes of state and conservation of matter.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated Middle School Chemistry Unit 3: “Chemical changes”, “Learn and try: Chemical properties and changes”, “Understand” and “Apply” exercises. It identifies colour/odour change, gas, precipitate, heat or light as possible evidence and separates observation from conclusion.
4. Royal Society of Chemistry classroom resources provide age-appropriate demonstrations and emphasise that evidence must support formation of new substances.
5. OpenStax Chemistry provides accurate distinctions among physical properties/changes, chemical properties/reactions and energy exchange.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct mapping found; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Physical change alters state, size, shape or distribution without changing substance identity.
- Chemical change rearranges atoms into new substances with different properties.
- Possible indicators: unexpected gas, precipitate, sustained colour/odour change, light, or temperature change not explained by external heating/cooling.
- Indicators are evidence, not individually conclusive; boiling produces gas without a chemical reaction.
- Exothermic reactions transfer energy to surroundings; endothermic reactions take in energy from surroundings.
- Reactants are starting substances; products are newly formed substances.
- Mass is conserved in a closed system even if gas forms.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: physical/chemical comparison; particle before/after models; evidence hierarchy; energy profiles at qualitative level; investigation examples.
Classroom View: observation stations, claim–evidence–reasoning, closed/open mass demonstration.
Homework: classify and justify, interpret results tables, distinguish misleading indicators, plan a safe investigation.
Practice 48: 10 classification; 10 indicators/evidence; 8 particle models; 8 energy change; 6 reactant/product; 6 investigation/conservation.
Test 16: novel everyday/lab cases with multiple evidence items and explanation-focused distractors.

MISCONCEPTIONS
All colour changes are chemical; bubbles always mean reaction; dissolving always forms a new substance; irreversible always means chemical; mass vanishes with gas; exothermic means “contains heat”. Feedback must compare identities/properties before and after and explain alternative causes for observations.

AC9S8H01
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain how new evidence or different perspectives can lead to changes in scientific knowledge.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
This Nature of Science code requires a causal explanation: prior knowledge/model, new evidence or perspective, evaluation by a scientific community, and the resulting revision. “Science changes because opinions change” is inadequate. Examples should connect to Year 8 understanding such as cell theory, plate tectonics, microscopy, medical imaging or materials.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; repository-aligned elaborations identify improved microscopes/medical imaging and advances in repair or replacement of organs using synthetic materials.
2. IXL has no direct Science-as-a-Human-Endeavour mapping. Relevant partial evidence comes from A.1 The process of scientific inquiry, experimental-design/data skills, P.1 Understanding cells, and “Evidence of continental drift and plate tectonics”. The latter’s live progression distinguishes observation from inference and shows how Wegener’s proposal developed from repeated observations before later seafloor and magnetic evidence strengthened modern plate tectonics. Record IXL PARTIAL-MAPPING.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated/public: “Cell theory” shows accumulating microscope observations from Hooke, van Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann, Remak and Virchow leading to revised claims about cells. Plate-tectonics resources similarly separate early continental drift from later evidence. Khan provides strong case-study evidence but no direct ACARA mapping.
4. Australian Academy of Science education resources promote evidence-based science learning and provide Australian scientist/history contexts.
5. Geoscience Australia and USGS plate-tectonics histories supply a concrete sequence: continental fit/fossils and rock belts; later seafloor age and magnetic symmetry; earthquake/volcano patterns; direct GPS measurements.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct code mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Scientific knowledge is durable but revisable because conclusions are evidence-based.
- New technology can reveal previously unobservable data; new methods can improve precision or repeatability.
- A different perspective is valuable when it asks new questions or offers a model that explains more evidence, not because all opinions are equally valid.
- Revision can refine, extend or replace a model; an earlier model may remain useful within limits.
- Acceptance depends on converging evidence, reproducibility, peer scrutiny and explanatory/predictive power.
- Case study structure: old idea → limitation/anomaly → new evidence/perspective → testing/debate → revised knowledge.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: how knowledge changes; evidence-versus-opinion; model revision flow; cell-theory and plate-tectonics case studies; technology role.
Classroom View: evidence timeline, competing-model comparison and CER discussion.
Homework: reconstruct a case history, rank evidence, explain model limits, respond to “science cannot be trusted because it changes”.
Practice 48: 8 evidence concepts; 10 technology; 12 historical case studies; 8 perspectives/model limits; 6 peer scrutiny; 4 novel applications.
Test 16: independent case evidence and transfer, with at least 6 causal explanation items.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Scientific theories are guesses; one experiment overturns a theory; changing knowledge shows failure; every viewpoint has equal scientific weight; technology alone produces conclusions. Feedback must name the evidence standard and show why the revised account explains/predicts more.

AC9S8H02
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Investigate how cultural perspectives and world views influence the development of scientific knowledge.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students investigate influence rather than treating culture and science as opposites. They need evidence-based cases showing how questions, participation, methods, interpretation, priorities and recognition can be shaped by social and cultural contexts. Cultural knowledge must be represented respectfully, locally and with attention to permissions; it must not be generalised as one uniform “Indigenous view”.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. Curriculum elaborations include how historical views about women placed scientists in subordinate roles or removed them from histories, and how cultural building techniques such as bamboo construction informed structures and materials better able to respond to earthquakes.
2. IXL Australia: no direct Year 8 Science-as-a-Human-Endeavour skill mapping. Relevant but limited contexts include science-literacy passages, engineering-design evaluation and evidence/history in continental drift. Record IXL SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for direct coverage.

AC9S8H03
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
The task is multi-criteria examination, not choosing a universally “correct” technology. AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE

- Environmental: resources, emissions, biodiversity, waste, reversibility and life-cycle effects.

AC9S8I01
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Develop investigable questions, reasoned predictions and hypotheses to explore scientific models, identify patterns and test relationships.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students distinguish broad topics from investigable questions, and predictions from hypotheses. Questions need measurable variables and a feasible method. Hypotheses are testable explanations, not guesses or restated predictions. Correlation should not be asserted as causation without a suitable design.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; elaborations address causal relationships versus correlation and questions such as how salt concentration affects plant cells.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 8 B “Designing experiments”: B.3 Identify the experimental question; B.4 Identify questions that can be investigated with a set of materials; plus B.1 control/experimental groups and B.2 independent/dependent variables as prerequisites. Live B.3 evidence: a six-pot tomato-seed study holds temperature, light and humidity constant, changes fertiliser and measures seedlings after two weeks. The learner chooses the question about fertiliser versus no fertiliser, rejecting humidity because humidity was not varied. The skill begins with four consecutive correct answers and has three stages. This verifies alignment among question, changed variable and measured outcome.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated “Controlled experiments”: observation leads to an investigable question, testable hypothesis and conditional prediction; controlled experiments vary one factor while keeping others constant. Khan also explains that some hypotheses require observational pattern tests for ethical/practical reasons and gives Australian coral-bleaching cases.
4. Australian Academy of Science education resources and CSIRO school investigations support question formation in authentic Australian contexts.
5. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 8 code mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Investigable question template: How does [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] under defined conditions?
- Prediction states an expected result; a reasoned prediction includes scientific reasoning.
- Hypothesis offers a testable explanatory relationship and yields a prediction.
- Variables must be measurable or categorisable with an observable outcome.
- Range, comparison/control, practical resources, time, safety and ethics determine feasibility.
- Correlation is co-variation; causation requires ruling out plausible alternatives through design/evidence.
- Model questions should identify what aspect or prediction of the model can be checked.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: question quality ladder; prediction/hypothesis comparison; variable alignment; causation/correlation; worked rewrites.
Classroom View: improve-question routine, variable cards, prediction justification and design critique.
Homework: rewrite questions, identify variables, develop hypotheses/predictions, judge feasibility and causation claims.
Practice 48: 12 investigable/non-investigable; 10 variable alignment; 10 prediction/hypothesis; 8 model/pattern; 8 correlation/causation.
Test 16: novel contexts with at least 6 construction/selection items requiring justification.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Any question is investigable; hypothesis equals prediction; hypothesis must be correct; two changing variables prove causation; a fair test always requires a laboratory. Feedback must show the missing variable, measure, comparison or explanatory link.

AC9S8I02
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Plan and conduct reproducible investigations to answer questions and test hypotheses, including identifying variables and assumptions and, as appropriate, recognising and managing risks, considering ethical issues and recognising key considerations regarding heritage sites and artefacts on Country/Place.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
A valid plan must align question, hypothesis, variables, measurements, controls, repeats and analysis. Reproducible means another team can follow the description and obtain comparable data, not necessarily identical results. Risk, ethics and Country/Place considerations must be specific to context.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above; elaborations include controlled causal investigations and identifying assumptions in analogue models such as dialysis tubing modelling plant-cell membranes.
2. IXL Australia authenticated mappings: B.1 Identify control and experimental groups; B.2 Identify independent and dependent variables; B.5 Understand an experimental protocol about plant growth; B.6 diffusion; B.7 evaporation; A.2 laboratory tools; A.3 laboratory safety. Live B.5 evidence presents a complete materials/procedure/data-table protocol: ten tomato plants, equal pots/soil/water/location, earthworms added to five pots, and tomato yield measured after three months. The first task selects materials actually required; follow-on structure tests groups, variables, procedures and tables. Challenge requires 6 of 8 correct, with prerequisite links to variables, groups and investigable questions.
3. Khan Academy authenticated “Controlled experiments”: control group provides baseline; independent variable is deliberately changed; dependent variable is measured; large samples and repetition reduce random-variation risk. It also explicitly recognises that ethical/practical limits may require observational testing. “The scientific method” stresses testability, controlling alternatives and reproducibility.
4. Australian Academy of Science/CSIRO classroom investigations support risk-managed school methods. AIATSIS guidance is the key authority when research involves Country/Place, heritage, community knowledge or artefacts.
5. ABC Education search: no stable direct code mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Method specifies materials quantities, sequence, duration, measurement technique/units and data-recording plan.
- Independent variable: deliberately changed; dependent: measured response; controlled: kept consistent; control/comparison provides baseline.
- Use sufficient range, repeats/sample size and consistent timing; randomise where appropriate.
- Identify assumptions, especially how a model represents and differs from the target system.
- Risk management: hazard → possible harm → likelihood/severity → control; PPE is one control, not the whole plan.
- Ethics: minimise harm, voluntary informed participation where relevant, privacy and fair treatment.
- Country/Place: seek relevant permissions, avoid disturbance/removal, follow Traditional Owner/site protocols and record provenance respectfully.
- Reproducibility requires operational definitions, not vague instructions such as “add some” or “measure growth”.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: planning flow; variable/control table; reproducibility checklist; model assumptions; risk/ethics/Country decision guides; full worked protocol.
Classroom View: repair a flawed method, mini risk assessment, Country/Place scenario and peer reproducibility check.
Homework: complete plans, identify omissions, rewrite vague steps, analyse ethics/heritage contexts.
Practice 48: 8 variables/groups; 12 reproducible methods; 8 repeats/data tables; 8 risk; 6 ethics; 6 model/Country considerations.
Test 16: independent investigation scenarios with at least 4 risk/ethics/Country items across the test bank.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Control variable equals control group; reproducible means identical numbers; more trials fix systematic bias; “be careful” is risk control; public artefacts may be sampled; analogue models have no assumptions. Feedback must name the flaw, its effect and a targeted correction.
