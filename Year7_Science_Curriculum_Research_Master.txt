AC9S7U01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Explain why classification is useful, order biodiversity using observable/shared characteristics, use an existing dichotomous key and develop a valid key.
- Required boundary: kingdom/taxonomy recall is only supporting knowledge. The descriptor explicitly requires classification tools and student-created dichotomous keys.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed against the repository’s current Year 7 Science curriculum alignment.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - N.1 “Describe, classify and compare kingdoms” presents an organism image and short biological description, then asks about kingdom-defining traits.
  - Sampled item used Daphnia pulex, identified it as an animal and asked whether it is multicellular. This tests extracting evidence and connecting it to kingdom criteria.
  - Closest IXL skills: N.1 kingdoms; N.2 common/scientific names; N.3 origins of scientific names; N.4 use scientific names to classify organisms.
  - IXL does not supply an obvious Year 7 dichotomous-key skill: this crucial ACARA component must be authored independently.
- Khan Academy:
  - “Systems of Classification” surveys changes in biological classification.
  - “Taxonomy and the tree of life” covers Linnaean categories and scientific naming.
  - Both are supplementary and broader/older than the exact Australian Year 7 tool-use requirement.
- ABC Education, “Science Years 7–8 with Diana: Classification and constructing dichotomous keys”:
  - Explains classification as logical organisation by characteristics; defines taxonomy and dichotomous as “split into two”; models a branching key using observable pasta properties; asks learners to construct their own.
  - Strong age-matched source for the exact use-and-develop requirement.
- Additional trusted-source principle: modern classifications may change when new structural or genetic evidence appears. Avoid presenting a single kingdom scheme as permanently fixed.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Core sequence: purpose of classification -> observable features -> nested groups -> scientific names -> read a paired-choice key -> construct and test a key.
- Key design rules: two mutually exclusive choices at each step; same type of comparison; observable/unambiguous wording; each choice leads to another pair or one identification; every organism resolves exactly once.
- Practice coverage: classify from evidence; identify useful/useless traits; follow text and flow-chart keys; repair overlapping choices; choose the next couplet; construct a short key; compare alternative valid keys.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar Australian organisms, a complete key, a flawed key, a small dataset and an independent key-construction task.
- Misconceptions: keys are guesses; colour is always reliable; a dichotomous key must begin with kingdom; scientific names describe common names; there is only one correct key order.
- Feedback must identify the specific choice that is ambiguous or non-exclusive and show how to rewrite it.

AC9S7U02 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Use food-web and other ecosystem models to represent both matter and energy flow and predict population responses to changed abiotic or biotic factors.
- Required boundary: arrows must be interpreted as transfer from food/source to consumer; predictions require tracing direct and indirect effects rather than memorising organism roles.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - V.3 “Interpret food webs II” explicitly states that food webs model how eaten matter moves through an ecosystem.
  - Sampled tundra item asked which organisms contain matter that was once part of lichen, requiring students to trace one or more feeding pathways through the web.
  - Closest IXL skills: V.1 matter in food chains; V.2/V.3 interpret food webs; V.4 predict population changes; U.1–U.3 ecosystem descriptions; V.6 succession.
  - IXL provides strong pathway and prediction formats; authoring must explicitly distinguish matter cycling from one-way energy transfer.
- Khan Academy, “Matter and energy in food webs”:
  - Food webs model transfer among producers, consumers and decomposers.
  - Useful for trophic role, transfer and decomposer sequencing; supplementary rather than AC9-mapped.
- Khan Academy “Energy flow in food chains & webs” adds trophic-level/energy-flow support, but examples must be simplified to Australian Year 7 depth.
- Trusted public-source direction: CSIRO/ABC Australian ecosystem cases are appropriate for changing rainfall, temperature, invasive species, fire regime or predator abundance. Use simplified datasets and avoid claiming certainty when multiple factors change.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Teach: producer captures energy; consumers obtain matter/energy by feeding; decomposers return matter; energy dissipates and is not recycled like matter.
- Abiotic factors: light, water, salinity, temperature, soil and disturbance. Biotic factors: predation, competition, disease, food availability and introduced species.
- Prediction method: identify changed factor -> find directly affected population -> trace food-web arrows -> predict secondary effects -> qualify if competing paths exist.
- Practice coverage: arrow direction; roles; trace matter; trace energy; build food chain from a web; predict single-factor change; compare direct/indirect effects; state model limitation.
- Test coverage: novel Australian food web, changed abiotic factor, removal/addition of a species, competing prediction evidence and a model critique.
- Misconceptions: arrows show “who chases whom”; matter and energy both recycle identically; all predators rise when prey falls; one diagram proves exact population sizes.
- Feedback must trace the relevant pathway explicitly and use “likely” for model-based population predictions.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7U03 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Model cyclic relative positions of Earth, Sun and Moon and explain eclipses, seasons and tides.
- Required boundary: the descriptor combines spatial modelling with causal explanation. Lunar phases may support positional reasoning, but they must not displace eclipses, seasons and tides.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - DD.5 “What causes the seasons on Earth?” distinguishes Earth’s 24-hour rotation from annual revolution and presents a labelled model with opposite seasons in the hemispheres.
  - Sampled task required identifying poles, hemispheres and equator on a seasonal model.
  - Closest IXL skills: DD.1 Earth–Sun–Moon model analysis; DD.2 lunar phases; DD.3 solar eclipses; DD.4 lunar eclipses; DD.5 seasons.
  - IXL has strong diagram-labelling and alignment tasks by itself. The Year 7 list has no clear tides skill, so tidal modelling must be authored from trusted sources.
- Khan Academy: searches did not surface one single lesson covering the complete Australian descriptor; relevant astronomy explanations are supplementary and not AC9-mapped.
- Trusted-source direction: NASA education material is authoritative for orbital geometry, shadow alignment and scale/model limitations; Australian sources are needed for southern-hemisphere seasonal framing. Tides should be taught as predominantly lunar gravitational effects, modified by Sun–Moon alignment.
- ABC Education Year 5–6 Solar System material provides a useful prerequisite model/scale warning: a model may represent relative size while not representing relative distance accurately.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Seasons: axial tilt plus revolution changes sunlight angle and day length; not distance from the Sun.
- Eclipses: new-moon alignment can produce a solar eclipse; full-moon alignment can produce a lunar eclipse; orbital tilt explains why eclipses do not occur every month.
- Tides: Moon’s gravity produces tidal bulges; Earth’s rotation carries locations through tides; aligned Sun/Moon strengthens spring tides, right-angle alignment produces neap tides.
- Practice coverage: identify relative positions; match cause to phenomenon; label shadow regions; compare seasonal sunlight; interpret a tide diagram/table; critique model scale.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar orientations, southern-hemisphere seasons, eclipse vs phase confusion, spring/neap tide alignment and an explicit model-limit question.
- Misconceptions: phases are Earth’s shadow; eclipses occur monthly; seasons result from distance; high tide occurs only on the side facing the Moon; Moon produces its own light.
- Feedback must name the positional relationship causing the phenomenon.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7U04 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Investigate and represent balanced/unbalanced forces including gravity, then relate motion change to mass and force magnitude/direction.
- Required boundary: balanced force does not mean “no forces”; it means vector sum/net force is zero. Motion can remain constant while forces are balanced.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - H.4 “Balanced and unbalanced forces” presents opposing force magnitudes and asks whether net force is zero.
  - Sampled context: a dog pulled a leash forward with 250 N while a person pulled backward with 180 N; because forces differ, the forces are unbalanced and net force acts forward.
  - Closest IXL skills: H.1 identify acceleration; H.2 mass–force–acceleration; H.3 Newton’s third law; H.4 balanced/unbalanced forces; I.1 gravitational potential energy.
  - IXL provides clear numerical/opposing-force stems; authored work must add force-arrow diagrams, investigations and gravity/mass reasoning.
- Khan Academy:
  - “Unbalanced forces and motion” links non-zero net force to acceleration in its direction.
  - Newton’s second-law resources reinforce that acceleration increases with net force and decreases with mass, but formula-heavy senior variants must not set the Year 7 level.
- Trusted-source direction: use recognised physics education treatments of free-body/force diagrams and net force. Keep gravity as a downward field force and distinguish mass (amount of matter, kg) from weight/gravitational force (N).

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Represent every relevant force with arrow direction and relative magnitude; calculate one-dimensional net force only where appropriate.
- Motion reasoning: zero net force -> rest or constant velocity; non-zero net force -> speed and/or direction changes. Greater net force gives greater acceleration for the same mass; greater mass gives less acceleration for the same net force.
- Investigation contexts: trolley/push, friction and surface, parachute/drag, hanging object, tug-of-war and falling object.
- Practice coverage: identify forces; draw/select arrow diagrams; calculate net force; decide balanced/unbalanced; predict motion change; compare effect of mass; separate mass and weight.
- Test coverage: moving object with balanced forces, two-axis force diagram, changed force/mass scenario, gravity plus support force and evidence from a simple investigation.
- Misconceptions: moving objects must have forward net force; heavier objects always fall faster; action–reaction forces cancel on one object; gravity disappears in space; balanced means no forces.
- Feedback must state the net force direction and connect it to acceleration rather than to motion direction alone.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7U05 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Use particle theory to describe particle arrangement, motion and attraction in substances and explain properties including melting point, compressibility, density and diffusion.
- Required boundary: the particles themselves do not expand, melt or become hot; changes arise from spacing, motion and interactions among particles.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed against the current Year 7 Science alignment.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - K.2 “Particle motion and changes of state” explicitly contrasts particle behaviour in solids, liquids and gases.
  - Sampled task required matching each state to a diagram after establishing: solid particles tightly packed; liquid particles close but sliding; gas particles far apart and moving quickly.
  - Closest IXL skills: K.1 particle motion and temperature; K.2 state changes; K.3 gas pressure; K.4 temperature/pressure effects; M.2 diffusion across membranes.
  - IXL is strong for diagram interpretation but only partial for attraction, density, compressibility and melting-point explanations.
- Khan Academy:
  - Diffusion resources define net molecular movement from higher to lower concentration.
  - General states-of-matter material is supplementary and must be translated to the exact Year 7 particle-model outcomes rather than copied.
- Additional trusted-source direction: use recognised chemistry education particle diagrams and demonstrations, while flagging model limitations (not to scale; 2D; particles are always moving above absolute zero).

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Solid: close, ordered positions, vibration, strong attractions; fixed shape/volume, hard to compress.
- Liquid: close, disordered, slide past; fixed volume but variable shape, low compressibility.
- Gas: widely spaced, rapid random motion, weak attractions; fills container, compressible, low density.
- Heating raises average kinetic energy; melting/boiling overcome attractions without changing particle identity. Cooling reverses the pattern.
- Diffusion is faster at higher temperature and in gases than liquids because particle motion/spacing differ.
- Practice coverage: identify diagrams; explain properties; predict heating/cooling changes; compare density/compressibility; interpret diffusion; find flaws in particle drawings.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar substance/state evidence, before/after diagrams, diffusion investigation data and a model-limitation item.
- Misconceptions: gas particles have no mass; particles grow when heated; empty space contains air particles in the model; particles stop moving in solids; melting changes one substance into another.
- Feedback must connect a macroscopic observation to particle spacing, motion or attraction.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7U06 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Use a particle model to distinguish pure substances from mixtures and apply understanding of substance properties to separate mixtures.
- Required boundary: “pure” means one type of particle/substance, not clean, natural or safe; separation method must be justified by a physical property difference.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - D.1 “Identify mixtures” uses real materials and multiple selection.
  - Sampled item asked for two mixtures among chocolate bar, ice cream, titanium and carbon; the composite foods were mixtures, while titanium and carbon were single elements.
  - Closest IXL skills: D.1 mixtures; M.1 solution concentration; F.5/F.6 element/compound classification from formulae/models; G.5 physical versus chemical changes.
  - IXL does not provide full Year 7 separation-technique selection, so filtration, evaporation/crystallisation, decanting, sieving, magnetism, chromatography and distillation require independent authoring.
- Khan Academy: mixture, solution and particle resources are supplementary; no AC9-mapped Year 7 sequence was identified.
- Trusted-source direction: use school-safe separation demonstrations and evidence-based method selection. Particle diagrams must distinguish identical particles from two or more intermingled particle types.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Pure element or compound: one particle type throughout. Mixture: two or more substances physically combined; components retain properties and proportions can vary.
- Method/property matches: sieve–particle size; magnet–magnetism; filtration–insoluble solid/particle size; decantation–density/settling; evaporation or crystallisation–solubility/boiling; chromatography–different attraction/solubility; distillation–different boiling points.
- Practice coverage: classify particle diagrams and materials; identify solute/solvent/solution; choose and justify a method; sequence multi-step separation; interpret chromatograms; distinguish physical separation from chemical change.
- Test coverage: novel multi-component mixture, method flow chart, apparatus diagram, particle model before/after and a choice between two plausible methods.
- Misconceptions: compounds are mixtures; dissolved material disappears; filtration removes dissolved salt; evaporation recovers both components; pure water means ordinary tap water; any mixture can be separated in one step.
- Feedback must name the relevant property and explain why the chosen method acts on that difference.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7H01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum focus
- Explain how new evidence or different perspectives can lead to changes in scientific knowledge.
- Required boundary: science changes through evidence-based revision; “scientists changed their minds” without evidence, method or perspective is inadequate.

Evidence collected
- ACARA v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia:
  - No direct Year 7 skill maps to changing scientific knowledge: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.
  - N.1–N.4 classification and scientific naming can provide a supporting case where categories change as new structural/genetic evidence becomes available, but their items assess classification rather than the nature of science.
- Khan Academy:
  - “Systems of Classification” describes historical changes in biological classification, providing a suitable supplementary case study.
  - Khan is not AC9-mapped and should not be treated as the curriculum authority.
- Trusted-source direction: use short evidence timelines such as revised classification, changing Solar System models or improved disease explanations. Compare the earlier evidence/model with later evidence and show exactly what changed.
- CSIRO/peer-reviewed practice supports the core idea that scientific knowledge is provisional but reliable: explanations are retained, refined or replaced according to the quality and consistency of new evidence.

Concept and authoring blueprint
- Separate observation/evidence, interpretation/model and perspective/question. A different perspective can direct attention to different evidence, but does not make every claim equally scientific.
- Revision sequence: existing explanation -> new observation/method/perspective -> conflict or gap -> testing/review -> refined explanation.
- Practice coverage: order an evidence timeline; identify what evidence challenged a claim; distinguish change due to evidence from change due to preference; compare two models; choose a justified revision.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar historical/current case, competing explanations with evidence, and a question about why revision strengthens rather than weakens science.
- Misconceptions: scientific knowledge is just opinion; one new result overturns a theory instantly; old knowledge becomes useless; scientists must never disagree; accepted knowledge can never change.
- Feedback must state the evidence–revision link explicitly and acknowledge uncertainty appropriately.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

- Test coverage: unfamiliar public case study, stakeholder/knowledge-governance choice, evidence integration and an ethical communication decision.

AC9S7H03 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum descriptor
- Examine how proposed scientific responses to contemporary issues may impact on society and explore ethical, environmental, social and economic considerations.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- Students must examine a proposed science-informed response, not merely describe the underlying issue or technology.
- Evaluation must integrate evidence with four distinct lenses: ethical, environmental, social and economic. A defensible conclusion may be conditional and should acknowledge trade-offs, affected groups, uncertainty and unintended effects.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor confirmed. ACARA supporting material connects this code with bioprospecting, medicines/agriculture, Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property, free prior and informed consent, benefit-sharing and risks of biopiracy.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - X.1 “Evaluate claims about natural resource use: fossil fuels” begins with evidence interpretation. A sampled graph item asked learners to choose the statement supported by global energy-use data from 1965–2015; the supported claim was that use increased in most years.
  - A subsequent item linked the fossil-fuel response context to non-renewability by contrasting formation over millions of years with the rate of human use.
  - W.1/W.2 coral-reef problem/solution skills are additional partial mappings. IXL is strong for evidence and environmental consequences but does not require a full four-lens impact analysis: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- Khan Academy:
  - No direct Australian Curriculum mapping identified. Climate, conservation and resource-use materials can supply supplementary science background, but cannot replace the explicit social, ethical and economic analysis.
- ABC Education:
  - Relevant Australian contemporary-issue reporting can provide accessible stimulus cases. Treat reports as secondary stimuli: extract the scientific claim, evidence, proposed response, affected groups and stated uncertainties rather than accepting authority alone.
- Additional authoritative source:
  - ACARA’s bioprospecting elaboration provides a model case: traditional knowledge can help develop useful products, while ethical evaluation must address knowledge ownership, consent, attribution, control and equitable benefit-sharing.
  - AIATSIS ethical guidance reinforces respect, Indigenous self-determination and leadership, impact/value, sustainability and accountability.

Authoring blueprint
- Reusable analysis frame: issue -> proposed response -> scientific mechanism/evidence -> intended benefit -> four-lens consequences -> stakeholders -> uncertainty -> justified recommendation.
- Ethical lens: rights, consent, fairness, ownership, distribution of risks/benefits and duties to future generations.
- Environmental lens: resource use, emissions, biodiversity, waste, reversibility and time scale.
- Social lens: health, access, equity, culture, employment, public acceptance and community disruption.
- Economic lens: implementation/maintenance costs, affordability, employment, opportunity cost, long-term savings and who pays/benefits.
- Suitable cases: fossil-fuel transition, reef protection, invasive-species control, vaccination, drought responses, plastics, bioprospecting and renewable energy. Cases involving First Nations knowledge require authorised public sources and precise protocols.
- Practice coverage: distinguish issue/response; classify impacts by lens; interpret evidence; identify stakeholders; compare two options; detect omitted costs; weigh short/long-term effects; write a conditional recommendation.
- Test coverage: novel Australian case, data-plus-stakeholder evidence, competing solutions, at least one cross-lens trade-off and an independent justified conclusion.
- Misconceptions: “scientific” means automatically ethical; lowest cost is always best; every stakeholder is affected equally; one benefit proves the response should proceed; ethical and social considerations are interchangeable.
- Feedback must name the evidence used, the lens applied and the trade-off or missing stakeholder.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7H04 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum descriptor
- Explore the role of science communication in informing individual viewpoints and community policies and regulations.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- The outcome is about how communication informs viewpoints and collective decisions. It is not simply “make a poster” or recall a regulation.
- Students should evaluate message, evidence, source, audience, medium, uncertainty and possible influence while recognising that values, costs and law also shape policy.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia:
  - No Year 7 skill directly assesses science communication, viewpoints, policy or regulation: SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.
  - X.1 “Evaluate claims about natural resource use: fossil fuels” is a useful partial prerequisite. Interactive sampling showed learners selecting a claim supported by a labelled data source and then using explanatory information about non-renewability.
  - W.2 coral-reef solution evaluation can supply a policy-like context, but authored tasks must add audience, communication form and policy consequences.
- Khan Academy:
  - No direct AC9 mapping. Its scientific-method guidance models a key communication norm: distinguish observation, testable explanation, prediction, result and revision; results support or contradict rather than absolutely prove a hypothesis.
- ABC Education:
  - Australian science reporting is appropriate for comparing how a news story, expert explanation, infographic or interview frames evidence for a public audience. Students should separate quoted evidence from headline/framing and note uncertainty.
- Additional authoritative source:
  - CSIRO communication and public-facing research material provides models of evidence explanation, qualifications and implications. Use primary agency material alongside media treatment so learners can compare source purpose and audience.
  - Community policy examples may include water restrictions, fire warnings, vaccination rules, fishing limits, biosecurity or pollution regulation.

Authoring blueprint
- Communication chain: research/evidence -> communicator and purpose -> message/representation -> audience interpretation -> viewpoint/discussion -> policy or regulation, with feedback and competing influences.
- Reliability checks: expertise/provenance, currency, method/data visibility, corroboration, uncertainty, distinction between correlation and causation, and separation of evidence from persuasion.
- Explain policy versus regulation: policy is an agreed course or framework; regulation is an enforceable rule. Scientific evidence can inform both but does not solely determine either.
- Practice coverage: identify claim/evidence; match format to audience; compare headline and source; interpret graph uncertainty; spot exaggeration; revise misleading language; trace possible policy influence; distinguish policy/regulation.
- Test coverage: two communications about one issue, audience/purpose analysis, an evidence-quality decision, a viewpoint consequence and a justified policy recommendation.
- Misconceptions: popularity equals reliability; all expert disagreement invalidates science; a single graph proves causation; policy follows automatically from evidence; simplifying a message must mean making it inaccurate.
- Feedback must state which communication feature affects trust or interpretation and how it could alter a viewpoint or decision.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7I01 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum descriptor
- Develop investigable questions, reasoned predictions and hypotheses to explore scientific models, identify patterns and test relationships.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- Students must generate, refine and link questions, predictions and hypotheses. A hypothesis is a testable explanatory proposition; a prediction is the expected observation if it is supported.
- Investigations may interrogate a model, seek a pattern or test a relationship between variables.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor confirmed.
- IXL Australia, authenticated interactive sampling:
  - B.4 “Identify questions that can be investigated with a set of materials” presents a scenario, a bounded equipment list and three possible questions.
  - Sampled chocolate-melting item could be investigated only by comparing milk and dark chocolate on a stove; distractors required unavailable white chocolate or a microwave. This makes feasibility and measurable comparison explicit.
  - Closest skills: A.1 scientific inquiry; B.3 identify the experimental question; B.4 feasible investigable questions; B.2 variables. IXL strongly supports selection but must be extended to student-generated hypotheses and model/pattern questions.
- Khan Academy, authenticated:
  - “The scientific method” sequences observation, question, testable hypothesis, prediction, test and iteration.
  - It explicitly distinguishes hypothesis as a possible testable explanation from prediction as an expected outcome, and cautions that supportive results do not conclusively prove a hypothesis.
- ABC Education:
  - Age-appropriate experiment resources provide observable contexts for asking “what happens if…” questions and testing one purposeful change. Authoring should elevate these to explicit Year 7 hypothesis/model/relationship language.
- Additional source principle:
  - Use realistic constraints: measurable dependent variable, ethical and safe method, available equipment, feasible time/range and a question whose evidence could answer it.

Authoring blueprint
- Question form: How does [independent variable] affect [measured dependent variable] when [key controls] are kept constant?
- Hypothesis form: [relationship] because [scientific model/mechanism].
- Prediction form: If [planned change], then [measurable result], because [reason tied to hypothesis].
- Pattern questions may be comparative, correlational or time-series; causal wording is reserved for controlled designs.
- Model exploration asks what observable result the model predicts and which evidence would support, refine or challenge it.
- Practice coverage: investigable/not; refine vague questions; identify IV/DV; write a reasoned prediction; distinguish prediction/hypothesis; connect mechanism; identify expected graph pattern; choose evidence that could test a relationship.
- Test coverage: novel materials constraint, student-generated question, model-based hypothesis, quantitative prediction, pattern interpretation and a non-causal observational case.
- Misconceptions: every question is experimentally testable; hypothesis means guess; prediction and hypothesis are synonyms; one confirming result proves the hypothesis; a correlation automatically tests causation.
- Feedback must identify feasibility/testability and show the logical link question -> hypothesis -> prediction -> evidence.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

Curriculum descriptor
- Plan and conduct reproducible investigations to answer questions and test hypotheses, including identifying variables and assumptions and, as appropriate, recognising and managing risks, considering ethical issues and recognising key considerations regarding heritage sites and artefacts on Country/Place.

Curriculum focus and boundary

  - IXL gives strong variable/protocol evidence but does not adequately cover assumptions, ethics or Country/Place heritage considerations: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- Khan Academy, authenticated:
  - “The scientific method” requires testing a prediction and iterating after results.
  - Its adjacent “Controlled experiments” resource is supplementary for control groups, variable isolation and repeatability. It is not an AC9 or Australian cultural-protocol mapping.
- ABC Education:
  - Australian classroom investigations can provide practical contexts for safe equipment selection, fair tests and repeated measurements. Authored methods must specify quantities, sequence, measurement resolution, repeats and recording structure.
- AIATSIS Code of Ethics:
  - Ethical work involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, knowledge, culture or heritage centres respect, self-determination, Indigenous leadership, impact/value, sustainability and accountability.
  - For school investigations on Country/Place: obtain appropriate permission and guidance; do not enter restricted/significant sites, disturb or remove artefacts, specimens or cultural material; protect sensitive locations and knowledge; attribute authorised information; follow community/Custodian and site requirements.
- Risk-management principle: identify hazard, potential harm and affected person/environment; assess likelihood/consequence; apply a control that reduces exposure; monitor and revise.

Authoring blueprint
- Reproducible plan fields: aim/question; hypothesis; variables; justified range; controls; materials with quantities; numbered method; measurement units/resolution; repeats/sample size; data table; assumptions; risk/ethics/protocol controls; disposal/restoration.
- Distinguish variables: independent deliberately changed; dependent measured; controlled kept consistent; uncontrolled/confounding factors threaten interpretation.
- Assumptions should be explicit and testable where possible (for example, instruments calibrated, samples comparable, background conditions stable).
- Cultural/heritage scenarios must reward stopping and consulting appropriate Traditional Custodians or site authorities, not treating permission as generic or artefacts as collectable data.
- Practice coverage: identify variables; repair an irreproducible method; select repeat count/range; write a risk control; expose an assumption; sequence a protocol; judge ethical consent; respond to a possible artefact/site; design a recording table.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar complete-plan critique, independent plan fragment, variable/confound identification, risk matrix decision, ethics case and Country/Place heritage decision.
- Misconceptions: “be careful” is a sufficient risk control; repeating a measurement removes bias; more variables make an investigation better; assumption means known fact; a publicly accessible site or online image removes cultural permissions; finding an artefact permits handling/removal.
- Feedback must connect each correction to reproducibility, validity, safety, ethics or cultural authority and identify the precise missing detail.

AC9S7I03 — AUTHORING-READY

Curriculum descriptor
- Select and use equipment to generate and record data with precision, using digital tools as appropriate.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- Equipment must be selected for the quantity, expected range, capacity, resolution, safety and setting. “Digital” does not automatically mean accurate or appropriate.
- Students must generate and record data, not only name apparatus. Recorded precision must match instrument resolution and include quantity, unit and uncertainty where appropriate.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor and elaborations confirmed against the current Year 7 Science alignment. Elaborations include choosing thermometers for an expected range, using digital stopwatches/scales for precision, constructing data tables/spreadsheets/graphic organisers, using standard units/conversions, sensors for abiotic factors and identification technologies in fieldwork.
- IXL Australia, authenticated single-tab interactive sampling:
  - A.2 “Identify laboratory tools” presented visual apparatus recognition; sampled task asked learners to select a round-bottom flask from four images.
  - A.3 “Laboratory safety equipment” is a prerequisite mapping.
  - IXL supports apparatus recognition but not enough decision-making about range, resolution, calibration, digital logging or recording conventions: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- Khan Academy, authenticated:
  - “Controlled experiments” identifies observations as experimental data and models systematic collection across control and experimental groups.
  - It supports variables, replication and data generation but is not an equipment-selection or precision lesson and has no AC9 mapping.
- Additional trusted sources:
  - Australian measurement principles require traceable units, suitable instruments and attention to measurement uncertainty.
  - Science Buddies data guidance reinforces labelled tables, units, repeated trials, consistent units and using spreadsheets to retain and process raw data.
- ABC Education: relevant classroom investigations are suitable Australian contexts for tool choice and data collection; no single direct code mapping was identified.

Authoring blueprint
- Selection reasoning: quantity -> expected range/capacity -> smallest useful division/resolution -> safety/material compatibility -> field/lab constraints.
- Precision is repeatability/detail; accuracy is closeness to an accepted/reference value. Calibration, zero error, parallax, reaction time, sensor placement and sampling rate can affect results.
- Digital tools: stopwatches, balances, temperature/light/pH/soil-moisture probes, data loggers, spreadsheets and field-identification apps. Require setup, calibration, battery/sampling checks and verification of automated outputs.
- Recording standards: raw values retained; headings use quantity and unit; consistent decimal places justified by the instrument; observations distinguished from measurements; missing/anomalous readings flagged, not silently altered.
- Practice coverage: identify apparatus; compare instruments by range/resolution; read analogue scales; choose digital sensor/sampling interval; convert units; detect false precision; build a raw-data table; verify an app identification.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar equipment specifications, a field sensor scenario, an analogue reading, digital-logging limitation, table correction and a justified equipment choice.
- Misconceptions: more decimal places mean greater accuracy; largest-capacity instrument is safest/best; digital readings cannot be wrong; precision and accuracy are synonyms; units belong in every data cell rather than the heading.
- Feedback must name the selection criterion or measurement error and show the corrected recording convention.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

Curriculum descriptor

- IXL Australia, authenticated single-tab interactive sampling:

  - The item models clear quantity/unit headings and extracting a relationship by controlled comparison.
  - Other partial mappings: V.1–V.4 food chains/webs, N classification tools, CC climate data, DD Earth–Sun–Moon models. No single IXL skill spans the descriptor: PARTIAL-MAPPING.

- Additional trusted sources:

  - It also emphasises that graphs are analytical tools, not only presentation products.
- ABC Education/CSIRO: Australian ecosystem, astronomical and environmental datasets provide suitable contexts; source and model limitations must be retained.

Authoring blueprint
- Representation-purpose matches: raw table for exact data; column graph for categorical comparison; line graph for continuous ordered change; scatter plot for relationship between paired numerical variables; food web for transfers; dichotomous key for identification; timeline for sequence; equation/rule for quantified relationship.

- Models select relevant features and omit others; students must name at least one limitation.
- Practice coverage: choose representation; repair labels/scales; construct table/graph; translate table-to-graph; build food web/key/timeline; use spreadsheet formula; interpret equation; critique model.
- Test coverage: novel mixed datasets, one constructed graph/table, a non-graph representation, mathematical relationship, misleading display and model-limitation item.
- Misconceptions: any graph type works; line graphs suit all data; joining points proves continuity; models are exact copies; changing scale changes the data; spreadsheet output is automatically valid.
- Feedback must link representation choice to variable type and intended comparison.

AC9S7I05 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum descriptor
- Analyse data and information to describe patterns, trends and relationships and identify anomalies.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- Students move beyond reading individual values to evidence-based descriptions of overall behaviour, relationships and exceptions.
- They should quantify where possible, distinguish pattern/trend/relationship, identify anomalies without automatically deleting them, and avoid causal claims unsupported by design.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor confirmed. Current elaborations include predator–prey cycles, qualitative mass/gravity relationships and anomaly identification.
- IXL Australia, authenticated single-tab interactive sampling:
  - I.2 kinetic-energy task used a controlled table comparison: penguins had masses 6 kg and 12 kg at the same 2 m/s, with kinetic energies 12 J and 24 J. The structure reveals a direct proportional pattern between mass and kinetic energy when speed is fixed.
  - Closest mappings also include V.3/V.4 food-web/population change, CC climate data, E.2 conservation graphs and X.1 resource-use claims.
  - IXL supplies strong structured pattern items, but authored work must add noisy datasets, trend description and anomaly decisions: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- Khan Academy:
  - “Controlled experiments” explains that repeated trials and larger samples reduce the risk of conclusions driven by random variation.
  - Its coral-bleaching example tests a predicted natural pattern against temperature data, showing how models and observations support a relationship without relying on one isolated value.
- Additional trusted sources:
  - Science Buddies recommends reviewing completeness, calculating suitable averages, graphing trials and checking mistakes; it defines outliers as unusual points that may reflect error, data entry, malfunction or a real rare event.
  - Tables and graphs can expose trends and anomalies; anomalies should be investigated against method and raw data before exclusion.
- ABC Education/CSIRO: suitable contexts include predator–prey numbers, rainfall/temperature, tides, dissolving time, plant growth and ecosystem monitoring.

Authoring blueprint
- Pattern: repeated arrangement/regularity; trend: overall direction through ordered data; relationship: how one variable changes with another; anomaly: point inconsistent with the overall pattern.
- Analysis sentence: identify variables -> state direction/form -> support with two or more values/range -> identify anomaly/qualification -> avoid unsupported causation.
- Use mean/range only where appropriate for Year 7; preserve raw repeats so spread is visible.
- Anomaly decision sequence: check transcription/unit -> instrument/method -> repeat/compare -> determine plausible rare event -> retain, flag or justify exclusion.
- Practice coverage: describe increasing/decreasing/cyclic/no trend; compare groups; calculate mean/range; find anomalies; use graph and table; distinguish correlation/causation; predict cautiously; write evidence statement.
- Test coverage: unfamiliar dataset with noise, time trend, paired-variable relationship, repeated trials, anomaly requiring judgment and conflicting representations.
- Misconceptions: one high value is a trend; any unusual point should be removed; averages reveal every feature; correlation proves causation; flat sections mean no measurement variation.
- Feedback must quote/compute the evidence and explain whether the anomaly weakens, changes or merely qualifies the pattern.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.

AC9S7I06 — AUTHORING-READY
================================================================
Curriculum descriptor
- Analyse methods, conclusions and claims for assumptions, possible sources of error, conflicting evidence and unanswered questions.

Curriculum focus and boundary
- Evaluation must connect a method weakness or assumption to its effect on evidence and then to the strength/scope of a conclusion.
- “Human error” and “do more repeats” are inadequate without specifying the error mechanism, direction/variability and targeted improvement.

Evidence collected
- Australian Curriculum v9 descriptor confirmed. Elaborations emphasise data-quality indicators, uncontrolled variables, improvements, conflicting evidence and further questions.
- IXL Australia, authenticated single-tab interactive sampling:
  - B.5 “Understand an experimental protocol about plant growth” supplied a complete materials list, numbered procedure, control/experimental groups, standardised pots/soil/water/location, five replicates per group, three-month duration and results tables.
  - Sampled protocol compared tomato plants without earthworms (pots 1–5) with plants receiving two earthworms (pots 6–10), measuring number of tomatoes.
  - B.6/B.7 diffusion and evaporation protocols provide further method-reading contexts; B.1/B.2 support groups and variables.
  - IXL strongly assesses protocol comprehension but does not fully require assumptions, error effects, conflicting evidence or unanswered questions: PARTIAL-MAPPING.
- Khan Academy, authenticated:
  - “Controlled experiments” explains control baselines, IV/DV, hidden biological variability, sample size and repetition.
  - It notes that ethical/practical limits sometimes require observational pattern tests, and uses coral bleaching to compare model predictions with natural observations.
- Additional trusted sources:
  - Science Buddies data-analysis guidance requires checking completeness, mistakes, repeated trials and graph anomalies.
  - Its conclusion framework explicitly links claim, evidence, reasoning, uncertainties and new questions.
- ABC Education/CSIRO: Australian investigations and public datasets are suitable for comparing school results with secondary evidence; source, sampling and context differences must be stated.

Authoring blueprint
- Evaluation chain: identify assumption/error/conflict -> explain effect on measurement or comparison -> judge claim strength/scope -> propose specific improvement or further question.
- Random errors increase spread and reduce precision; systematic errors shift results in one direction and threaten accuracy. Blunders/data-entry mistakes are checked against records rather than relabelled “random”.
- Method analysis: variable control, fair comparison, sample representativeness, repeats, measurement resolution/calibration, procedure clarity, bias, timing and ethical constraints.
- Conclusion analysis: matches question; supported by actual data; uses cautious language; acknowledges anomaly/conflict; does not generalise beyond sample/range.
- Conflicting evidence may result from method, sample, environmental context, uncertainty or a genuinely incomplete explanation. Compare quality before choosing a side.
- Practice coverage: find assumption; classify error; predict effect; improve method; evaluate conclusion; compare conflicting datasets; identify unanswered question; rewrite overclaim.
- Test coverage: complete unfamiliar investigation, repeated data with anomaly, systematic-error scenario, conflicting external evidence, conclusion critique and justified follow-up question.
- Misconceptions: “human error” explains everything; repeats fix calibration bias; a control group removes all confounders; conflicting evidence means both studies are useless; a supported hypothesis is proven; unanswered questions make current evidence invalid.
- Feedback must use the structure problem -> evidence effect -> specific improvement or bounded conclusion.
Status: AUTHORING-READY.
