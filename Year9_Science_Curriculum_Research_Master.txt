AC9S9U01
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Compare the role of body systems in regulating and coordinating the body’s response to a stimulus, and describe the operation of a negative feedback mechanism.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students compare nervous and endocrine coordination and trace a stimulus-response pathway through receptor, coordination/control centre and effector. They must explain negative feedback as a response that opposes deviation and returns a regulated variable towards a normal range. Detailed neuronal biochemistry and advanced hormone pathways are beyond the code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. The wording requires comparison between systems, coordinated response to internal/external stimuli, and operation—not merely definition—of negative feedback.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: F.1 Organisation in the human body: the heart and circulatory system; F.2 Homeostasis and feedback loops; F.3 Science literacy: phantom pain. Live F.2 opens with Naomi moving between snow and a warm restaurant while internal temperature remains stable. Learners select that organisms have regulatory systems maintaining a stable internal environment and that homeostasis supports cell survival, while rejecting “internal conditions match the outside” and “little energy is required”. Stage 1 requires 6 correct in a row, directly exposing core misconceptions.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated High school biology “Feedback in living systems”: homeostasis maintains internal conditions within acceptable limits; negative feedback detects a change and triggers responses opposing it. The worked thermoregulation example links increased temperature to sweating, vasodilation, thirst and behaviour, and contrasts this with shivering/vasoconstriction when temperature falls. The sequence also includes articles plus Understand and Apply exercises.
4. OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology public resources describe homeostatic control using receptor, control centre and effector and distinguish negative from positive feedback. Useful as the anatomy accuracy reference.
5. MedlinePlus/NIH endocrine resources explain that nervous and endocrine systems communicate, with hormones producing generally slower, longer-lasting responses than rapid nerve signalling.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 9 resource mapping located in the current search; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Stimulus: detectable internal/external change; receptor senses it; control centre compares/integrates information; effector produces response.
- Nervous coordination uses electrical impulses and neurotransmitters, is usually rapid, targeted and shorter-lived.
- Endocrine coordination uses hormones carried in blood, is usually slower, more widespread and longer-lasting; only cells with appropriate receptors respond.
- Systems cooperate rather than work independently: hypothalamus/endocrine signalling, nerves controlling muscles/glands, circulation transporting hormones.
- Negative feedback reduces the original deviation; it does not mean “bad” feedback.
- Thermoregulation: too hot → thermoreceptors/control centre → sweating and skin vasodilation → heat loss; too cold → shivering and vasoconstriction → heat conservation/production.
- Blood-glucose example at this level: high glucose promotes insulin-mediated uptake/storage; low glucose promotes glucagon-mediated release.
- Homeostasis maintains a range around a set point, not one perfectly constant number.
- Positive feedback amplifies change to completion and should be used only as a contrast.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: stimulus-response pathway; nervous/endocrine comparison; feedback-loop anatomy; thermoregulation and blood-glucose worked cases; coordination/model limits.
Classroom View: pathway card sort, rapid-versus-slow response comparison, annotate a feedback diagram and diagnose faulty loops.
Homework: label pathways, compare systems, complete thermoregulation/glucose chains and explain system failure.
Practice 48: 8 stimulus pathways; 10 nervous system; 10 endocrine system; 12 negative-feedback applications; 4 system coordination; 4 model critique.
Test 16: independent physiological contexts with balanced comparison and causal feedback reasoning; no copied practice stems.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Homeostasis matches the environment; negative feedback worsens a change; one system acts alone; hormones affect every cell; feedback stops once the exact set point is reached; all responses are conscious. Feedback must identify the broken causal link and restore the correct direction of response.

AC9S9U02
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Describe the form and function of reproductive cells and organs in animals and plants, and analyse how the processes of sexual and asexual reproduction enable survival of the species.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
The code combines structure-function knowledge in both animals and plants with analysis of reproductive strategies at species level. Students compare sexual and asexual reproduction, fertilisation, dispersal and genetic variation. Human reproductive health detail and full molecular genetics are outside this code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. “Enable survival of the species” requires reasoning about offspring number, speed, variation, dispersal and environmental change rather than claiming reproduction guarantees survival.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: K.1 and K.2 Sexual reproduction and genetic variation; L.1 Angiosperm and conifer life cycles; L.2 Moss and fern life cycles; H.1–H.4 cell division supports prerequisite knowledge. Live K.1 explains genes are inherited, then compares one-parent asexual reproduction with two-parent sexual reproduction. The first table classifies “two parents”, “genetically identical to one parent” and “produces new organisms”; Stage 1 requires 3 of 4 correct and expands across three stages.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated “Asexual and sexual reproduction”: one-parent cloning through binary fission, budding and fragmentation is contrasted with fusion of egg and sperm carrying genetic information from parents. The lesson explicitly links sexual reproduction to greater variation and improved likelihood that some offspring suit changing environments. Its course also provides review articles and two exercises.
4. Khan “Sexual reproduction and genetic variation” adds meiosis/fertilisation and the half-genetic-contribution of gametes. Useful supplementary depth without making meiosis the entire code.
5. OpenStax Biology 2e public resources provide accurate animal and plant reproductive structures, pollination, fertilisation, seed/fruit formation and vegetative propagation.
6. NHGRI’s public gamete definition confirms that gametes are reproductive cells in animals or plants; ova and sperm are animal examples.
7. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 9 mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Gametes are specialised reproductive cells carrying one chromosome set; fertilisation forms a zygote.
- Animal sperm: small, motile in many animals, flagellum and many mitochondria; egg: larger, nutrient-rich cytoplasm and protective layers. Avoid implying every animal gamete has identical form.
- Animal organs produce, transport and support gametes/fertilisation; teach named examples accurately without reducing the code to humans.
- Flower structures: anther produces pollen; pollen carries male gametes; stigma receives pollen; style guides pollen tube; ovary contains ovules; ovule contains female gamete; fertilised ovule becomes seed and ovary may form fruit.
- Sexual reproduction combines genetic material, usually creates variation, and can improve resilience across changing conditions, but costs time/energy and may require mates or pollination.
- Asexual reproduction uses one parent and no fusion of gametes; binary fission, budding, fragmentation/spores in suitable organisms, and vegetative propagation in plants.
- Asexual reproduction can be rapid and preserve successful traits, but low genetic variation may increase shared vulnerability.
- Species survival depends on context; neither strategy is universally superior. Many species use both.
- Pollination is transfer of pollen; fertilisation is fusion of gametes.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: animal and flower structure-function diagrams; gametes/fertilisation; sexual-asexual comparison; strategy trade-offs; plant/animal case studies.
Classroom View: organ-function matching, flower dissection/model, strategy decision scenarios and misconception checks.
Homework: label and explain structures, sequence pollination/fertilisation, compare strategies and analyse unfamiliar organisms.
Practice 48: 10 cells/structures; 10 animal organs; 10 plant organs/processes; 10 sexual-asexual comparison; 8 species-survival applications.
Test 16: novel plant and animal cases with at least 6 structure-function and 6 strategy-analysis items.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Pollen is a seed; pollination equals fertilisation; asexual means no cell division; clones are always perfectly identical in every trait; sexual reproduction always needs two separate organisms; sexual is always better; individuals reproduce “to survive”. Feedback must distinguish process, inherited variation and population-level consequence.

AC9S9U03
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Represent the carbon cycle and examine how key processes including combustion, photosynthesis and respiration rely on interactions between Earth’s spheres (the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere).

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students construct and interpret a carbon-cycle model with stores/reservoirs and fluxes/processes, and explain sphere interactions. The emphasis is conservation and movement of carbon, including altered transfer rates through human activity. Detailed carbon chemistry or quantitative climate modelling is extension only.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. Students must represent the system and explain how named processes link multiple spheres.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: O.3 The carbon cycle: biosphere processes and interactions; O.4 geosphere processes and interactions; G.1 photosynthesis; G.2 cellular respiration; LL.1 Earth spheres; NN.2 greenhouse effect. Live O.3 defines reservoirs and asks learners to match carbon matter to spheres: rocks/fossil fuels to geosphere, dissolved carbonic acid/bicarbonate to hydrosphere, carbon dioxide/methane to atmosphere, and organisms to biosphere. Stage 1 requires 3 of 4 correct and progresses across three stages.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated “The carbon cycle”: plants fix atmospheric carbon through photosynthesis; consumers and organisms release carbon dioxide through metabolism/respiration; oceans absorb carbon and carbonate can become shells/limestone; burial can form fossil fuels; combustion returns stored carbon to the atmosphere. The lesson explicitly treats carbon as recycled rather than created/disappearing.
4. NASA Earth Observatory carbon-cycle resources provide the authoritative Earth-system framing: fast biological and slow geological cycles connect land, ocean, atmosphere and rocks, and human fossil-fuel combustion changes fluxes.
5. CSIRO carbon-cycle and greenhouse-gas research provides Australian context for land/ocean sinks, atmospheric observations and human-driven change.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct code mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED. Do not infer an ABC alignment.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Reservoir/store holds carbon; flux/process moves it. Arrows must be labelled and directed.
- Atmosphere: CO₂ and methane; biosphere: living/dead organic matter; hydrosphere: dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate/carbonate and aquatic life; geosphere: soils, sediments, carbonate rocks and fossil fuels.
- Photosynthesis transfers atmospheric/hydrospheric CO₂ into biomass using light energy.
- Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers returns CO₂ to air/water; decomposition also transfers carbon into soil/sediment.
- Combustion of biomass/fossil fuels transfers stored carbon rapidly to atmosphere as CO₂.
- Ocean-atmosphere exchange operates both directions; uptake depends on conditions. Marine organisms/sedimentation can move carbon toward long-term geological stores.
- Weathering, burial, fossil-fuel formation, volcanism and rock processes operate over longer timescales.
- Carbon atoms are conserved; cycle balance depends on rates, not a single circular route.
- Fossil-fuel use moves geological carbon to the atmosphere faster than slow processes replace it, altering reservoir sizes.
- A model is a simplification and should show boundary, stores, labelled processes, direction and relevant timescales.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: four-sphere map; reservoir-versus-flux; fast/slow cycles; worked arrow chains; human perturbation; model limits.
Classroom View: carbon-token transfer model, construct/critique diagrams, trace named atoms and compare pre-industrial/human-changed fluxes.
Homework: label stores/processes, trace sphere interactions, repair arrow errors and explain changing reservoir size.
Practice 48: 8 spheres/stores; 12 process arrows; 8 photosynthesis/respiration; 8 combustion/human change; 8 multi-sphere pathways; 4 model critique.
Test 16: independent diagrams and scenarios, including at least 6 multi-step transfer explanations.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Carbon exists only as CO₂; plants only remove carbon and do not respire; combustion destroys carbon; the cycle has one starting point; oceans only absorb; fossil fuels form quickly; every reservoir stays constant. Feedback must trace matter between named spheres and correct the arrow/process.

AC9S9U04
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Use wave and particle models to describe energy transfer through different mediums and examine the usefulness of each model for explaining phenomena.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students use and compare models, not merely define waves or particles. Wave models explain transfer through oscillations/disturbances; particle models explain mechanisms such as collisions, vibrations and bulk movement in matter. Students must state what each model explains and where it is limited. Full quantum wave-particle duality is outside this code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. The curriculum expects energy transfer through different media and explicit evaluation of model usefulness.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: FF.1 transverse waves; FF.2 longitudinal waves; FF.3 Compare energy of waves; FF.4 waves on strings; HH.1 electromagnetic waves; HH.2 infrared applications; HH.3 ultraviolet effects; GG sound forthcoming; energy/thermal prerequisites also contribute. Live FF.3 shows two wave graphs travelling at the same speed in the same medium and asks learners to compare amplitude and frequency. Stage 1 requires 6 correct in a row, supporting quantitative visual interpretation but not the complete particle-model comparison.
3. Khan Academy, authenticated “Wave properties”: a wave is a propagating disturbance that transfers energy without net transfer of matter; mechanical waves require a medium, electromagnetic waves do not; period, wavelength and frequency describe the model; speed depends on wave type and medium. The course includes mechanical/light comparison, sound properties, Understand/Apply exercises and reviews.
4. Khan “Sound waves” explains particle vibration/compressions through solids, liquids and gases and why sound cannot travel in a vacuum. “Introduction to light” supports electromagnetic-wave and particle-like descriptions, but quantum detail should remain outside the Year 9 boundary.
5. OpenStax Physics public resources provide accurate relationships among amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed and energy, and particle-level explanations for conduction.
6. The Physics Classroom public tutorials use wave/slinky/string models to distinguish medium-particle motion from energy propagation and are useful for misconception checking.
7. ABC Education search: no stable direct Year 9 mapping located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- A model is a purposeful representation; choose it for the question and acknowledge omissions.
- Mechanical wave: disturbance propagates through a medium while particles oscillate about equilibrium; energy moves, matter has no net transport with the wave.
- Transverse: oscillation perpendicular to travel; longitudinal: oscillation parallel, producing compressions and rarefactions.
- Amplitude relates to energy carried (within a given system); frequency is cycles per second; wavelength is spacing; v=fλ.
- Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave and needs matter; medium particle properties affect speed and transmission.
- Electromagnetic radiation can travel through vacuum; wave model explains reflection, refraction, diffraction, wavelength/frequency and spectrum.
- Particle model for conduction: higher-energy particles vibrate/move more and transfer energy through interactions; in metals mobile electrons contribute.
- Particle model for convection: energy transfer accompanies bulk movement of warmer, less-dense fluid and cooler, denser fluid.
- Radiation is not explained as particles bumping through a material; it needs no material medium.
- One phenomenon may need different models at different scales. A water-wave surface sketch does not literally depict sound particles or EM fields.
- Evaluate usefulness with: what feature/evidence it explains, prediction it permits, and limitation/feature it omits.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: model-purpose framework; transverse/longitudinal; wave quantities; sound/light media; particle conduction/convection; side-by-side model evaluation.
Classroom View: rope/slinky demonstrations, particle-roleplay, medium comparison, model selection and limitation statements.
Homework: interpret wave graphs, use v=fλ, explain transfer in solids/fluids/vacuum and evaluate competing models.
Practice 48: 10 wave basics; 10 quantities/graphs; 8 sound/media; 8 EM/radiation; 8 particle thermal transfer; 4 model evaluation.
Test 16: novel transfer phenomena with at least 6 model-choice/usefulness items and no copied practice stems.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Waves transport matter forward; all waves need a medium; particles travel from the heat source to the far end in conduction; frequency and amplitude are interchangeable; higher frequency always means higher speed; light is sound at higher frequency; models are exact copies. Feedback must identify the model, direction of oscillation/transfer and the limitation causing the error.

AC9S9U05
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Apply the law of conservation of energy to analyse system efficiency in terms of energy inputs, outputs, transfers and transformations.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students define a system boundary, account for energy entering, leaving and changing form, and calculate or compare efficiency using useful output relative to total input. They distinguish conservation of energy from degradation/dissipation. Advanced thermodynamic entropy is outside the code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. “Apply” and “analyse” require energy-accounting diagrams, calculations and evaluation of real systems, not recall alone.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: EE.1 Conservation of mechanical energy is the strongest direct component mapping. The live first question gives a 60 g steel marble on a 1.5 m hill, initial speed 0.5 m/s and later speed 1.6 m/s, then asks for kinetic and gravitational potential energy while ignoring non-gravitational forces. This establishes quantitative input/output accounting, but IXL has no direct Year 9 efficiency skill; efficiency itself is SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.
3. Khan Academy: the public conservation-of-energy lesson and work/energy sequence treat energy as transformed or transferred rather than created or destroyed. The mechanical-advantage lesson explicitly links input and output work through conservation of energy. No direct Year 9 system-efficiency assessment was located; use as conceptual support.
4. OpenStax Physics resources explain conservation of mechanical energy, work done by non-conservative forces, power and efficiency. They support the distinction between total energy conservation and declining useful mechanical output.
5. BBC Bitesize energy-transfer and efficiency materials provide accessible efficiency equations and Sankey-diagram conventions for secondary learners.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct code-level resource located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Name the system and boundary before accounting for energy; transfers cross the boundary, transformations occur between stores/forms.
- Conservation: total energy input equals total energy output when every output is included.
- Useful output depends on the system’s purpose; heating from a heater may be useful, while heating from a motor is usually less useful.
- Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%; the same ratio can use powers when measured over the same interval.
- Efficiency cannot exceed 100% in a correctly defined ordinary energy-conversion system.
- “Wasted” energy is not destroyed; it is transferred to surroundings, commonly as thermal energy and sound, becoming less available for the intended task.
- Sankey-arrow widths represent energy amounts; all output branches sum to the input width.
- J measures energy, W measures power (J/s); do not mix them in one ratio unless the calculation consistently uses energy or power.
- Mechanical energy examples combine kinetic and gravitational potential energy; friction transfers mechanical energy to thermal stores.
- Compare designs using equal functional output, stated boundaries and evidence rather than assuming the device with lower input is always better.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: system boundaries; input-transfer-transformation-output chains; conservation; useful/dissipated outputs; efficiency equation; Sankey diagrams; mechanical worked example.
Classroom View: energy-chain card sort, marble/pendulum accounting, Sankey reconstruction, appliance-efficiency comparison and error analysis.
Homework: label chains, calculate missing inputs/outputs and efficiencies, interpret Sankey diagrams, justify fair comparisons.
Practice 48: 8 systems/boundaries; 8 transfers/transformations; 10 conservation calculations; 10 efficiency calculations; 8 Sankey/data analyses; 4 design evaluations.
Test 16: new contexts with at least 6 multi-step quantitative items and 4 explanations of conservation despite lower useful output.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Energy is used up; efficiency measures speed; all heat is wasted; output may exceed input; energy and power units are interchangeable; a narrower Sankey arrow means slower flow; conservation means every device is 100% efficient. Feedback must restore the complete energy account and identify the intended useful output.

AC9S9U06
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain how the model of the atom changed following the discovery of electrons, protons and neutrons and describe how natural radioactive decay results in stable atoms.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students connect new experimental evidence to revisions of atomic models and describe how unstable nuclei change through natural decay. They use proton, neutron, electron, isotope and simple alpha/beta/gamma ideas. Detailed quantum mechanics, decay mathematics and nuclear engineering are extension only.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. The causal link is evidence → discovery → revised model, followed by nucleus stability and natural decay.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: S.1 Count subatomic particles in atoms and ions; S.2 Nuclear notation; T. Types of radioactive decay; T.1 Classify nuclear reactions. The live decay skill begins with four naturally occurring radium isotopes and asks learners to distinguish atomic number, nucleon number, protons and neutrons. It scaffolds nuclear notation before later decay classification across three stages. IXL does not directly teach the full historical model sequence.
3. Khan Academy: “Protons, neutrons, and electrons in atoms” establishes particle charge/location; “Rutherford’s gold foil experiment” links unexpected alpha scattering to the small dense nucleus; carbon-14 and radiometric-dating resources explain probabilistic decay and daughter atoms. These map directly to evidence-based model revision and decay.
4. Royal Society of Chemistry education resources on atomic-model history trace Dalton’s indivisible spheres, Thomson’s electron-containing model, Rutherford’s nuclear model, and Chadwick’s neutron.
5. ANSTO education resources explain isotopes, ionising radiation, half-life and radioactive decay in an Australian scientific context.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct code-level resource located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Dalton’s model treated atoms as solid indivisible particles; later evidence showed internal structure.
- Thomson’s cathode-ray evidence revealed negatively charged electrons, prompting a model with electrons embedded in diffuse positive charge.
- Geiger–Marsden/Rutherford scattering: most alpha particles passed through foil but a few deflected strongly, supporting a mostly empty atom with a tiny dense positive nucleus.
- Rutherford identified the proton/positive nuclear charge; Chadwick’s evidence for a neutral nuclear particle explained missing nuclear mass and isotopes.
- Modern school model: protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons occupying regions around it. Models are revised explanations, not literal photographs.
- Atomic number = protons; mass/nucleon number = protons + neutrons; isotopes share proton number but differ in neutrons.
- Some proton–neutron combinations are unstable and spontaneously decay, producing a daughter nucleus and radiation; a daughter may be stable or continue a decay chain until a stable nuclide is reached.
- Alpha emission reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2; beta-minus converts a neutron to a proton, leaving mass number unchanged and increasing atomic number by 1; gamma emission releases energy without changing either number.
- Decay is random for one nucleus but predictable statistically for a large sample; it is not triggered because an atom “wants” stability.
- Chemical reactions rearrange electrons/bonds; radioactive decay changes the nucleus and may change the element.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: evidence/model timeline; particle properties and notation; isotope stability; alpha/beta/gamma changes; daughter nuclei and decay chains; model limitations.
Classroom View: evidence-to-model matching, gold-foil prediction task, nuclear-notation tiles and decay-chain modelling.
Homework: explain each model revision, calculate particle counts, complete simple nuclear changes and diagnose historical/decay misconceptions.
Practice 48: 8 historical evidence; 8 model comparisons; 10 particle/isotope notation; 12 decay type/nuclear changes; 6 stability/decay chains; 4 model evaluation.
Test 16: independent evidence and isotope cases, including at least 4 model-revision explanations and 6 nuclear-change items.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Electrons orbit like fixed planets; Rutherford discovered every subatomic particle; neutrons have no mass; isotopes are ions; gamma changes the element; decay makes an atom vanish; half-life predicts one atom’s exact decay time; radioactive material becomes stable after one emission in every case. Feedback must connect evidence to the revised model or conserve nucleon/charge changes correctly.

AC9S9U07
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Model the rearrangement of atoms in chemical reactions using a range of representations, including word and simple balanced chemical equations, and use these to demonstrate the law of conservation of mass.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students translate among particle models, word equations and simple symbol equations; balance equations by changing coefficients; and use equal atom counts to explain mass conservation. Complex stoichiometry, redox half-equations and equilibrium are outside the code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. The code requires multiple linked representations and an explanation of conservation, not equation balancing in isolation.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: Y.1 Count atoms in chemical reactions using models; Y.2 Count atoms in chemical equations; Y.3 Balance chemical equations I; Z.1 Classify chemical reactions; Z.2 Single replacement reactions. The live Y.3 question asks for the smallest whole-number coefficients in H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → HCl(g), permits a blank coefficient of 1, and recommends atom counting as prerequisite. This exposes coefficient/subscript and simplest-ratio traps.
3. Khan Academy: “Chemical equations” states that matter conservation requires equal numbers of each atom and coefficients are used to balance equations; “Visually understanding balancing chemical equations” uses particle representations; the balancing walkthrough models a systematic count-adjust-recount method.
4. OpenStax Chemistry resources define reactants/products, symbolic equations, coefficients, state symbols and balancing as conservation of each element.
5. Royal Society of Chemistry education resources use particle diagrams and practical closed/open-system examples to connect balanced equations to conservation of mass.
6. ABC Education search: no stable direct code-level resource located; SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Reactants are written left of the arrow and products right; the arrow means “yields/forms,” not mathematical equality.
- Word equation names substances; particle model shows numbers/types of particles; formula equation encodes composition; each representation reveals and omits different information.
- In a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged as bonds break and form. Atoms are not created, destroyed or changed into different elements.
- Count every element on both sides, including atoms inside parentheses where used.
- Balance only by placing whole-number coefficients before formulae; never change subscripts because that changes substance identity.
- Multiply a coefficient by every atom in that formula; an omitted coefficient means 1.
- Use the smallest possible whole-number coefficient ratio and verify every element after each adjustment.
- In a closed system, total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. Apparent mass loss in an open vessel can result from a gas escaping; apparent gain can result from gaseous reactant entering.
- State symbols add physical-state information but do not alter atom counts.
- A balanced equation demonstrates atom-number conservation; measured mass evidence requires the system boundary to include all matter.

CONTENT BLUEPRINT
Topic Page: reaction/representation overview; translating word–particle–symbol forms; counting atoms; balancing algorithm; closed-system mass evidence; worked gas example.
Classroom View: atom-token rearrangement, representation stations, balance-and-justify routine, sealed/open vessel comparison and error correction.
Homework: translate representations, count atoms, balance simple equations, explain mass results and critique invalid models.
Practice 48: 8 reactant/product/word equations; 10 particle models; 10 atom counting; 12 balancing; 4 mass data; 4 representation critique.
Test 16: novel reactions with at least 6 cross-representation items, 6 balancing/counting items and 4 conservation explanations; no practice stems reused.

MISCONCEPTIONS
Coefficients change only the first element; subscripts may be edited to balance; atoms disappear; the arrow means equals; balanced means equal molecule counts; mass is lost whenever gas forms; mass conservation applies only to solids; catalysts must be counted as products. Feedback must preserve each formula and recount each element across the stated system boundary.

AC9S9H01
CURRICULUM DESCRIPTION
Explain how scientific knowledge is validated and refined, including the role of publication and peer review.

CURRICULUM BOUNDARY
Students explain a continuing evidence-checking process: transparent methods, scrutiny, publication, peer review, replication, critique, synthesis and correction. They must understand both the value and limitations of peer review. Technical journal metrics and advanced philosophy of science are outside the code.

SOURCE EVIDENCE
1. Australian Curriculum v9: exact description above. “Validated and refined” means science is checked and updated through community processes; peer review and publication are required named components.
2. IXL Australia, authenticated Year 9 Science: no direct publication/peer-review skill—SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED. A.1 The process of scientific inquiry is a partial prerequisite; the live item presents an investigation of soap lather in distilled water with different Epsom-salt amounts and asks learners to identify the investigable question within observation, method, results and conclusion. KK.1–KK.2 claim-evaluation skills add evidence appraisal but do not replace peer-review instruction.
3. Khan Academy: no direct Year 9 publication-and-peer-review lesson located—SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED. Scientific-method and evidence resources can support prerequisites only.
4. The Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research (NHMRC, ARC and Universities Australia) establishes honesty, rigour, transparency, fairness, responsible dissemination and correction as foundations of trustworthy Australian research.
5. CSIRO Publishing’s journal and author guidance illustrates editorial screening, expert peer review, revision and publication as real scientific communication stages.
6. ABC Education/media-literacy resources are suitable for comparing claims, sources and evidence, but no stable direct curriculum-code mapping was located; use only as supplementary claim-evaluation context.

AUTHORING-READY KNOWLEDGE
- Scientists validate claims by checking whether methods are appropriate, evidence supports conclusions, analyses are sound and results can withstand independent scrutiny.
- Publication makes methods, evidence and reasoning available to a scientific community; it enables checking, reuse, replication and debate.
- Before publication, editors assess scope/basic suitability and independent experts usually review quality, validity, clarity and significance; authors may revise, respond or be rejected.
- Peer review improves reliability but does not prove a claim true, guarantee no error or eliminate bias/fraud.
- Replication repeats a study; reproducibility checks whether reported data/methods yield the result. Converging evidence across methods and groups strengthens confidence.
- Post-publication checking includes letters, comments, new studies, systematic reviews and reanalysis.
- Refinement may narrow a claim, improve a model, revise an explanation, publish a correction or retract unreliable work.
- Scientific confidence is graduated and evidence-responsive. Change is a strength when new evidence or better methods justify it.
- Transparent methods/data, appropriate uncertainty, conflicts-of-interest disclosure and clear distinction between observation and inference support evaluation.
