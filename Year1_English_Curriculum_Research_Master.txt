CODE: AC9E1LA01
DESCRIPTOR: Understand how language, facial expressions and gestures are used to interact with others when asking for and providing information, making offers, exclaiming, requesting and giving commands.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL maps three shades-of-meaning skills to this code: Find the words with related meanings, Describe the difference between related words, and Order related words based on meaning.
- The inspected related-meaning example asked learners to select two words in the same semantic category and explained a difference in intensity using "smile" and "grin."
- IXL's picture-feeling worked example, although officially mapped to AC9E1LA08 rather than this code, models attending to facial expression, action and situation together.
- These tasks support vocabulary choice and reading non-verbal cues but do not cover the full interaction-purpose set. Offers, exclamations, requests, information exchanges and commands require direct curriculum-led teaching.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Coordinate words, voice, face, gesture and body position with the speaker's purpose and relationship.
- Distinguish asking from providing information; making an offer from making a request; and a request from a command.
- Show that the same words can be received differently when tone, expression or gesture changes.
- Teach interpretation in context rather than treating one gesture as having one universal meaning.
- First Nations signing, gesture or body-language examples require specific, authoritative and culturally appropriate sources; avoid pan-Indigenous rules.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired face/gesture scenes, speech bubbles, short audio-supported exchanges, purpose cards and same-words/different-delivery comparisons.
- Include classroom, family and community interactions with varied speakers and respectful alternatives.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A smile always means the speaker agrees.
- Loud speech automatically makes a command.
- An offer and request are interchangeable.
- Words alone determine meaning.
- One gesture has the same meaning in every cultural and social context.
- Any question asks for useful information.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Use purpose → language/non-verbal cue → likely reception reasoning.
- Include recognition, comparison, repair and choosing an appropriate response.
- Selected-response tasks can assess interpretation; Classroom View/worksheet evidence must include students speaking, listening or role-playing.

CODE: AC9E1LA02
DESCRIPTOR: Explore language to provide reasons for likes, dislikes and preferences.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL maps Compare pictures using adjectives, Compare pictures using comparative and superlative adjectives, and Does the adjective compare two or more than two?
- The inspected worked example contrasted two pictured objects using a comparative adjective, then explicitly separated comparative "-er" from superlative "-est."
- This supports precise comparison language but does not directly require a personal preference plus a relevant reason.
- The essential "I prefer ... because ..." relationship must therefore remain curriculum-led, not attributed to IXL.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- State a like, dislike or preference and give a reason that logically explains it.
- Use "because" and suitable alternatives orally and in short written responses.
- Compare options using accessible words such as better, easier, faster, quieter or more interesting only when the evidence supports them.
- Recognise that different people may hold different preferences and still give valid reasons.
- Separate a reason from repetition: "I like it because I like it" does not add evidence.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Paired books, games, foods, activities, images or text excerpts; preference/reason matching; reason/no-reason sorting; short discussion prompts.
- Provide visual and oral access without forcing unsupported reading load.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- A preference needs no reason.
- Repeating the preference counts as explaining it.
- Any sentence beginning with "because" is a relevant reason.
- A comparative word makes a preference objectively true.
- A different preference must be wrong.
- "Best" can be used without stating the comparison group or basis.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Progress from recognising a reason to matching, repairing and independently giving one.
- Keep answer options defensible by making the stated criterion visible.
- Practical evidence should allow students to express and respond to genuine preferences.

SOURCES
- QCAA Prep–Year 6 English content-description sequence: https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_p-6_cd_sequence.pdf

CODE: AC9E1LA03
DESCRIPTOR: Explore how texts are organised according to their purpose, such as to recount, narrate, express opinion, inform, report and explain.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL's Year 1 Australian Curriculum alignment page lists no mapped skill beneath AC9E1LA03.
- The inspected "What is the picture about?" example asks for a picture's main idea and rejects an option not shown. It is useful adjacent evidence for using visible evidence, but it is mapped to a literature code and does not teach text organisation.
- IXL is SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for direct descriptor coverage.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Connect purpose to predictable organisation: recount events in time order; narrative builds characters/events/problem or change; opinion states a view and reasons; informative/report texts group facts; explanations show how or why.
- Compare familiar texts that share a topic but have different purposes and structures.
- Recognise that words, images, labels and diagrams can work together within the structure.
- Teach purpose as what the text is trying to do, not simply its topic or format.
- Year 1 learners explore typical patterns; they are not expected to memorise rigid genre templates.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Short paired texts on the same topic, mixed text-stage cards, purpose/feature matching, recount/procedure sequences, simple fact and opinion texts.
- Include spoken, print, digital and multimodal examples.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Topic and purpose are the same.
- Every story or information text has exactly one fixed layout.
- A heading alone proves a text's purpose.
- Time words belong only to narratives.
- Pictures are decorative and never part of organisation.
- A text cannot combine purposes.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Require evidence: identify the purpose, point to organisational clues and explain the connection.
- Cover all descriptor examples across the resource set without making every quiz item a terminology test.
- Use creation/comparison tasks in the worksheet and Classroom View; multiple choice alone cannot show students can organise a text.

CODE: AC9E1LA04
DESCRIPTOR: Explore how repetition, rhyme and rhythm create cohesion in simple poems, chants and songs.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- Direct IXL mapping: Choose the picture that rhymes with the word; Which word does not rhyme?; Complete the rhyme; Complete the poem with a word that rhymes.
- The inspected poem-completion example required choosing an end word whose final sound matched the preceding line. The worked explanation compared rhyming and non-rhyming word endings, then reread the completed two-line poem.
- IXL gives strong rhyme progression but does not directly cover repetition, beat/rhythm, performance or how all three devices connect a whole chant/song.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Rhyme depends on matching final sound patterns, not matching first letters or spelling alone.
- Repetition can occur in words, phrases, whole lines or sentence patterns and helps parts feel connected.
- Rhythm is the pattern of beats/stresses heard when the text is spoken or sung; it is not the same as rhyme.
- Explore how the devices support prediction, memory, emphasis, participation and cohesion.
- Include listening, speaking, clapping/tapping, performing and inventing—not only silent word matching.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Short original poems, call-and-response chants, refrain cards, beat marks, audio/read-aloud support, rhyming-picture sets and incomplete patterned lines.
- Avoid reproducing copyrighted songs or long published poems.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Words rhyme because they start with the same sound.
- Identical spelling is required for rhyme.
- Rhythm and rhyme are synonyms.
- Any repeated word creates a coherent poem.
- Visual line endings determine rhyme without listening.
- A poem must rhyme.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Progress from hearing/identifying to completing, comparing, performing and inventing patterns.
- Include repetition and rhythm in equal depth with rhyme across the full resource set.
- Ensure audio or teacher read-aloud access where sound is essential.
- Keep written examples original and short.

SOURCES

- IXL inspected: Complete the poem with a word that rhymes.

CODE: AC9E1LA05
DESCRIPTOR: Understand how print and screen texts are organised using features such as page numbers, tables of content, headings and titles, navigation buttons, swipe screens, verbal commands, links and images.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL's Year 1 Australian Curriculum alignment page lists no mapped skill beneath AC9E1LA05.
- The Year 1 catalogue contains general reading tasks but no inspected activity that directly compares print navigation with screen navigation.
- IXL is SOURCE-NOT-MAPPED for this descriptor; coverage must be driven by the exact curriculum list and supported by authentic print/digital demonstrations.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Name a feature, explain its job and use it to locate or move to information.
- Compare title (whole text) with heading (section), page number with table of contents, and static print navigation with screen buttons, links, swipe actions or verbal commands.
- Treat images as organisational/meaning-making features when they guide, label or locate information—not automatically as decoration.
- Recognise that print and screen texts can contain equivalent information organised differently.
- Include accessibility-aware navigation such as descriptive link labels and verbal commands without implying every device works identically.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Realistic book spreads, contents pages, headings/page numbers, safe mock webpages or app screens, navigation paths and print-versus-screen comparison cards.
- Questions must show the feature; asking from a decontextualised definition is insufficient.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Title and heading are interchangeable.
- A table of contents gives the full information.
- Page numbers and navigation buttons do the same job in the same way.
- Every image is decoration.
- A link always moves to the next page.
- Swipe or voice commands work identically on every screen.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach feature → job → navigation action → check result.
- Include locating, comparing, choosing, repairing and explaining.
- Use accessible mock interfaces rather than live external links for assessment stability.
- Classroom View/worksheet tasks should require students to navigate an actual age-appropriate print and digital text.

CODE: AC9E1LA06
DESCRIPTOR: Understand that a simple sentence consists of a single independent clause representing a single event or idea.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL maps "Is it the naming or action part of the sentence?" to this code.
- The inspected worked example splits a sentence into the participant/topic part and the part that tells what the participant does or is like.
- This supports identifying who/what and what happens/is, but IXL's child-facing labels do not fully establish independent clause, completeness or the boundary between one event/idea and joined clauses.
- Direct curriculum-led examples are required for complete sentences, fragments and over-joined ideas.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- A simple sentence is one independent clause that can stand as a complete message.
- Its single event or idea includes a participant (who/what) and a process, happening or state; useful circumstances may add where, when or how without creating another clause.
- Distinguish a complete clause from fragments that name only a participant or give only an action/detail.
- Distinguish one expanded simple sentence from two independent ideas incorrectly joined together.
- Use meaning before terminology: students should build and check complete messages, not memorise a grammar definition alone.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Sentence/fragment pairs, participant–process cards, sentence-building strips, picture-to-sentence matches and one-idea/two-idea comparisons.
- Include spoken rehearsal and punctuation without conflating sentence structure with punctuation alone.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Any group of words ending in a full stop is a sentence.
- A long sentence must contain more than one idea.
- An action phrase by itself is always complete.
- Adding "and" always keeps a sentence simple.
- Every sentence describes a visible action; states such as "is tired" also complete an idea.
- Capitalisation alone fixes an incomplete clause.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach choose the event/idea → name who/what → say what happens/is → check it stands alone.
- Include recognition, completion, expansion and repair tasks.
- Classroom/worksheet work must require students to orally compose and independently write complete simple sentences.

SOURCES
- IXL Australian Curriculum Year 1 mapping: https://au.ixl.com/standards/english/year-1
- IXL inspected: Is it the naming or action part of the sentence?
- QCAA Prep–Year 6 English sequence: https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_p-6_cd_sequence.pdf

CODE: AC9E1LA07
DESCRIPTOR: Understand that words can represent people, places and things (nouns, including pronouns), happenings and states (verbs), qualities (adjectives) and details such as when, where and how (adverbs).
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL maps multiple noun, agreement, verb and adjective skills to this code, including classifying nouns, selecting nouns, identifying nouns in sentences, common/proper nouns, possessive nouns, subject–verb choices and adjective identification.
- The inspected noun example required locating one noun in a complete question and classifying nouns as people, animals, places or things.
- IXL provides strong noun/adjective/action-verb practice, but the descriptor also requires pronouns, verb states and adverbial details of when/where/how. These components must not be underrepresented because fewer directly mapped Year 1 tasks expose them.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Teach word classes through their job in a specific sentence; the same written word may have different jobs in different contexts.
- Nouns and pronouns represent participants; verbs represent happenings, actions or states; adjectives express qualities; adverbs/adverb groups add when, where or how.
- Include noun groups and short word groups where age-appropriate rather than implying every function is carried by one isolated word.
- Show how precise word choices change meaning, not merely how to label terms.
- Keep formal grammar within Year 1 scope; avoid advanced clause analysis or exhaustive exception rules.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Word-job cards, highlighted sentences, picture-supported sentence choices, replace-a-word tasks and sentence-expansion models.
- Include actions and states, concrete and familiar abstract nouns, pronoun reference and details of time/place/manner.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Every noun is a visible object.
- Every verb is a physical action.
- Describing words always appear directly before nouns.
- Any word ending in "-ly" is an adverb and every adverb ends in "-ly."
- Pronouns do not represent people or things.
- A word has one fixed class regardless of sentence context.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Use sentence meaning → ask the word's job → identify class → explain its contribution.
- Balance all descriptor categories rather than overproducing noun questions.
- Include selecting, sorting, replacing and improving words in meaningful sentences.

SOURCES
- IXL Australian Curriculum Year 1 mapping: https://au.ixl.com/standards/english/year-1
- IXL inspected: Identify nouns in a sentence.
- QCAA Prep–Year 6 English sequence: https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_p-6_cd_sequence.pdf

STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- Direct IXL mapping: "Which feeling matches the picture?"

- IXL strongly models extracting meaning from one narrative-style image, but the descriptor requires comparison across different text types and explaining contribution, not only naming a feeling.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Compare what an image adds, emphasises, clarifies or suggests alongside words.
- Include narrative illustrations, informative diagrams/photos, signs, posters and simple digital/multimodal texts.
- Identify information shown only in the image, information repeated by words and image, and cases where the image changes mood or focus.
- Compare two images of the same subject or two text types to explain different effects.
- Use works by First Nations Australian authors/illustrators only with accurate attribution and appropriate contextual framing.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Word–image pairs, alternative covers/illustrations, labelled diagrams, posters, image sequences and same-subject/different-purpose comparisons.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Images are always decoration.
- The words and image always communicate identical information.
- Any feeling inferred from an image is equally supported.
- A photograph is automatically more truthful or informative than an illustration.
- One visual detail determines the whole meaning.
- Image size alone determines importance.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Ask: what do the words say, what does the image show, what meaning comes from combining them, and how would meaning change with another image?
- Include evidence-based comparison, not preference-only responses.
- Classroom/worksheet tasks should allow discussion and creation of alternative images for a text.

CODE: AC9E1LA10
DESCRIPTOR: Understand that written language uses punctuation such as full stops, question marks and exclamation marks, and uses capital letters for familiar proper nouns.
STATUS: AUTHORING-READY
============================================================

IXL EVIDENCE AND LIMIT
- IXL maps statement, question, exclamation and command identification; choosing end marks; mixed sentence-purpose classification; and capitalising people/pet names, days/months, sentence starts and "I."
- The inspected worked example taught four sentence purposes and linked statements to full stops, questions to question marks, exclamations to strong feeling and commands usually to full stops but sometimes exclamation marks.
- The mapped capitalisation range is broader than the descriptor's familiar proper-noun focus. Authoring must retain the exact Year 1 boundary while using sentence-start capitals as relevant supporting knowledge.

CURRICULUM DEPTH
- Punctuation marks sentence boundaries and guides meaning, pausing and intonation.
- Match full stops, question marks and exclamation marks to meaning and purpose, not merely to a cue word.
- Recognise that commands can end with a full stop or exclamation mark depending on intended strength.
- Capitalise familiar proper nouns such as names of people, pets, places, days or months where appropriate.
- Distinguish a proper name from a common noun and avoid teaching that every important word receives a capital.

REQUIRED REPRESENTATIONS
- Unpunctuated sentences, speech/read-aloud comparisons, sentence-purpose sorts, editing tasks and familiar-name/common-noun contrasts.
- Include examples where intonation changes after punctuation and where context determines the defensible end mark.

MISCONCEPTIONS TO ASSESS
- Every loud sentence needs an exclamation mark.
- Every command ends with an exclamation mark.
- A sentence containing a question word always needs a question mark.
- Capital letters are used for any important object.
- A full stop is only a signal to breathe.
- Punctuation can be chosen without considering meaning.

AUTHORING REQUIREMENTS
- Teach determine meaning/purpose → choose boundary mark → check capitals → reread with suitable intonation.
- Include recognition, editing, explanation and independent application.
- Avoid ambiguous isolated sentences where more than one end mark is equally defensible without context.

SOURCES
- IXL Australian Curriculum Year 1 mapping: https://au.ixl.com/standards/english/year-1
