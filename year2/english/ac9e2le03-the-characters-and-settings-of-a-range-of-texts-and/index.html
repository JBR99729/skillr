Explain how story words create an impression AC9E2LE03

Learning intention: I can compare words that help readers imagine characters and settings.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Changing a verb changes the impression

Original versions: “Mara walked down the hall” and “Mara stomped down the hall.” Stomped suggests forceful steps and may suggest anger or frustration. Walked gives less detail about the movement. Neither sentence proves what Mara always feels. Compare actual language, then explain a supported impression.

Learn the skill

1. Compare actions and dialogue

Look at verbs, whole verb groups and speech as well as adjectives. Explain how a word helps present the character in that moment.

“Rae was carrying the bags. ‘You can rest,’ Rae whispered.” Compare “Rae was dragging the bags. ‘Hurry up!’ Rae snapped.”

Try it · Name one language change and explain a possible effect.

Was carrying / was dragging changes the action; whispered / snapped changes how speech is presented. The offer of rest can suggest care, while the sharp command can suggest impatience. Other interpretations need the actual words, not a permanent label for Rae.

2. Compare setting and seasonal clues

Read a whole description before selecting words. Sounds, light, plants and weather can shape a reader’s picture.

Version A: “Soft rain tapped the window. New leaves shone.” Version B: “Hard rain hammered the window. Bare branches shook.”

Try it · How do tapped and hammered change the impression? Can you name a season with certainty?

Tapped can suggest gentler rain; hammered suggests a stronger sound or force. The plant clues may suggest seasonal change, but these short invented scenes do not establish a named season for every place in Australia.

3. Read landscape language in a specific First Nations text

Use the actual creator’s words and their context. A glossary can help explain an unfamiliar word; do not invent its meaning from a picture alone.

Adult prepares two actual landscape descriptions in Cooee Mittigar by Jasmine Seymour and Leanne Mulgo Watson. Keep the lawful book open, with the glossary and source preparation in More examples.

Try it · Point to a word in each prepared description. What picture or relationship does that wording help you understand?

Name the real word and explain a supported effect using the page and glossary. Nura refers to Country in this Darug source, including relationships and belonging. Do not substitute a generic invented “Aboriginal landscape quote” or assume the word is local everywhere.

Common mistakes

“Only adjectives matter.” Actions, dialogue and verb groups shape meaning too.

“A possible feeling is a certain fact.” Explain the clue and qualify an inference where needed.

“Different words always mean the same thing.” Compare the impression created in the complete context.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Change one word and explain the effect

Use the complete paired passages in More examples. Highlight an action or setting word, replace it to make a new fictional version, then explain the changed impression. Compare this with one real phrase in the prepared literary book.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare walked and stomped. Core: explain a word–impression link in two passages. Stretch: compare a whole verb group or a specific landscape phrase without making a universal claim.

An adult may read the passages and accept speech, pointing, AAC or a scribed explanation. Attribute actual cultural language and avoid reproducing or imitating cultural artwork.

Three tricky questions

1. Does stomped prove Mara is always angry?

No. It suggests a way of moving and a possible feeling in this moment.

2. Can the same place be described as peaceful or lonely?

Yes. Different details can create different impressions; show the actual words.

3. Is a rewritten line still the author’s original wording?

No. Clearly call it your changed fictional version.

FAQ

Do I need to learn difficult analysis labels?

Begin with the actual word, what it describes and its effect. Use useful terms after an example.

Can two readers give different impressions?

Yes, if they can explain real language clues and keep unsupported guesses separate.

Complete original character and setting comparisons

Character A: “Rae was carrying the bags. ‘You can rest now,’ Rae whispered to the tired visitor.” Character B: “Rae was dragging the bags. ‘Hurry up!’ Rae snapped at the tired visitor.” Compare was carrying / was dragging and whispered / snapped: these action and dialogue choices can suggest different attitudes in this moment. They do not prove Rae’s permanent personality. Setting A: “Soft rain tapped the window. New leaves shone on the tree.” Setting B: “Hard rain hammered the window. Bare branches shook in the wind.” Compare sound and plant clues, then explain the different impression. These are two invented seasonal scenes; do not infer a named season everywhere in Australia from one clue. Rewrite one line to change its mood, and say which words you changed.

Actual landscape words from a specific text

Adult prepares a lawful copy of Cooee Mittigar, written by Jasmine Seymour and illustrated by Leanne Mulgo Watson, with its glossary and publisher teacher notes. Choose two seasonal spreads where Mulgo, the black swan, guides the reader. Find an actual word or picture clue on each spread and record its page or opening. Explain how the guide draws attention to that particular Country and seasonal change. The publisher identifies these creators and this setting as Darug. Country includes relationships and belonging, not just a generic place. Do not assume this language is local everywhere or imitate ceremony, cultural artwork or songlines. For this language task, the adult first selects two short landscape descriptions actually present in the lawful book and points to each on its spread. Compare what their words help you imagine. Keep the author’s wording separate from your own paraphrase; use the glossary for a Darug word. Do not invent a quotation or assume that an unfamiliar word names a landscape. A valid response identifies a real word and a supported impression.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Listing adjectives only: Explain what impression the language creates.
  • Ignoring verbs and dialogue: Actions and speech also reveal character and setting.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Identify how language presents literary features in different ways
  • Use the routine: Find feature → Collect words → Infer impression → Compare texts
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

Earlier worked model

‘Mara stomped down the hall.’ What does stomped suggest?

Answer: Mara may be angry.

The forceful verb stomped suggests an angry or frustrated movement.

Curriculum alignment
