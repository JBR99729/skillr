Compare how texts present the same topic AC9E2LY01

Learning intention: I can find shared information and explain how different texts organise or show it.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Same topic does not mean the same information

Original pair: “A tadpole wriggled past Tilly’s boot. Later Tilly noticed small legs.” Information card: “Tadpoles hatch from frog eggs. As they develop, legs grow and their bodies change.” Both mention tadpoles and legs. The story follows a character’s noticing; the information groups facts and includes eggs, which the story does not mention.

Learn the skill

1. Compare a poem, narrative and information

Begin with the shared topic. Read each complete text, then identify the information it actually supplies and how it presents it.

The three original frog-growth texts in More examples use a poem’s lines and sound pattern, Tilly’s events, and an information card’s stage facts.

Try it · What shared topic links them? Does every text name all the life stages?

Frog growth links them. No: the information card names eggs, tadpoles, froglets and adults; the poem and narrative select fewer details. A title or text-type label alone does not supply missing facts.

2. Sort shared and unique details

Compare one detail at a time. A detail is shared only when each chosen text supplies it. Separate prior knowledge from what the texts state.

In the worked pair above, both mention tadpoles and legs; only the information card mentions eggs. Only the story names Tilly.

Try it · Where would Tilly and eggs go in a both / story only / information only chart? Explain.

Tilly belongs under story only; eggs under information only. Neither belongs under both. Their placement depends on these actual supplied texts, not a rule about what every story or report contains.

3. Compare recipe formats

A packet panel, book page, video or digital card can share a making purpose. Their features can help readers follow ingredients, order or movement in different ways.

More examples supplies three original fruit-bowl recipe layouts. An adult can open the named ABC Annaka vegetable-truck recipe video and compare an actual making section.

Try it · What stays similar across the recipes, and what could the actual video add to the static versions?

Ingredients and ordered actions support making something. A viewed video can show movement over time and provide spoken explanation. Use an actual observed detail after viewing; if only a transcript was read, do not claim video observation. Different recipes need not have identical ingredients.

Common mistakes

“Same topic means identical facts.” Check each actual detail before calling it shared.

“Every story has headings like a report.” Compare the organisation that is really present.

“A description of a video means I watched it.” Distinguish reading a transcript from viewing movement.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build a shared-and-different chart

Use the complete frog texts in More examples. Record one shared detail, one detail only in a selected text and one presentation difference. Compare the prepared recipe formats and explain which feature helps a reader follow steps.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare two short cards with an adult. Core: classify supplied information and organisation. Stretch: choose a format for a particular reading need and justify it.

An adult may read text aloud. This is a reading comparison: no cooking or food handling is needed. Use the named video with adult guidance only when its viewing is part of the comparison.

Three tricky questions

1. Does a poem have to include every fact about its topic?

No. It can select details for its particular purpose.

2. Can a digital recipe look like a printed one?

Yes. Screen delivery does not guarantee animation or different wording.

3. Can a story contain a true fact?

Yes. Decide from the complete text, not a rigid fiction-versus-fact shortcut.

FAQ

Must both texts have the same title?

No. Use their actual topic and information.

Can a picture or diagram change the presentation?

Yes. Inspect the real labels, arrows or image information and explain what they add.

Three complete original texts about frog growth

Poem: “A tiny tadpole flicks its tail. / Small legs grow beside the trail. / A young frog rests beside the pool; / The shaded water feels so cool.” Imagined narrative: “Tilly watched a tadpole in the class record. In the next drawing it had legs. Later she drew a young frog beside the pond. ‘It has changed!’ she said.” Information: “A frog life cycle includes eggs, tadpoles, froglets and adults. Tadpoles hatch from eggs. As they develop, legs grow and their bodies change.” The information gives a broader stage sequence; the poem uses line breaks and a sound pattern, and the narrative follows Tilly’s noticing over time. They all concern frog growth; the poem and story do not include every life stage. Choose two of these texts first and name them A and B. Sort details actually present into both chosen texts / only text A / only text B. Do not add a scientific detail merely because you know it from elsewhere.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Comparing unrelated details: Begin with the shared topic and the question being investigated.
  • Only saying different: Name exactly what is presented differently and why.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Identify how different text types present similar information
  • Use the routine: Identify shared topic → Identify text type → Compare information → Compare presentation
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

Earlier worked model

A story and a report are both about whales. The story follows a calf on a journey. The report groups facts under headings. What is a clear difference?

Answer: The story presents events, while the report organises facts.

Both texts share the whale topic, but they present it through events and grouped facts.
