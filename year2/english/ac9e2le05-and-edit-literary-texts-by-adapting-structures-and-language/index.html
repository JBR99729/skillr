Adapt a story and edit your new version AC9E2LE05

Learning intention: I can keep a recognisable story pattern, make meaningful changes and improve my own creation.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep a pattern while changing the story

Original story: “Nell carried a seed to the garden. Rain began. Nell waited under a shelter. When the rain stopped, Nell planted the seed.” A new version can give a robot a seed-delivery goal, a wheel stuck in moon dust and help from a friend. Goal, difficulty, response and ending remain recognisable, while the characters and events change.

Learn the skill

1. Notice a structure you can adapt

Read or hear the whole familiar model first. Map the connected events before deciding what to keep and change. Not every literary text needs a problem–solution pattern.

In the supplied Nell story, the planting goal meets rain, Nell waits, and planting follows after the rain stops.

Try it · If your version is on the moon with a robot, what should remain connected?

The robot needs a clear goal, a fitting difficulty or sequence and an ending that follows its response. Changing a name alone gives little new story meaning. Other literary structures can be adapted when they are the chosen model.

2. Change language and mode deliberately

Invent dialogue, behaviour or description that suits the new version. Drawing, writing, performance or an adult-approved digital tool can tell connected events.

Original model: “The robot called.” New fictional detail: “The robot with a long silver arm called from the garden gate.” The extra phrases describe which robot and where it calls.

Try it · Create a new line of speech for the stuck robot and a drawing or caption that fits it.

Accept a coherent original line such as asking a friend for help, with a fitting visual or performance. Label added details as invented. A spoken line, drawing or digital panel should tell story meaning rather than merely name a tool.

3. Edit meaning, then reread

Check event order, clear references, useful detail and whether the ending fits. Editing includes more than spelling and neatness.

Out-of-order draft: “Nell planted the seed. The rain stopped. Nell waited under a shelter.” The complete original and changed story are in More examples.

Try it · Move the events into a coherent order, then make one visible edit in your own version.

For the model: Nell waits, the rain stops, then Nell plants. In the learner’s story, inspect the actual revision and ask why it helps. Accept another coherent sequence when its different meaning is explained.

Common mistakes

“Adapting means copying everything.” Keep an identifiable pattern and make meaningful original choices.

“An ending can ignore the earlier events.” Check that the new ending follows the established story.

“Editing only fixes spelling.” Improve sequence, clarity, dialogue or detail too.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make four panels, then revise one

Use the complete familiarised Nell story in More examples. Plan a changed character, setting and event across four panels. Tell or write the story, revise one part and show how it improves the meaning.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult models the event map. Core: make a coherent changed version. Stretch: adapt dialogue and description across two modes, keeping events consistent.

Observe the actual creation and edit, with support recorded. Use original drawings and safe performance; do not imitate culturally restricted stories, ceremonies or artwork.

Three tricky questions

1. Is changing only the title a rich adaptation?

Usually not. Show how a character, event, language choice or telling method changes meaning.

2. Can a drawing carry an event?

Yes. Explain what it shows and how it connects to the next part.

3. Can two different endings both work?

Yes, when each fits the established events and intended new meaning.

FAQ

Must I use a digital tool?

No. Practise available drawing, writing or performance first; explore digital telling with an adult when available.

Can an adult help write my words?

Yes. Record the support and keep the learner’s own story choices and explanation visible.

A complete original story, adaptation and edit targets

Original story: “Nell carried a seed to the garden. Rain began before Nell could plant it. Nell waited under a shelter. When the rain stopped, Nell planted the seed.” Four panels: character and goal / rain problem / waiting response / planting ending. Adapted version: “A robot carried a seed across a moon garden. Its wheel stuck in soft dust. ‘Please help me roll free,’ it called. A friend laid a board across the dust, and the robot reached the planting bed.” This changes character, place, obstacle, response and speech while retaining a goal–difficulty–response–ending structure. Sentence edit: “The robot called” can become “The robot with a long silver arm called from the garden gate.” The added phrases describe which robot and where it calls; these are new fictional details. Out-of-order draft: “Nell planted the seed. The rain stopped. Nell waited under a shelter.” Move the planting after the rain stops, then reread the whole draft. Make your own drawing, words or performance, and show one real edit; naming an edit is not the same as doing it.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Copying instead of adapting: Keep a pattern but make meaningful original changes.
  • Editing only spelling: Also improve sequence, detail, language and clarity.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

A familiar story follows a hero searching for treasure. Which change creates an adaptation?
