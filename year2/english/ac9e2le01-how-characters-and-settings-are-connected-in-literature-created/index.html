YEAR 2 ENGLISH

Connect characters with their story settings AC9E2LE01

Learning intention: I can explain how a particular place connects with what a character notices, needs or does.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A place can change a character’s choice

Original model: “Ari carried a kite to the oval. A strong wind pulled it sideways. Ari rolled up the string and waited.” The oval and wind are setting clues. Waiting responds to the windy conditions. The text supports that connection; it does not tell us Ari’s age or prove how every windy day ends.

Learn the skill

1. Link a fairytale character and place

Name who and where, then explain a relationship. A setting can make an action possible, difficult or important.

In the Grimm-version Rapunzel premise, the forest tower has a high window but no door or stairs. In Little Red-Cap, the wooded route and flowers affect the child’s journey. Brief original premise cards are in More examples.

Try it · How does the tower setting affect an ordinary visit? Compare it with a house that has a door.

Ordinary access is difficult because the tower has no door or stairs. A house with a door offers a different route. This is discussion of story design, not an invitation to climb.

2. Follow a recurring guide on Country

Read the actual named work with an adult. Track a recurring character across two parts and explain how the guide and the particular place connect.

In Cooee Mittigar, written by Jasmine Seymour and illustrated by Leanne Mulgo Watson, Mulgo the black swan guides readers on Darug Country. Prepare the lawful book, glossary and two seasonal spreads using More examples.

Try it · What does Mulgo help readers notice on each prepared spread? Point to an actual clue.

Use a word or visible detail from each actual spread and explain its connection with Country or seasonal change. The guide returns across the work. Do not invent a quotation, assume all First Nations stories are the same or treat a summary as the full book.

3. Compare Australian journeys and settings

Creators depict particular places in different ways. Compare what a character sees or does, using evidence from each work.

For Are We There Yet?, Alison Lester’s publisher identifies Grace and her family travelling through the outback, bush and coast. The adult prepares an actual outback spread and a contrasting place before discussing their pictures and words.

Try it · Compare one character–place connection in that spread with Mulgo’s guide role. What supports each connection?

Name both works and creators, identify the actual place clues and connect them to the character’s action or role. Accept different supported comparisons. Do not label Darug Country “outback” merely because it is First Nations Country.

Common mistakes

“Setting is only a place name.” Conditions and time can also affect a character’s actions.

“All stories from a culture say the same thing.” Name the specific creators, work and evidence.

“A summary proves I viewed the illustrations.” Look at the actual supplied spread before making a picture claim.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Build a character–setting evidence chart

Use the chart in More examples with a fairytale premise, the prepared Cooee Mittigar spreads and an actual Are We There Yet? outback spread. Add one clue and explain one connection for each. An adult can read or scribe while the child discusses.

Adjust the challenge Starter: point to who and where in one short model. Core: connect a setting clue to an action. Stretch: compare two named works and explain an important difference.

Supply the named lawful books and staged pages before their tasks. Respect each creator’s context; do not imitate ceremonies, cultural motifs or risky story actions.

Three tricky questions

1. Does a forest prove a character is frightened?

No. Use actual words, actions or picture clues; a feeling is not guaranteed by a setting.

2. Is every Australian story set in the outback?

No. Use the actual work and its place clues.

3. Can a character change a setting too?

Yes. For example, a story character might plant a garden. Explain the change shown by the text.

FAQ

Can we begin with the original kite model?

Yes. It teaches the connection method. Follow it with the named authentic literature; it does not replace those works.

Must everyone give the same interpretation?

No. Different ideas can be supported. Explain the actual clue and avoid unsupported certainty.

Named world fairytale premises

Brief original premise notes for the versions collected by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm: in Rapunzel, a young woman is kept in a forest tower with a high window but no door or stairs. That setting limits ordinary visits. In Little Red-Cap, the child travels through a wood to her grandmother and is drawn away from the path by flowers. The wooded route affects the journey. Discuss story choices; do not act out climbing, leaving safe paths or meeting strangers. Compare who, where and the connection, rather than merely naming a tower or forest.

First Nations shared reading

Adult prepares a lawful copy of Cooee Mittigar, written by Jasmine Seymour and illustrated by Leanne Mulgo Watson, with its glossary and publisher teacher notes. Choose two seasonal spreads where Mulgo, the black swan, guides the reader. Find an actual word or picture clue on each spread and record its page or opening. Explain how the guide draws attention to that particular Country and seasonal change. The publisher identifies these creators and this setting as Darug. Country includes relationships and belonging, not just a generic place. Do not assume this language is local everywhere or imitate ceremony, cultural artwork or songlines.

Australian outback shared reading

Adult also supplies Are We There Yet? by Australian author and illustrator Alison Lester. The publisher identifies Grace and her family travelling through the outback, bush and coast. Select an actual outback spread and one contrasting place. Read the words and look at the actual illustrations together; record one place detail and what a character does there. Check the book before naming any particular action or feeling. The short publisher facts here are not a replacement for the full book or its pictures.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Treating cultures as one story: Discuss the specific creator and text; avoid broad assumptions.
  • Describing setting without connection: Explain how the place affects a character's actions or feelings.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Discuss how setting shapes characters in literature from varied creators
  • Use the routine: Identify character → Identify setting → Find connection → Respect context → Discuss
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

The river rises over the walking track. What is the character most likely to do?

Answer: choose a marked path away from the water

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LE01: discuss how characters and settings are connected in literature created by First Nations Australian, and wide-ranging Australian and world authors and illustrators

  • discussing the depiction of characters in particular settings in well-known fairytales from wide-ranging world authors; for example, children in forests, and royalty in castles and towers
  • recognising recurring characters in particular settings in texts by First Nations Australian authors
  • exploring the way wide-ranging Australian authors and illustrators depict the Australian outback and the associated characters
