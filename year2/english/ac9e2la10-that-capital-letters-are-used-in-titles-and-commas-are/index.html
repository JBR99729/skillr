Use title capitals and clear list commas AC9E2LA10

Learning intention: I can follow a stated title style and separate complete items in a list.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

State the title style before editing

For this title-case model, capitalise the first word and main words: “A Walk to the Pond”. Internal to and the stay lower-case. Sentence case is another valid style: “A walk to the pond”. Both keep the first word capitalised and preserve proper-name capitals. A title task must tell you which convention to follow.

Learn the skill

1. Notice capitals in actual titles

Identify the work’s name and follow the given model. Title styles vary; capitalising every word is not a universal rule. Proper names keep their capitals.

For these exercises, use title case: capitalise the first word and main words; keep internal a, an, the, and, or, to and of lower-case unless part of a proper name.

Try it · Edit the title “mia and the moon” using this stated style.

“Mia and the Moon”. Mia is a name, Moon is a main title word, and internal and/the remain lower-case in the given model. A task without a declared style should allow a valid consistent alternative.

2. Separate whole list items

Find each complete item before adding commas. Use commas between listed items and and before the final item. Do not separate the words inside one item.

Items: a red hat / two blue pens / a ruler / paper. Model: “We packed a red hat, two blue pens, a ruler and paper.”

Try it · Add commas to “We saw ducks hens and goats.”

“We saw ducks, hens and goats.” Also accept a clear serial-comma version, “We saw ducks, hens, and goats”, unless a stated style excludes it. Keep the full stop.

3. Edit for the reader’s meaning

Check title boundaries, proper names and the whole list. Reading aloud can help notice grouping, but commas are not simply marks for every breath.

Draft title and sentence: “a trip to canberra. We took a sun hat a red bag and water.” In the stated title-case model: “A Trip to Canberra”. The list sentence is “We took a sun hat, a red bag and water.”

Try it · Create a title using the given title-case model and a sentence listing three complete items. Explain one capital and one comma.

Accept a title following the declared model and a clear three-item list. Credit appropriate name capitals and either clear serial-comma convention unless a style is specified. Do not split a multiword item.

Common mistakes

“Every title word must be capitalised.” Follow the chosen style and keep name capitals.

“Every pause needs a comma.” Find grammatical groups and actual list boundaries.

“A two-item list always needs a comma.” Two simple items usually use and without a separating comma.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make and edit a reading card

Write an original title using the stated title-case rule and a sentence listing four things needed for its story. Mark each whole list item, then insert commas and final and. Swap with a partner and explain the changes.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult models one title and a three-item list. Core: apply the stated title style and separate whole items. Stretch: compare sentence case with title case, or edit a list containing multiword items.

Use familiar words and proper names with confirmed spellings. Do not mark an unstated style preference as the only correct answer. These exercises concern title capitals and list commas, not every use of punctuation.

Three tricky questions

1. Is A Walk to the Pond the only valid way to style that title?

No. It matches the declared title-case model; sentence case can also be valid in another context.

2. Should a comma split red hat?

No. A red hat is one complete item.

3. Does a list sentence still need an end mark?

Yes. List commas do not replace the sentence’s final punctuation.

FAQ

What is a serial comma?

It is an optional comma before the final and in a list under some styles. Use the stated convention or accept a clear consistent alternative.

Why compare actual book titles?

They show how capitals identify names of works and how publishers’ styles can vary.

Titles use capital letters. Which additional words take capitals depends on the chosen style; names keep their capitals. Commas separate items in a list so the reader can see each item clearly.

Actual title and list editing cards

For this activity, use title case: capitalise the first word and main words; keep internal a, an, the, and, or, to and of lower-case unless part of a proper name. Title cards: “the red kite”; “a walk to the pond”; “mia and the moon”. Models: “The Red Kite”, “A Walk to the Pond”, “Mia and the Moon”. Another valid style is sentence case: “A walk to the pond”. Apply the declared style for the task. List draft: “We packed a red hat two blue pens a ruler and paper.” Whole items: a red hat / two blue pens / a ruler / paper. Edit: “We packed a red hat, two blue pens, a ruler and paper.” A clear serial comma before and is also accepted unless a stated style excludes it. Do not split red from hat or blue from pens. End the sentence with its full stop.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Capitalising every word automatically: Follow the title style and focus on important words rather than small joining words when appropriate.
  • Putting commas everywhere: Use commas specifically between items in the list.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
