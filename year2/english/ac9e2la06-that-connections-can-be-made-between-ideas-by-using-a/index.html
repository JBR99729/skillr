Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

A compound sentence joins two complete ideas. Conjunctions such as and, but, or and so show the relationship between the ideas.

Learning target: understand that connections can be made between ideas by using a compound sentence with 2 or more independent clauses usually linked by a coordinating conjunction

Big idea: Connect complete ideas with coordinating conjunctions

Learning routine: Find complete idea → Find second idea → Choose conjunction → Check meaning

4-Step Instructional Sequence

4. 4-Step Instructional Sequence

Step 1: Explicit Teaching & Modelling

Name the target in child-friendly language and demonstrate one concrete example connected to sentence, clause.

Step 2: Guided Analysis

Compare or practise a second example together. Students point to, say or show the clue.

Step 3: Collaborative / Independent Practice

Create or analyse one example together, then use a fresh familiar example independently.

Step 4: Formative Assessment

Use a one-minute check with a new example; require observable evidence rather than a guess.

Guided practice

  1. Sort and notice — Sort sentence halves into complete and incomplete ideas.
  2. Build or improve — Join pairs of complete ideas using different conjunctions and compare the meaning.
  3. Explain your choice — Use the words clause, conjunction, compound sentence to explain what you changed and why.
Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: using coordinating conjunctions; for example, “and”, “but”, “so” to construct compound sentences; for example, “The wolf huffed / and he puffed / and he blew the house down!”
Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Joining fragments: Each independent clause must express a complete idea.
  • Choosing any conjunction: Select the conjunction that shows addition, contrast, choice or result.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Worked example and reasoning checks

Choose the connection between two complete ideas

Join independent clauses with a conjunction that expresses their relationship.

Worked example

Join ‘The path was muddy’ and ‘We kept walking’ to show a contrast.

  1. Each part can stand as a sentence: The path was muddy. We kept walking.
  2. The second idea is unexpected after the first, so ‘but’ expresses the contrast.
  3. Write ‘The path was muddy, but we kept walking.’ Both clauses remain complete ideas.

Watch for this mistake: Adding ‘and’ does not always join two independent clauses. In ‘red and blue shoes’, it joins describing words, not two complete clauses.

Try it, then explain

  1. Join ‘I wanted to read’ and ‘The room was too noisy’ to show a contrast.

    Hint

    Choose a conjunction that means the ideas pull in different directions.

    Answer and explanation

    I wanted to read, but the room was too noisy.

  2. Does ‘The dog barked and the gate opened’ contain two independent clauses? Name them.

    Hint

    Try saying each side as its own sentence.

    Answer and explanation

    Yes: ‘The dog barked’ and ‘the gate opened’ can each stand independently.
