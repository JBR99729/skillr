Change sounds to make new words AC9E2LY09

Learning intention: I can hear the sounds in a word, change the sound asked for, then read and write the new word.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the other sounds in order

An adult says clamp. Hear five sounds: /k/, /l/, the short vowel in cat, /m/, /p/. Remove only /l/. Four sounds remain and blend to camp. After saying the answer, look at clamp and camp: the spelling records the change. For a new oral task, turn the screen away so the learner hears the word before seeing print.

Learn the skill

1. Blend and segment spoken words

Blend means join sounds to say a word. Segment means separate the spoken word into its sounds. Move one counter for each phoneme, even when two letters later spell that sound.

An adult models fresh as /f/, /r/, the vowel in bed, /sh/: four sounds. In spoon, /s/ and /p/ stay separate; oo later spells one vowel sound. Cloudy has two syllables when spoken naturally, but five phonemes: /k/, /l/, the vowel in cloud, /d/, the final vowel in happy. Syllables and phonemes count different things.

Try it · Adult: say stamp without showing its spelling. Learner: segment it with counters, then blend the sounds back together. How many counters do you need?

Five: /s/, /t/, the vowel in cat, /m/, /p/. Blending all five in order gives stamp. If a sound is missing, the adult models it and the learner tries again; record that support. Show the printed word only after the oral turn.

2. Remove exactly the sound named

Say the word, find the named sound, remove its counter and blend what remains. A sound may occur inside a consonant cluster. Keep the other sounds in their original order.

Say slip without its first /s/: lip remains. Say best without /s/: bet remains, because the final /t/ stays. Say tent without its final /t/: ten remains. These examples remove one phoneme each.

Try it · Adult: say bend. Ask the learner to say it without /n/, without showing the printed word. What word remains?

Bed remains: /b/, the vowel in bed, /d/. The middle /n/ was removed; the final /d/ was kept. Once the learner has said bed, ask them to write it and read their spelling back. Check oral manipulation and spelling separately.

3. Swap a sound, then link it to print

Substitute means swap the specified phoneme for another. Say and compare the old and new words. Then use known sound spellings to write the new word and reread it.

Change /r/ in fresh to /l/: flesh. Change the last /p/ in clap to /m/: clam. To change stack to stick, swap the vowel in cat for the vowel in sit; keep /s/, /t/, /k/ in order. An adult models actual sounds rather than adding a spoken uh after each consonant.

Try it · Adult: say slam and ask for its vowel to change to the vowel in sit. After the oral answer, ask the learner to write the new word and read it back.

Slim. Only the vowel changed. The spelling is s-l-i-m. If the oral answer is correct but the spelling is not, keep the successful sound change and teach the missing sound–letter match. Use the learner’s consistent local pronunciation.

Common mistakes

Counting letters instead of sounds In fresh, sh spells one sound. In its fr cluster, /f/ and /r/ are two sounds. Listen before counting.

Removing a whole cluster when one sound was named In clamp, removing /l/ leaves camp. Removing both /k/ and /l/ would be a different instruction.

Reading a displayed answer as proof of hearing the change Use a fresh adult-spoken turn with print covered, then a separate reading and writing turn.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Hear it, change it, write it

Use counters, paper and a pencil. The adult first models one sound change from the lesson. Turn the screen away for the fresh oral tasks in More examples. The learner says each result before seeing print, then writes and rereads it. Finish by asking which sound changed. Keep a note of independent responses and any model or prompt needed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: use counters with a modelled three-sound word. Core: remove or swap one phoneme in a cluster word. Stretch: perform two named changes in turn, explaining each, then write and reread the final word.

An adult must supply clear spoken sounds and accept consistent local pronunciation. Written notation and automatic text-to-speech are not substitutes for a checked sound model. Use an agreed accessible response mode and record the support.

Three tricky questions

Does sh in ship need two sound counters because it has two letters?

No. It spells one phoneme. Ship has three sounds: /sh/, the vowel in sit, /p/.

Say nest without /s/. What must stay at the end?

Net; /t/ stays at the end. Only /s/ is removed.

An adult says clap. Change only its last sound to /m/. Is the answer clam or camp?

Clam. Camp would also change the order and middle consonant sounds.

FAQ

Can a task remove more than one sound?

Yes, if it explicitly asks for that sequence. The beginning scr in scratch represents several phonemes; it must not be called one sound. First learn one specified change clearly.

How do I know the learner can do it independently?

Use a fresh spoken word without a displayed answer or advance reading model. Observe the actual response, then check writing and rereading separately.

Complete sound-card routine

Use counters or finger taps, not small loose objects with a learner who might put them in their mouth. The adult models fresh: /f/, /r/, the vowel in bed, /sh/. Four phonemes need four counters. The letters sh spell one sound; f and r in the cluster are separate sounds. Show fresh after the spoken model.

For a two-syllable model, say cloudy naturally and clap its two syllables. Then segment five phonemes with counters: /k/, /l/, the vowel in cloud, /d/, the final vowel in happy. Do not treat a whole syllable as one phoneme.

Adult oral-task card · keep the screen away from the learner

Say each word clearly and give only the spoken instruction. Do not read its answer first. 1. “Say slip without the first /s/.” 2. “Say best without /s/.” 3. “Say tent without the final /t/.” 4. “Say clap. Change the final /p/ to /m/.”

Check the oral tasks

1. Lip. 2. Bet, keeping final /t/. 3. Ten. 4. Clam. Observe what the learner actually says; reteach only the changed sound if needed. A supplied model counts as support.

Build, improve and explain a word chain

Adult models the spoken chain slam → slim → swim. First swap the vowel in cat for the vowel in sit. Next swap /l/ for /w/. All other sounds stay in order at each change. After the oral turns, show the print, write each word and reread it. Explain where the phoneme was changed.

For a fresh turn, the adult uses a different card from the formative check below with print covered. Reading this displayed chain is useful practice, but does not establish independent oral manipulation.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Counting letters instead of sounds: Say the word slowly; one phoneme may use more than one letter.
  • Changing multiple sounds: Hold the other phonemes steady and change only the target.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Quick-answer checks and fresh oral transfer

Check the earlier quick questions

A phoneme is a speech sound that can distinguish words. Frog has four phonemes: /f/, /r/, its vowel, /g/. Stop without /s/ is top. Changing the initial /m/ in map to /t/ gives tap.

Fresh adult oral card · keep print covered

Ask “Say plan without /l/.” Then ask “Say clap. Change its first /k/ to /f/.” Let the learner answer aloud before showing print. Ask them to write and reread each resulting word. For Stretch, say slam, change its vowel to the vowel in sit, then change /l/ to /w/, one step at a time.

Check the fresh responses

Plan without /l/ gives pan. Clap with initial /f/ gives flap. The two-step chain gives slim, then swim. Accept the learner’s consistent local sounds; written results are pan, flap and swim. Oral manipulation, written encoding and rereading each require their own observed response.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LY09: manipulate more complex sounds in spoken words and use knowledge of blending, segmenting, phoneme deletion and phoneme substitution to read and write words

  • blending and segmenting sounds in words; for example, “b-r-o-th-er” or “c-l-ou-d-y”
  • deleting and substituting sounds (phonemes) in spoken words to form new words; for example, delete the initial “scr” in “scratch” and substitute new initial sounds (phonemes) to form words such as “catch”, “batch” and “hatch”; substituting a medial sound (phoneme) to form a new word; for example, “stack” becomes “stick”
