WORKED EXAMPLE

A prediction can change after a new clue

Original text: “Nell reached the garden with a seed envelope. Rain began, so Nell waited under a shelter.” A supported prediction is that she may wait for the rain to stop before planting. It is a possibility, not a fact already stated. The next line, “When the rain stopped, Nell planted the seeds,” confirms this particular prediction.

Learn the skill

1. Visualise, predict and connect

Imagine the stated scene, use relevant knowledge and predict from clues. Keep invented details separate from what the text actually says. Check predictions as you read.

Title: Seeds for the garden. Before reading, possible topic words include garden or seed. The complete station-and-garden story is in More examples.

Try it · After reading, which predicted words actually occur? Name one stated detail and one imagined detail that is not supplied.

Check the real passage. Garden and seed-related words occur; other predictions may not. Nell’s rain shelter is stated, but a chosen coat colour is not. A relevant connection can help explain meaning without making it a new text fact.

2. Combine print, an image and prior knowledge

Literal meaning is directly stated. An inference links actual clues with relevant knowledge. Check that it fits and acknowledge what remains unknown.

Words: “The child filled the tray.” Inspect the actual illustration below before making a picture claim.

Try it · What does the picture add? What supports the idea of a bird feeder, and what can you not know yet?

It shows seeds pouring into a hanging tray and a bird nearby. These clues support the feeder inference. They do not prove the bird’s next action or species. The text does not give the child’s name. Accept other supported observations; keep an invented next event labelled as a prediction.

3. Summarise, question and repair understanding

Keep the main idea and important connected events. Ask a useful question about missing information; reread when a proposed meaning conflicts with the text.

The organiser below gives the shorter seed story’s order. The complete longer story, a fact card and listening directions are in More examples.

Try it · Give a short summary. What is wrong with “Nell planted before the rain stopped”? Ask one useful unanswered question.

Nell brings seeds, waits through rain and plants after it stops. The proposed order conflicts with the actual ending. A question such as what grew later is unanswered by this story. Accept another relevant question; do not invent its answer as a stated fact.

Common mistakes

“An inference is any guess.” Link a real clue and useful knowledge, then check consistency.

“A summary repeats every detail.” Keep the main idea and essential connected events.

“A mental picture is all in the text.” Distinguish actual details from what you imagined.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Before, during and after reading

Use the complete story in More examples. Predict likely topic words, visualise a scene, connect one relevant idea and check a prediction. Summarise the events with an organiser, then ask a useful question. Listen to the actual fact card and directions and check the stated details.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult reads and models a clue–idea link. Core: explain a supported inference and short summary. Stretch: revise an inference after a new clue or explain why an unanswered question remains open.

An adult may read when comprehension is the target and accept speech, drawing, AAC or writing. Record support. The feeder picture is for interpreting evidence; it does not require feeding wildlife or taking a real train trip.

Three tricky questions

1. Can two predictions both be reasonable?

Yes, if both fit the clues. Later text may confirm one, reject one or leave both open.

2. Does the fact card’s four shoots mean the other seeds can never grow?

No. It records what was visible on the stated day.

3. Can an organiser help with information as well as stories?

Yes. Labels, groups or sequence arrows can show the main information and its relationships.

FAQ

Must I use every strategy on every sentence?

No. Choose a strategy for the reading need and explain how it helps.

What if my personal connection differs from another reader’s?

That is fine. Keep the link relevant and check any claim about the text against its evidence.

Complete story, fact and direction material

Story title: Seeds for the garden. “Nell packed a seed envelope for a train trip. At the station she checked the platform sign. She arrived near the garden just as rain began. Nell put the envelope under her coat and waited under a shelter. When the rain stopped, she planted the seeds.” Before reading, predict possible topic words such as station or garden, then check which actually occur. Literal: Nell waits under a shelter. Inference: keeping the envelope under her coat may protect it from rain; this is supported, not a quoted reason. Visualise the stated place and actions without treating imagined colours as facts. Summary: Nell travels with seeds, waits for the rain to stop, then plants them. Sequence cards: packed seeds / reached rainy garden / waited / planted. Fact card: “Our class planted six seeds on Monday. Four shoots were visible on Friday.” Listen for the stated numbers and days; do not infer that the other seeds can never grow. Directions: draw a rectangle, write Garden inside it, then put a star above it. Listen and follow the actual sequence, ask about an unclear word, and check the finished response. A relevant connection helps understanding; a personal story should not replace the text clues.

Combine the actual picture and words

Read “The child filled the tray” and inspect the picture supplied here. Seeds pouring into a hanging tray, with a bird nearby, support the inference that it is a bird feeder. The text alone does not identify the tray’s purpose. It does not prove the bird eats next or identify a species. This is evidence discussion, not an instruction to feed wildlife.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Prediction as wild guess: Base predictions on title, images, events and known patterns.
  • Summary includes every detail: Keep only the main idea and most important supporting points.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

The sky turns dark and Dad brings the washing inside. What is a supported prediction?

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LY05: use comprehension strategies such as visualising, predicting, connecting, summarising, monitoring and questioning to build literal and inferred meaning

  • listening for specific information and providing key facts or points from an informative or persuasive text
  • listening and responding to detailed instructions
  • integrating information from print, images and prior knowledge to make supportable inferences
  • identifying the main idea of a text
  • predicting vocabulary that is likely to be in a text, based on the topic and the purpose of the text; for example, predicting that “station” and “arrive” would be in a text recounting a train journey
  • using prior knowledge to make and confirm predictions when reading a text
  • using graphic organisers to represent the connections between characters, order of events or sequence of information
