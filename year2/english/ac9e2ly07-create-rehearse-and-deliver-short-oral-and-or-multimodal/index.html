YEAR 2 ENGLISH

Prepare, rehearse and share a short talk AC9E2LY07

Learning intention: I can organise a short presentation, practise with a listener and adjust my delivery to help my audience.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A useful opening tells listeners what is coming

Original presentation for classmates: “Today I will explain how our reading box works. First, choose one book from the box. Next, return it with its cover facing the front. This helps the next reader find a book. Thank you for listening.” The opening names the topic; first and next organise the directions; the ending explains their purpose. An adult models it aloud, pausing between the steps. Reading these delivery notes alone does not demonstrate speaking.

Learn the skill

1. Plan for a familiar audience and purpose

Choose what the listeners need to know. Put an opening, related points and an ending in a useful order. Use accurate topic words and language that suits the occasion. More formal school language can be clear and polite without using difficult words.

Casual rehearsal note: “Here’s our book box.” A clear opening for a visiting class is “Welcome. I will explain how to choose and return a book.” Both can suit their contexts. The second states the purpose for visitors.

Complete original presentation and useful cue

For classmates: “Today I will explain how our reading box works. First, choose one book from the box. Next, return it with its cover facing the front. This helps the next reader find a book. Thank you for listening.”

Facts for this model: the box is beside the mat; it holds books for quiet reading; return each book with its cover facing the front.

Cue to make and use: draw three boxes, label them Choose, Read and Return, and connect them in that order. Point to the relevant box as you explain it. The cue shows the sequence; it does not stand in for speaking.

Try it · Use these model facts for a short class report: the reading box is beside the mat; it holds books for quiet reading; return each book with its cover facing the front. Give an opening, two useful points and an ending.

For example: “Today I will describe our reading box. It is beside the mat and holds books for quiet reading. Please return each book with its cover facing the front. That helps our class use the box.” Other clear sequences are valid. Keep the supplied facts and explain the purpose.

2. Rehearse with a useful cue and a listener

A cue reminds you what comes next. It can be a few words, a drawing or a simple diagram. Rehearse the actual talk, ask what the listener understood and change one feature before trying again.

Cue words for the reading-box demonstration: “Choose → Read → Return”. Draw three boxes with those labels and point to each as you explain it. If a listener says “I missed how to return the book”, pause before that step and explain the cover direction again.

Rehearse, listen and improve

  1. Deliver the complete reading-box talk to a familiar listener, using your three-box cue.
  2. Ask “Where does the book go when you finish?” and “Was there a part you wanted me to explain again?”
  3. Listen to the actual response. Choose one change, such as pausing before the return step or making cover facing the front clearer.
  4. Practise and deliver the revised version. Ask whether the change helped.

Observer notes: Was the topic clear? Could the listener follow the order? Were the topic words useful? Was the message audible or accessible, with helpful pace and pauses? Record any modelling, prompting, script, AAC or other support used.

Try it · Make the three-box cue on paper. Rehearse your talk, ask “What should you do when you finish reading?” and use the listener’s answer to choose one improvement.

The listener should explain the return step. If it is unclear, change the words, cue or pause, then rehearse again. Note the actual feedback and the change made. A good-looking cue is support for the talk; it does not by itself show that the talk was delivered.

3. Deliver with purposeful tone, volume and pace

Tone is the attitude you convey; volume helps the audience hear; pace and pauses give time to follow. Adjust these to the setting and listener. There is no single pitch pattern or loudness that fits every speaker and occasion.

An adult models “Please put the books here” as a friendly invitation, then as a calm reminder to handle the books carefully. Discuss how each delivery fits its situation. For the reading-box talk, use an audible, comfortable volume and pause between choose and return.

Two purposes; purposeful delivery

Welcome to a visiting class: “Welcome to our reading corner. I will show you how our reading box works.”

Care reminder: “Please put the books here. Keep their pages flat so the next reader can use them.”

An adult models each aloud in its stated situation. Then model the same words, “Please put the books here”, as a friendly invitation and a calm care reminder. The learner tries each and asks the listener which intention was clear.

Tone conveys intended attitude, volume supports hearing and pace controls the rate and pauses. Discuss actual listener feedback; no printed mark can specify a single correct pitch pattern for every voice.

Try it · Deliver the short reading-box presentation to a familiar listener. Try the welcome and care reminder too. Ask what the listener understood and whether the pace and volume helped.

Look for an organised message, useful topic words and delivery that suits the stated purpose. Accept different natural voices and appropriate access modes. If a listener misses a step, slow or pause there, clarify it and try again. Judge the actual performance, not a printed choice about delivery.

Common mistakes

“Louder is always clearer.” Use comfortable volume for this listener and room. Clear wording, sensible pace and useful pauses also help.

“A cue must contain every word.” Short words or a meaningful drawing can help you keep the order. A full script is also useful support while learning.

“Rehearsing means saying it unchanged again.” Listen to specific feedback, improve one useful feature and try the changed version.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Plan it, practise it, share it

Use the complete book-return or paper-fold presentation card in More examples. Prepare an opening, ordered points, a useful cue and an ending. Rehearse with an adult or partner, ask a specific question about what they understood, improve one feature and deliver the changed version.

Adjust the challenge Starter: an adult models a different short talk and helps you use two cues. Core: organise and deliver your own short talk with a cue and one feedback-based improvement. Stretch: adapt the same message for a familiar visiting class, explaining changes to your opening, tone or pace.

Use spoken language, AAC or agreed communication support. Record what was modelled or prompted. Do not grade eye contact, accent, standing still or a fixed speaking speed. Written planning can support the learning; actual delivery needs observation in the chosen mode.

Three tricky questions

1. Can the same words sound welcoming or like a reminder?

Yes. Context and delivery can change the attitude listeners hear. Model both, ask the listener what they understood and avoid treating one pitch pattern as a universal rule.

2. Does reading every cue prove the audience understood?

No. Ask the listener to explain the main point or steps. Use their response to check and improve clarity.

3. Must I keep speaking if the listener is confused?

Pause, find the unclear part and explain or show it differently. That is a useful delivery adjustment.

FAQ

How long must the talk be?

Keep it short enough to organise and deliver comfortably. The goal is a clear purposeful presentation, not a universal time or speed target.

Can a visual help?

Yes, if you actually refer to it and it explains or cues something useful. A decorative picture alone does not organise the message.

A different presentation to prepare

Familiar audience: a classmate. Purpose: explain how to make a folded paper card. Materials: one piece of scrap paper; no scissors.

Given sequence: put the paper flat; bring one short edge to the other short edge; press along the fold; open the card to show the inside.

Prepare your own opening, explain the steps in order and finish by naming what you made. Make a cue with four numbered boxes or demonstrate with the paper. Rehearse, ask which step was least clear, improve it and deliver again.

What should a listener understand?

They should recognise the card-making goal and follow the supplied order. A useful ending names the folded card. Accept different clear wording and appropriate access modes. Observe actual delivery and the feedback-based change.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Relying on words alone: Use a useful cue or script and check what the audience understands; support needs may differ.
  • Loud equals clear: Use suitable volume plus steady pace and precise articulation.
  • Answer without evidence: Use the actual presentation and listener response as evidence; a printed delivery choice alone is not a performance.

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Present ideas clearly using structure, vocabulary, tone, volume and pace
  • Use the routine: Plan → Create support → Rehearse → Deliver → Reflect
  • Before answering: use the actual talk, useful cue and listener feedback to explain one delivery choice.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

A useful reflection

After the actual presentation, complete: “My listener understood ___. I changed ___ because ___. In the next attempt I will ___.” Use real feedback. The reflection supports assessment but does not replace the presentation.
