Link ideas with clear references and omissions AC9E2LA04

Learning intention: I can track who or what words mean and recover words left out of a clear context.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep the person and the owned thing clear

“Mia found her hat. She put it on. The hat was hers.” Her, She and hers refer to Mia; it refers to the hat. The words connect the sentences without repeating Mia and hat every time. If another person could be meant, use the name again.

Learn the skill

1. Track personal and ownership words

A personal pronoun can stand for a person or thing. Ownership forms connect an item to its owner. Use the whole context, not just the nearest noun.

“The eggs were in a bowl. Jo moved them carefully.” Them refers to the eggs, not the bowl. “Jo found his bag. The bag was his.” His refers to Jo in this stated example.

Try it · In “Ava has a kite. It is hers”, what do It and hers connect to?

It refers to the kite; hers shows that it belongs to Ava. Accept an explanation that restores the complete meanings: the kite is Ava’s. Do not infer anyone’s pronouns from a name alone; follow the supplied text.

2. Restore words understood from context

Sometimes words can be left out because the reader can supply them. Restore the words first to check that the shortened text keeps the meaning.

“Alex chose red paint. Sam chose blue.” The second sentence means Sam chose blue paint. In “Who watered the seeds?” “Jo did”, did stands with the understood earlier action.

Try it · Expand the reply in “Who packed the bag?” “Nell did.”

“Nell packed the bag.” The earlier question supplies the action and object. This kind of omission is different from writing three dots; not every incomplete phrase is made clear by leaving words out.

3. Repair reference when needed

Choose pronouns or omissions only when the intended meaning remains clear. Related words can track a person in different roles, but not every related noun names the same person.

“Mia met Ava. She carried the bag.” She could mean either person. If Ava carried it, write “Ava carried the bag.”

Try it · Repair that draft if Mia carried the bag. Explain why the repair helps.

“Mia met Ava. Mia carried the bag.” The repeated name removes the ambiguity. Repeating a noun is useful when it clarifies meaning; removing every repeated word is not the goal.

Common mistakes

“A pronoun means the nearest noun.” Check number, meaning and the wider text.

“Every repetition should disappear.” Repeat a name when it prevents confusion.

“Any missing word is understood.” Restore it from the actual surrounding text and check.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Trace, expand and repair

Use the complete Mia, Alex and Jo texts in More examples. Draw arrows from reference words to their people or things. Expand one shortened reply. Rewrite one unclear sentence with the intended name supplied.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult highlights a name and its replacement. Core: track personal/ownership forms and restore an omission. Stretch: explain two possible readings of an ambiguous reference, then choose a clear revision.

Reference tasks concern meaning in the supplied text. Allow spoken explanation, arrows, AAC or writing. A grammar label is useful only when the child can explain what the word connects to.

Three tricky questions

1. Does hers replace a name alone?

It expresses an owned thing belonging to the person identified in context, such as Ava’s kite.

2. Is leaving paint out always clear?

Only when the surrounding text supplies the intended paint meaning.

3. Can a repeated name be the best choice?

Yes. Clarity can matter more than avoiding repetition.

FAQ

What does cohesive mean?

The parts of a text connect so a reader can follow the meaning.

Do we need advanced ellipsis terminology?

No. Show the understood words in a short familiar text and explain how the reader recovers them.

Actual connected texts for reference and omission

Repetitive draft: “Mia found Mia’s hat. Mia put Mia’s hat on. The hat was Mia’s.” Clear version: “Mia found her hat. She put it on. The hat was hers.” Her, She and hers refer to Mia; it refers to the hat. Alex says: “I chose red paint. Sam chose blue.” The omitted word after blue is paint. Dialogue: “Who watered the seeds?” “Jo did.” The reply means Jo watered the seeds. Ambiguous draft: “Mia met Ava. She carried the bag.” Either person could be She; name the intended person to repair it. Association chain: “Isy is a student. Her sister calls her Isy. Isy says, ‘I enjoy reading.’” Track Isy/her/I without assuming that every related noun means the same person.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Unclear pronoun reference: Repeat the noun if the reader may not know who she, he, they or it means.
  • Removing too many words: Omit words only when the missing meaning is obvious.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Earlier worked model

Read: 'The chicks hatched today. They are fluffy.' What does 'They' refer to?

Answer: the chicks

They carries the reference to the plural noun chicks into the next sentence.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LA04: understand how texts are made cohesive by using personal and possessive pronouns and by omitting words that can be inferred

  • identifying language used to build information across a text; for example, through connecting similar and dissimilar things
  • mapping examples of word associations in texts; for example, words that refer to the main character in a story: “Isy”, “she”, “I”, “sister”, “student”
  • tracking how a person or thing is identified through a section of a text; for example, “eggs”, “they”, “them”
  • identifying words left out that can be inferred from the surrounding text; for example, “Xanthe went to school. She had a lovely day.” (at school is inferred)
  • using personal and possessive pronouns to link entities previously mentioned in the text
