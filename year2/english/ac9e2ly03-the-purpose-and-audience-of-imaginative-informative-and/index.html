Find a text’s purpose and audience AC9E2LY03

Learning intention: I can explain what a text aims to do and who its choices address.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Topic, purpose and audience ask different questions

Original advertisement: “Families, join our Saturday library story club. Share a story together and discover a new favourite book!” Topic: a library story club. Main purpose: encourage participation. Intended audience clue: Families. Other people can read it too; the named audience is not a ban on other readers.

Learn the skill

1. Find what the text aims to do

Read the whole text. Imaginative texts can entertain or explore ideas; informative texts give information; persuasive texts try to influence a reader. A text can have more than one purpose.

Compare: “The books whispered to the moon boat”; “The library opens at nine”; “Join our Saturday story club!”

Try it · Which asks the reader to take action? Which gives an opening-time fact?

Join our Saturday story club encourages action. The library opening sentence informs. The moon-boat line imagines an event. Use these actual clues, rather than a rule that a certain picture or format proves a purpose.

2. Identify intended readers from clues

Look at direct address, information needed, wording and context. A sign, advertisement or poster can have different purposes.

Sign: “Library visitors: return borrowed books in this box.” Advertisement: “Families, join our Saturday library story club.”

Try it · Who does each address, and what does it ask them to do?

The sign addresses library visitors returning books; the advertisement addresses families and encourages joining the club. These are intended-reader clues, not claims that nobody else can read the messages.

3. Discuss a named First Nations author’s purpose

Use the actual work and its specific creator. Do not assume all books by a group of authors have one purpose.

Prepare Jasmine Seymour’s Open Your Heart to Country using the lawful book or authorised ABC author reading named in More examples. The work invites reconnection with Country, family and language.

Try it · Which actual clue supports an invitation or connection purpose? Can it also help readers learn?

Point to a real part of the supplied work and explain the purpose it supports. More than one purpose may be justified. The brief description here is not the full book, and no ceremony or personal family disclosure is required.

Common mistakes

“Purpose is the same as topic.” Topic is what it is about; purpose is what it aims to do.

“Every poster persuades.” A poster may inform, direct, invite or persuade; inspect the actual text.

“Audience means everyone else is excluded.” Intended readers are suggested by choices; others may still read it.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Give a purpose, audience and clue

Sort the complete texts, sign and advertisement in More examples. Explain one purpose and intended audience with a real clue. With the prepared named literary work, discuss another supported purpose.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose between an imagined event and a stated fact. Core: connect purpose and audience with evidence. Stretch: explain two purposes in one text or rewrite an original message for a different reader.

An adult can read and prepare the actual source. Attribute First Nations creators and Country correctly; do not infer cultural meaning from identity alone or imitate restricted cultural practices.

Three tricky questions

1. Does a photograph prove a text is informative?

No. Advertisements, stories and informative texts can all use photographs.

2. Can an invitation inform and persuade?

Yes. It may give event facts while encouraging attendance.

3. Is every First Nations text written for the same audience?

No. Examine the specific work and its choices.

FAQ

Must a text have only one purpose?

No. Explain its main purpose or supported combination.

Can a sign be helpful without an image?

Yes. Its actual words and context may clearly identify readers and an action.

Ask at the desk if you need help.” Persuasive advertisement: “Families, join our Saturday library story club. Share a story together and discover a new favourite book!” Sign A: “Library visitors: return borrowed books in this box.” Sign B: “Story club meets here at ten.” Ask what each text helps a reader know, imagine or do, and who the words address. A sign can direct or inform, and a poster can advertise or tell facts. Form alone does not determine purpose. Another valid purpose or audience is welcome when supported by the full supplied text.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Purpose equals topic: The topic is what the text is about; purpose is what it aims to do.
  • Guessing audience from pictures only: Use vocabulary, detail, design and context together.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh, code-aligned example. The student should show the exact word, sound, sentence, image feature, audience clue or text evidence that supports the answer.
