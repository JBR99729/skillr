WORKED EXAMPLE

Contents and an index answer different needs

In the prepared mini-book below, the contents list gives “Feeding a rabbit — 8”. The index gives the more specific “Water — 8”. The supplied printed page 8 says water is in the white bowl in this class model. Page numbers guide the search; reading the destination checks the answer.

Learn the skill

1. Use chapter and contents order

A chapter is a section of a book. The contents list shows its sections in book order and where they begin. Choose a broad heading that fits the information need.

Use the complete prepared contents and page extracts below. The chapter numbers 1, 2 and 3 are different from the printed page numbers 3, 8 and 14.

Try it · Which chapter and page begin information about feeding? Check the supplied page extract.

Chapter 2, Feeding a rabbit, begins on printed page 8. The actual extract discusses the model’s feed and water bowls. The number 2 names the chapter, not its page.

2. Use an alphabetical index

An index lists specific terms, usually alphabetically, with page references. Check later letters when two terms start alike, then follow the correct reference.

For the same mini-book, Bedding comes before Bowls: after B, compare e and o. Cleaning, Home and Water follow in alphabetical order. The page extracts are in focus 1.

Try it · Find the specific term Water and use its page reference. Which bowl holds water in this model?

Water points to printed page 8; the white bowl holds it in the supplied model text. Do not guess from a heading alone. On an actual PDF, printed page numbers may differ from the viewer’s sheet counter.

3. Navigate and verify on screen

A side-bar lists routes; a drop-down or expandable menu reveals choices; a link moves to a destination. Use its label, then check the heading and actual information.

Common mistakes

“Chapter number equals page number.” Read the contents columns carefully.

“Contents and index are identical.” One follows section order; the other usually sorts specific terms alphabetically.

“A click finishes the search.” Check that the destination answers the original need.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Find three things and explain your routes

Use the actual prepared book extracts to find the feeding chapter, water-bowl colour and bedding location. Then use the native screen menu to visit both topics and return. Explain why each chosen route helped.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult models one route. Core: locate a chapter and specific index term, then follow real links. Stretch: compare two routes to the same information or explain a printed-page/viewer-counter mismatch.

This is a prepared teaching model, not complete rabbit-care advice. Use adult-approved websites for further navigation. Observe actual book or screen actions without a speed race.

Three tricky questions

1. Must an index follow chapter order?

No. Its terms are usually alphabetical, even when their page references jump around.

2. Does a menu label prove the answer?

No. Open the destination and check its information.

3. Can two routes reach the same page?

Yes. A broad contents heading and a precise index term can point to the same chapter.

FAQ

Can a paper website drawing show screen navigation?

It can help plan a route, but observe the actual screen action separately.

What if the first route is unhelpful?

Return, choose a more useful heading or term and check again.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Navigation features help readers locate information without reading every page or screen. Different features suit different searches.

Big idea: Use chapters, contents pages, indexes, menus and links efficiently

Learning routine: Decide what you need → Choose navigation feature → Follow location → Check information

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Reading from page one every time: Use navigation features to move directly to likely information.
  • Confusing contents and index: Contents lists sections; an index lists specific terms, usually alphabetically.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
