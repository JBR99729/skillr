Plan, write and improve a short text AC9E2LY06

WORKED EXAMPLE

A precise edit helps the reader

Original draft: “Mila put a thing in the garden. She was carrying it.” The reader cannot tell what the thing is or which action came first. Revised: “Mila was carrying a small seed tray. She put the tray in the garden.” Naming the tray and putting the carrying before the placing makes the events clear. The noun group “a small seed tray” names and describes the object; the whole verb group “was carrying” tells what Mila was doing.

Learn the skill

1. Choose a purpose and organise ideas

Decide who will read your text and what you want it to do. A story links events; information groups facts; persuasion states a view and gives a relevant reason. Read the three complete original model texts below.

For classmates, the story follows a lost note and its return. The library information groups facts under headings. The class request asks for a reading box and explains its benefit. Each has a different job.

Three complete original text models

Story for a classmate — The missing note. Mila carried a note to the school garden. A breeze blew it under a bench. She asked Amir to help, and he found it beside a plant pot. Mila thanked him and put the note safely in her bag.

Information for a new classmate — Our model class library.

Where books go: Our class library has two shelves. Story books go on the upper shelf. Information books go on the lower shelf.

How to return them: Put a book back with its cover facing the front. This helps the next reader see its title.

Request to our teacher — A reading box. Please put a reading box beside the mat. We could choose books without crossing the room, and we could return them after quiet reading. A box there would help our class find a book. Could we try it this week?

These are invented teaching situations. Notice the story’s linked events, the information headings and the request with a relevant reason. Sort the planning cards below by their purpose, then explain a match.

Try it · Plan a short story for a classmate: A paper hat blows away in the playground. Who helps, what happens next and how does it end?

Accept different coherent plans. For example: Jo loses the hat; Lee catches it beside the bench; Jo thanks Lee and puts it in a bag. The response solves the problem. Write the linked events so a reader can follow them, rather than listing unrelated ideas.

2. Make sentences and word groups work

A simple sentence can express one complete message. To make a compound sentence, join two messages that can each stand alone, using a link such as and, but or so. Choose words that make the connection clear.

“The box was full.” is simple. “The box was full, so Amir chose another box.” joins two complete messages and shows a result. In “The small seed tray was resting on the shelf”, “The small seed tray” is a noun group and “was resting” is the whole verb group.

Sentences and word choices

Simple: The small seed tray was resting on the shelf.

Noun group: The small seed tray. The words work together to name and describe a thing. Whole verb group: was resting. Keep both words when identifying what was happening.

Compound: The soil was dry, so Mila added water. “The soil was dry” and “Mila added water” can each stand alone; so links the first situation to its result.

Contrast: Mila packed seeds and soil. And joins two things here, not two complete messages. A comma is not needed just because and appears.

Word bank: seed tray, soil, shelf, basket, garden, paper. Basket, garden and paper each have two syllables in the model’s ordinary pronunciation. Say the word, notice familiar sound–letter patterns and check its conventional spelling.

Try it · Join “The path was muddy.” and “We wore boots.” to show a result. Then replace “thing” in “We filled the thing with soil” with a precise gardening word.

One answer is “The path was muddy, so we wore boots.” Both parts are complete messages; so shows the result. “We filled the seed tray with soil” uses a precise topic word. A pot or planter can also fit if that is the intended object. Keep the meaning accurate.

3. Reread, edit and make a multimodal version

Check meaning and order first. Add a missing detail, delete needless repetition or replace an unclear word. Then check sentence grammar, capitals, end marks and spelling. A pronoun such as she or it must point clearly to the intended person or thing.

Draft: “sam took the basket to the garden. He put it it by the gate” Revised: “Sam took the basket to the garden. He put it by the gate.” Sam needs a capital, one it is deleted and the final full stop is added. He refers to Sam; it refers to the basket. Basket and garden are common two-syllable words.

An edit you can explain

Draft: “mila took a thing to the garden. She put it it on the shelf”

Intended meaning: Mila carries a seed tray. She puts it on the shelf in the garden.

Revised: “Mila took a seed tray to the garden. She put it on the shelf.”

  • Replace thing with seed tray so the reader knows the object.
  • Delete the repeated it; keep one it to refer to the seed tray.
  • Add the opening capital and final full stop.

Read it back: do the actions stay in a sensible order, and do the words still convey the intended meaning?

Create and edit a simple digital text

With an adult, use a familiar drawing or document tool. Create two pages or panels: first Mila carries the seed tray; next she puts it on the shelf. Type a caption for each and draw a matching picture. Use these original starting captions: “Mila carries a seed tray.” and “She puts it on the shelf.”

  1. Create the two panels and captions.
  2. Check their order. If the shelf panel comes first, move it after the carrying panel.
  3. Replace an unclear word, such as thing, with seed tray; delete any repeated word and check capitals and full stops.
  4. Read the changed text with the adult. Check each picture matches its caption.
  5. Save locally with adult help. Share it only with the agreed familiar reader.

A paper drawing-and-caption version practises the same text choices. The digital elaboration requires actually making and revising a digital version; describing a button alone does not demonstrate it.

Try it · Edit “lena carried a thing to the garden. He put it by the shed” so Lena carries a seed tray. In this story Lena uses she. What should change?

Write “Lena carried a seed tray to the garden. She put it by the shed.” Name the intended object, use Lena’s capital and the supplied pronoun she, and finish both sentences. Then create a drawing and matching caption, or an adult-supported digital version, using the steps below.

Common mistakes

“Editing means fixing spelling only.” Check whether the ideas make sense and are in a useful order as well as checking spelling and punctuation.

“Every and makes a compound sentence.” “Mila packed seeds and soil” joins two things. It has one complete message. Check for two complete messages before calling a sentence compound.

“A longer word always improves writing.” Choose a precise word that helps this reader. Seed tray is useful when that is what you mean.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create, read back and improve

Choose one of the complete planning cards in More examples. Write a short story, information text or persuasive request for the named familiar reader. Read it back, make one meaning edit and one convention edit, and explain how each helps. Across later short sessions, try the other two purposes. Add a drawing with a useful caption if it helps your reader.

Adjust the challenge Starter: tell an adult your ideas, then write one sentence with a word bank. Core: write a short organised text with a simple sentence, a true compound sentence and a precise topic word. Stretch: change the purpose or reader and explain which structure and wording you revised.

An adult can read directions and help use a familiar device. Keep the learner’s own choices visible and note dictated or modelled support. The digital task saves locally with adult help; it needs no account, upload or personal information.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “Lena packed seeds and pots” a compound sentence?

Under this lesson’s primary-school convention, no. And links two objects, not two complete messages. “Lena packed seeds, and Kai carried the pots” has two complete messages.

2. Must I use the longest possible noun group?

No. Include the words that identify the intended thing clearly. “The blue tray” can be enough; extra details should serve the reader.

3. Can a text be correctly spelt but still need editing?

Yes. The events might be out of order, a pronoun might be unclear or a reason might not support the request.

FAQ

What makes a persuasive reason useful?

It connects to the request and matters to the reader. Different views can be supported; you do not have to share the model’s preference.

Do I need to make every version digitally?

No. Paper is a useful route. To practise the digital elaboration, use an adult-supported tool to actually create, rearrange and revise a text.

Check the planning matches

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Starting without purpose: Name the text type, purpose and audience first.
  • Editing only spelling: Also revise ideas, order, sentences and vocabulary.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
