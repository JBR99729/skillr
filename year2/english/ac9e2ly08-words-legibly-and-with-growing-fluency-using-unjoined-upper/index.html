Word spaces make a message easier to read

The typed examples below show spacing only: “Miahasabluebag.” and “Mia has a blue bag.” The second separates the five words. To practise handwriting, an adult first writes that sentence in the unjoined style taught at school. Watch the actual movements, then copy beside the model on suitable paper. A computer font is a target to read, not proof of handwriting or a stroke-order guide.

Learn the skill

1. Watch the taught movement, then write

Use a nearby school-approved unjoined letter model. Watch where each letter starts and how it moves, then write it yourself. Upper-case and lower-case forms can look different. Keep the letters unjoined and recognisable.

An adult writes “Mia” and “mia” using the learner’s taught forms. Compare the capital M with lower-case m, then watch each movement. The capital version can name a person. Copy both for a case comparison; an adult observes the actual writing.

Prepare a real handwriting model

Have suitable paper, a comfortable pencil or agreed adapted writing tool, and a nearby model in the learner’s school-taught unjoined script. An adult writes the target words slowly using that model, showing the actual starting points and movements. The learner watches, then writes beside it without tracing. The typed words here identify the targets only; they do not provide a stroke-order model.

Use the preferred writing hand. Adjust paper position and support the paper with the other hand if helpful. Look for comfortable control during writing, following individual school or specialist advice. Do not insist that every learner use one finger arrangement.

Complete handwriting targets

Case comparison: Mia and mia. The first can be a person’s name; the second is a case-comparison target. Watch the adult’s local-script capital M and lower-case m.

Word targets: Sam; bag; paper; garden. Watch each adult-written model and write it yourself with unjoined letters.

Sentence targets: Mia has a blue bag. / Sam has a red mug. / We put a bag here.

Copy one short target at a time beside the real model. Check the intended letter order, case, recognisable forms and word spaces. The task supplies the spellings so attention can stay on handwriting.

Try it · With an adult’s handwritten model beside you, write “Sam” and “bag” without tracing. What should the adult check?

The intended letters should be recognisable, in the supplied order and case, using unjoined taught forms. The adult also observes starting points and movement where these are being taught. Accept the school’s consistent forms rather than demanding an exact computer-font match.

2. Place letters and leave word spaces

Use the paper guides that help you. Letter bodies relate to a baseline; some letters reach up and parts of others reach below it. Keep letters close enough to form words, with clearer gaps between words.

An adult writes “a big bag” in the taught style on suitable lines. The a has a short body, b reaches up, and parts of g reach below the baseline. Compare this with the adult’s capital B. The whole letter does not have to stay above the line.

Check relative size and placement

An adult demonstrates a, b, g and B in the learner’s taught style on suitable lines. Compare the short body of a, the tall part of b and the part of g below the baseline. Capital B has a different form from lower-case b. Exact shapes follow the school model.

Then the adult writes “a big bag”. The learner copies it. Ask the adult to point to a readable short letter, a tall letter, a part below the baseline and the spaces between the three words. Keep letters within a word together, with clearer word gaps.

The baseline is a guide to placement. It does not mean forcing all parts of all letters above it. Use adapted guides if these support comfortable readable writing.

Try it · Copy the adult’s model “Sam has a red mug.” Leave clear word gaps and compare a short letter, a tall letter and a letter with a part below the baseline.

Look for recognisable unjoined words, sensible relative sizes and clear spaces. For example, a has a short body, d reaches up and g has a part below the baseline. Use the local model to check the actual forms; do not force every letter into the same height.

3. Build comfortable fluency and check your work

Practise a small useful target accurately, then use it in a word or sentence. A functional grasp lets you control the tool comfortably. Support your arm and paper in a position that works for you; different hands and suitable adapted tools may need different arrangements.

After writing “Mia has a blue bag.”, circle one clear word and choose one feature to improve, such as the gap after has. Write it again in another short session under similar conditions. Compare readable letter forms, gaps and ease of movement.

Compare actual attempts over time

Write the adult-modelled sentence “Mia has a blue bag.” Keep the dated sample. Circle one clear word and choose a target, such as the gap after has or the taught movement for b. Practise that feature briefly, then use it in the sentence.

In a later short session, repeat under similar conditions with the same useful model and tools. Compare recognisable forms, word gaps, hesitation, comfort and effort. An adult observes actual movement and grasp when these are being discussed. Better ease with readable words is more useful than rushing.

Note support and changed conditions. Two attempts made immediately after modelling show current practice; a series of comparable samples helps discuss growth. Stop if there is pain or strain.

Try it · Write the adult-modelled sentence “We put a bag here.” Then choose one strength and one improvement. What evidence would show growing ease over time?

Use the actual handwritten sample to identify a clear feature and a specific next step. Growing ease needs comparable attempts over time, with less hesitation or effort while the words remain readable. One quick or copied page alone does not prove growth. There is no single speed target for everyone.

Common mistakes

“A capital is just a bigger lower-case letter.” Some shapes differ. Watch and use the taught upper-case and lower-case forms.

“Every part of every letter stays above the line.” Parts of letters such as g, p or y can go below the baseline. Check the school model.

“Only one pencil grasp is allowed.” The aim is comfortable control. Follow individual school or specialist guidance and observe the learner writing.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Model, write, notice and retry

Prepare a comfortable pencil or agreed adapted tool, suitable paper and an adult’s school-style handwritten model. Write “Mia has a blue bag.” and “We put a bag here.” without tracing. Circle one clear word, choose one useful target and practise it briefly. Keep a dated sample and compare another short attempt later.

Adjust the challenge Starter: watch and copy one familiar word beside the model. Core: write a short mixed-case sentence with clear unjoined forms and spaces. Stretch: write a short label or message, then revise one feature while keeping the writing comfortable and readable.

Use the learner’s preferred writing hand and appropriate support. A comfortable functional grasp matters more than one prescribed finger arrangement. Stop and ask an adult for help if writing hurts or causes strain. Typing, selecting a picture or naming a grip does not demonstrate handwritten formation.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a neat typed answer prove my handwriting?

No. It can show the intended words. The handwritten result and, for movement or grasp, observation of writing provide the relevant evidence.

2. Should I make all letters exactly the same height?

No. Relative body height, tall parts and parts below the baseline vary. Follow the taught unjoined style and suitable line guides.

3. Is the fastest copy always the best?

No. Growing fluency means increasing ease while maintaining readable words, rather than rushing or comparing everyone with one speed.

FAQ

Must I change my writing hand?

No. Use the hand that works for you, with paper position and support that suit your needs.

What if my school’s a or g looks different from the screen?

Use the consistent form taught at school. The typed examples here show word targets or spacing, not a compulsory handwriting script.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Legible handwriting uses recognisable letter shapes, consistent direction, appropriate size, spacing and placement on the line. Fluency grows through accurate repeated practice.

Big idea: Form unjoined upper-case and lower-case letters consistently

Learning routine: Sit and position → Form letters → Space words → Maintain size → Review

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Prioritising speed: Slow down until formation and spacing are secure.
  • Inconsistent size: Use line guides and compare letters with similar height.
  • Answer without evidence: Use the real handwritten sample, and observe the writing process when checking movement or grasp.
