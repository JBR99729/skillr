WORKED EXAMPLE

Precision depends on the actual context

“The character spoke very quietly so the baby would not wake.” Whispered fits this stated soft speech. Shouted would change the action. A more precise word helps only when it matches the intended meaning; a long or impressive word can be a poor choice.

Learn the skill

1. Learn a topic word in context

Ask what the word names and how it fits the surrounding information. Explain it in everyday language before using it.

A thermometer is a tool for measuring temperature. In a temperature report, thermometer can replace the vague word thing when that is the actual tool used.

Try it · Choose a precise word for the tube carrying water to plants in a garden, using the supplied meaning: a hose is a flexible tube for carrying water.

Hose fits this context. Use the full sentence, such as “Water flows through the hose.” Do not choose a specialised word merely because it belongs to science; it must name the intended object.

2. Compare character and action words

Different nouns, adjectives and verbs can create different pictures. Check the actual character’s action and the writer’s intention.

“Nell hesitated at the doorway and spoke softly” can support timid, meaning shy or hesitant. “Nell shook when she heard the frightening noise” more directly supports frightened.

Try it · In a story about a character racing to catch a rolling ball, would strolled or hurried better fit the intended movement? Explain.

Hurried better matches moving quickly to catch it. Strolled suggests a more relaxed movement. Accept another accurate word with a reason; use actual context rather than a universal ranking of stronger words.

3. Choose words with their source and Country identified

First Nations language words can name particular places, actions or ideas. Learn from a verified creator or community source and keep that context clear.

For the Darug text Cooee Mittigar, adult uses the actual book and glossary. The publisher identifies Nura with Country and yana with walking in this source.

Try it · With the prepared book open, choose an actual topic word and explain its meaning using the glossary. Would you assume the same word is local everywhere?

Refer to the actual source and identify its language and creators. No: confirm local language and permitted use separately. The aim is a meaningful word choice with attribution, not an invented translation or a pronunciation quiz.

Common mistakes

“Choose the longest word.” Choose the accurate word that helps your reader.

“A related topic word always fits.” Check its exact meaning in the sentence.

“A cultural word is interchangeable everywhere.” Keep its particular language, source and Country clear.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Try a word and explain the difference

Use the actual garden and story paragraph in More examples. Replace one vague noun, verb or adjective with a word that fits the supplied context. Read both versions and explain the meaning gained or changed.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult explains two word choices with a sentence. Core: choose and justify a fitting word. Stretch: compare two valid choices for different intended effects or revise an overprecise unsupported description.

New vocabulary should be explicitly taught. Accept a clear everyday choice when it serves the purpose; do not reward unfamiliar language just for sounding advanced. Use actual approved cultural sources.

Three tricky questions

1. Does hurried always improve went?

No. It adds quickness, which must fit the intended action.

2. Are timid and frightened exact synonyms everywhere?

No. They can describe different responses; use the actual clues.

3. Can nice be useful?

Sometimes. If a particular detail matters, explain what is pleasant, peaceful or helpful instead.

FAQ

How can we check a new word?

Use a reliable dictionary or the source’s glossary, discuss its context and try it in a complete sentence.

Must a new story word be factually true?

In fiction, a writer may invent details deliberately. Distinguish that choice from describing evidence in an existing text.

Actual vocabulary contexts and revision paragraph

Garden topic: “Water goes through the long tube to the plants.” A garden hose is a flexible tube for carrying water; hose is a precise choice for this context. Story topic: “The character spoke very quietly so the sleeping baby would not wake.” Whispered fits that stated meaning; shouted would change it. Draft: “The person went to the quiet garden. The person said something softly. The place was nice.” One possible new fictional version: “The gardener walked to the peaceful garden. The gardener whispered.” This deliberately adds an occupation and new action details; it is a changed story, not a fact-preserving edit of the draft. For an edit that keeps the original facts, do not add those details. In a fictional story, magician, wizard and sorcerer can describe different imagined characters; use the actual author’s clues rather than assume the nouns always mean exactly the same thing. Timid describes a shy or hesitant response; frightened names fear. Choose the word that fits the actual action clues.

Read an actual Country-specific text with an adult

Supply a lawful copy of Cooee Mittigar by Jasmine Seymour and Leanne Mulgo Watson and use the publisher’s teacher notes and the book’s glossary. It is a Darug text: Nura refers to Country and yana to walking in this source. Read the actual pages and glossary together. Choose a word or setting detail actually present, explain it using that source, and refer to the real spread when discussing its effect. Nura concerns Country and relationships, not merely a generic location. Attribute the language and creators; do not assume these words are local everywhere, invent pronunciation, or reproduce cultural artwork. For local topic words, confirm a community-approved local source and its permitted use first.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Choosing the longest word: Choose the most accurate word, not the most complicated one.
  • Using topic words incorrectly: Check meaning and use the word in a sentence that makes sense.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
