Choose words for the situation and your role AC9E2LA01

Learning intention: I can explain how words change with the listener, situation and job I am doing.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The listener needs enough information

A familiar classmate knows the game: “Can I have the next turn?” A new visitor does not know the starting place: “Please stand behind the blue cone to start.” Both are respectful. The second adds information the visitor needs. Neither wording proves a person’s age, authority or background.

Learn the skill

1. Notice context and familiarity

Ask who is talking, what they want to do and what the listener already knows. Respectful language can sound relaxed, helpful or firm.

A classmate asks “Can you help me?” A visitor may need “Could you show me where the library is?” The useful difference is the information and shared knowledge.

Try it · Ask for help finding the art room, first from a friend who knows you, then from a new visitor.

Accept clear respectful requests that name the art room and suit the supplied familiarity. Explain a useful change or why the same wording could work. Do not demand one fixed formal phrase.

2. Match language to the interaction role

A host welcomes and guides, a leader explains a task, and a learner may ask for clarification. A person’s role can change during an activity.

Guide: “The reading table is beside the window.” Visitor: “Which window do you mean?” Guide: “The window next to the door.”

Try it · Act as the guide, then visitor. Repair “Put it there” when the object and place are unclear.

For example: “Put your bag on the shelf beside the door.” The listener now knows the object and place. Observe the actual exchange; accepting an answer on paper does not establish a spoken tone.

3. Explore cultural choices with the right source

Greetings and respectful expressions vary across languages and communities. Ask what a real expression means, when it is used and who has permission to use a particular protocol.

A Welcome to Country is delivered by Traditional Owners or Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people with their permission. Anyone can offer a respectful Acknowledgement of Country. These have different roles and purposes.

Try it · With an adult, examine an authorised local greeting and an actual protocol example. What context and role fit each?

Use the actual community-approved source and explain its stated meaning or purpose. A greeting used elsewhere is not automatically local. Compare roles; do not imitate a Welcome or invent a ceremony. The retained guide gives concrete adult preparation.

Common mistakes

“Polite means very formal.” Natural friendly wording can be respectful.

“A role stays fixed.” A leader can become a listener or ask for help.

“One cultural greeting fits everywhere.” Confirm the actual language, community, context and permitted use.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Same message, two listeners

Choose a request for a pencil. Say it to a familiar partner, then to an imagined new class visitor. Swap host and visitor roles, make one instruction clearer and discuss what each listener needed to know.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult supplies the context and a request stem. Core: choose and explain clear wording. Stretch: change roles or repair a misunderstanding while keeping the intended message.

Observe actual communication and record prompts. Do not grade accent, eye contact, social status or a family’s greeting customs. Use verified cultural sources with adult and local guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Can the same words suit a friend and teacher?

Yes. Explain whether they are respectful and clear in each actual context.

2. Is “please” enough to fix “Put it there”?

No. The listener may still need the object and location.

3. Can any visitor perform a Welcome to Country?

No. Use the permission-bearing protocol roles taught above and local guidance.

FAQ

Must every response be spoken?

Writing or AAC can support reasoning and communication; record any speech-specific feature not observed.

Where do we find a local greeting?

Start with the school’s approved local First Nations language source or local language centre. Confirm meaning and appropriate use before teaching it.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

People change their language depending on who they are speaking with, what is happening and the role each person has. Respectful language can still sound friendly, formal, helpful or firm.

Big idea: Choose words and tone to suit the context and the roles people take

Learning routine: Notice context → Identify roles → Choose words → Check tone

Actual speech cards and a repair

Context A: two familiar classmates share a game. Card 1: “Can I have the next turn?” Context B: a visitor does not know where to put a bag. Card 2 from the student host: “You can put your bag on this shelf.” Context C: the team leader is explaining the starting place. Card 3: “Please stand behind the blue cone.” Context D: a learner missed a word. Card 4: “Could you say the last word again?” Match each explicit purpose and role; some respectful wording could suit several listeners. Blunt draft: “Give me that.” Repair it for a familiar classmate and for a visitor, naming the needed object. Accept different clear respectful versions; do not sort a person’s social role from polite wording alone.

Prepare actual greetings and protocol examples

Adult uses a school-approved local First Nations language source or local language centre to confirm a greeting, its language, meaning, pronunciation model and appropriate use. Read or hear that actual model with the child; compare with another verified greeting from a family or community source. Record the sources; do not invent a local word or assume a language from a map boundary. A Welcome to Country is delivered by Traditional Owners or Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people with their permission. Anyone can offer an Acknowledgement of Country respectfully. Compare these purposes and roles using an authorised actual example. This is observation and discussion, not a classroom imitation of a Welcome or ceremony.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Using the same wording everywhere: Change the wording when the audience, purpose or role changes.
  • Polite means overly formal: Respectful language can be natural and age-appropriate.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Earlier worked model

You missed the last instruction because a truck passed the window. What could you say to the teacher?
