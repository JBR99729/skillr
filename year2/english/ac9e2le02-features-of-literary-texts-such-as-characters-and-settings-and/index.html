YEAR 2 ENGLISH

Explain which story features you prefer AC9E2LE02

Learning intention: I can identify a character or setting feature and give a reason for my own preference.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A preference connects a feature with a response

Original story: “Ollie the otter found a lost scarf by the school gate. Ollie put it on the class shelf so its owner could find it.” One reader says, “I like Ollie because returning the scarf is helpful.” Another may prefer an unusual setting. Personal taste can differ; a claim about an action must still fit the text.

Learn the skill

1. Identify a feature before judging it

Characters take part in a story. A setting gives where or when. An event tells what happens. Name the detail you are discussing.

“On the moon, Pip the fox guarded a silver garden. A robot asked for a seed.” Pip and the robot are characters; the moon garden is the setting; asking is an event.

Try it · Which supplied detail is a setting? Give a different character detail.

The silver garden on the moon is a setting. Pip is a fox guarding it, and the robot asks for a seed. Keep the feature and its evidence connected.

2. Give a preference and a relevant reason

Say what you prefer, then explain what that feature does for you. A familiar world or an unfamiliar one can each appeal to a reader.

Reader A: “I like the classroom story because helping a new friend feels welcoming.” Reader B: “I prefer the moon garden because I enjoy imagining unusual places.” The complete original stories are in More examples.

Try it · Choose one of those settings or characters. Explain your preference using a real detail from the supplied story.

Accept either choice with a connected reason. A child need not share the model reader’s taste or disclose a personal experience. “It is best” alone does not explain the feature.

3. Discuss animal behaviour and different responses

Story animals may act like people. Explain your response to a particular action, keeping it separate from a claim about all real animals.

In the complete Pip story, the fox first hides seeds from the robot, then shares one after noticing an empty pot.

Try it · How do you respond to hiding and sharing? Could two readers discuss the same action differently?

For example, a reader may dislike the hiding because it prevents sharing, yet appreciate the later change. Accept another supported response. The fictional fox’s behaviour does not show that every real fox is selfish or kind.

Common mistakes

“My favourite is the correct favourite.” Readers can prefer different features for valid reasons.

“Retelling is the whole response.” Add how a feature affects your preference.

“One action describes every animal.” Discuss this fictional character and this action.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare two reader responses

Read both complete stories and response cards in More examples. Name the feature each reader values. Make your own preference response with one detail, then listen to a different supported choice.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose a feature from the supplied cards. Core: connect a preference and a reason. Stretch: explain why two different responses can both make sense.

An adult may read the stories aloud or scribe a response. Judge the feature–reason connection, not agreement with the adult, handwriting or private experiences.

Three tricky questions

1. Can I prefer an unfamiliar world?

Yes. Explain which actual feature interests you and why.

2. Can I dislike a character’s action but enjoy the story?

Yes. A troubling action can create interest or help a character change.

3. Does “I like it because it is good” give much evidence?

No. Name a feature and explain its effect on you.

FAQ

Must I use the word because?

It is a useful starter, but another clear way of giving a reason is valid.

Can a picture be the feature I prefer?

Yes, when you can point to the actual supplied illustration and explain your response.

Two complete original stories and response cards

Story A: “Milo the mouse arrived in a new classroom. He could not find the book box. Tessa the turtle showed him the shelf. Later Milo saved Tessa a seat. They read together.” Story B: “On the moon, Pip the fox guarded a silver garden. A little robot asked for one seed. Pip hid the seed bag. Then Pip noticed the robot’s empty pot. Pip shared a seed, and together they planted it.” Feature cards: mouse helps turtle / new classroom / fox hides seeds then shares / silver moon garden. Sort character behaviour and setting; identify a problem or change when one is present. Reader 1: “I prefer A because the helpful characters make the new classroom feel welcoming.” Reader 2: “I prefer B because its unusual moon garden makes me curious.” Both preferences are valid and grounded in details. Discuss how you feel about Pip’s hiding and sharing; one action does not prove every animal behaves that way.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Retelling instead of responding: Name the feature and explain your reaction to it.
  • Preference without reason: Use because and refer to a detail.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Identify literary features and support preferences with reasons
  • Use the routine: Identify feature → Describe it → State preference → Give reason
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

Earlier worked model

Niko returns a lost wallet to its owner. Which character feature does this show?

Answer: honesty

Returning the wallet is an action that shows Niko is honest.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LE02: identify features of literary texts, such as characters and settings, and give reasons for personal preferences

  • discussing preferences for stories set in familiar or unfamiliar worlds, or about characters whose lives are like or unlike their own
  • discussing their feelings about the positive and negative behaviours of non-human characters, such as animals
