YEAR 2 ENGLISH

Combine what words and images tell you AC9E2LA08

Learning intention: I can explain what an actual image adds and check an inference against visible clues.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A picture can supply an unstated place

Words: “The kitten waited.” The actual illustration shows a kitten under a wooden chair in a room. Together they suggest it waits indoors under the chair. The words alone do not say that location. Neither the picture nor the sentence tells us its name, how long it waits or a certain hidden feeling.

Learn the skill

1. Separate written and visual clues

Read the actual words, then inspect the actual image. Name what each supplies before combining them. Treat an inference as supported thinking, not certainty.

Use the kitten image below with the word line “The kitten waited.” Visible chair legs, floor and walls support the indoor setting.

Try it · What does the illustration add that the words do not state? Can you know why the kitten waits?

It adds the place under a chair indoors. The reason for waiting is not supplied. A story writer could invent one, but label it as a new story detail rather than observed evidence.

2. Read information from a real graphic

A table or map can add facts that a short sentence leaves out. Read the headings, labels and values together.

Words: “Some seeds sprouted.” The supplied class table records the same week: tray A has 2 visible shoots; tray B has 5.

Try it · What extra information does the table give? Which tray has more visible shoots in the record?

It identifies each tray and gives its count; B has 5 compared with A’s 2, so B has more in this record. The table does not prove why the difference occurred or predict every future tray.

3. Explain action, reaction, speech and thought

Pictures can show poses or facial clues. Speech and thought bubbles use visual conventions to add spoken or unspoken meaning. Check exactly what is shown or authored.

Use the first story duck in the actual three-panel image below. Its step and open smiling bill can suggest a cheerful action. The separate bubble diagrams add two original possible story meanings.

Try it · Compare the supplied speech bubble with the thought bubble. How would each change the story?

The speech bubble makes “Come and read with me!” spoken to someone. The thought bubble represents “I wonder what we will read” as unspoken thinking. These are explicitly new diagram choices, not text already inside the reused artwork. Accept other evidence-supported reaction readings; do not claim a face proves one feeling.

Common mistakes

“Every picture is decoration.” Check for added location, action, reaction or information.

“An image proves a hidden feeling.” Explain visible clues and keep an inference qualified.

“Labels alone are enough.” Inspect the actual image, table values and words together.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Words first, then the complete picture

Read “The kitten waited” with the image covered. Say what you know. Reveal the real illustration and name two added visible details. Compare those observations with one possible inference, then explain what remains unknown.

Adjust the challenge Starter: adult points to one visible clue. Core: explain how words and image combine. Stretch: compare the duck’s speech/thought choices or explain a question the seed table cannot answer.

Use the actual supplied visual, with panning where needed on a small screen. Verbal descriptions and alt text support access but are not claims that an absent picture was inspected. Accept accessible ways to explain evidence.

Three tricky questions

1. Can you tell the kitten’s name from this picture?

No. No name is supplied.

2. Does the seed table explain the cause of different counts?

No. It records counts for the stated trays and week.

3. Is a thought bubble necessarily spoken aloud?

No. In this model it shows unspoken thinking, while a speech bubble shows spoken words.

FAQ

Must every text-image pair repeat the same information?

No. Images can add information, suggest a response or alter how the words are understood.

Can readers give different interpretations?

Yes, when each explains actual clues and distinguishes observation, inference and invention.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Calling every image decoration: Check whether the image adds information, mood or a clue.
  • Ignoring the written text: Meaning often comes from combining both modes.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LA08: understand that images add to or multiply the meanings of a text

  • identifying images and graphics in a text that add ideas or information not included in the written text; for example, a map or table in a factual text or an illustration in a story, which gives clues about the setting
  • identifying visual representations of characters’ actions, reactions, speech and thought processes in narratives, and considering how these images add to or multiply the meaning of accompanying words
