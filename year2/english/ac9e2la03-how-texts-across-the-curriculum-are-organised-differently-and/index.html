Worked example

Read: '1. Fold the paper. 2. Press the crease. 3. Open the paper.' Why are these instructions numbered?

Answer: To show the order of the actions.

Numbering guides the reader through a procedure in sequence.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

Texts are organised to help them do a job. Stories often follow events, procedures use ordered steps, and information texts group facts under headings.

Learning target: identify how texts across the curriculum are organised differently and use language features depending on purposes

Big idea: Identify how stories, procedures and information texts are organised

Learning routine: Identify purpose → Notice structure → Find language features → Explain

4-Step Instructional Sequence

4. 4-Step Instructional Sequence

Step 1: Explicit Teaching & Modelling

Name the target in child-friendly language and demonstrate one concrete example connected to purpose, language.

Step 2: Guided Analysis

Compare or practise a second example together. Students point to, say or show the clue.

Step 3: Collaborative / Independent Practice

Create or analyse one example together, then use a fresh familiar example independently.

Step 4: Formative Assessment

Use a one-minute check with a new example; require observable evidence rather than a guess.

  2. Build or improve — Turn a mixed set of instructions into a clear ordered procedure.
  3. Explain your choice — Use the words purpose, structure, feature to explain what you changed and why.
Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

Curriculum Coverage & Elaborations

The content description is the required target. The elaborations below are taught as examples and contexts, not as disconnected checklist wording.

  • E1: identifying the typical features of a text; for example, a typical introduction to a narrative or the use of dot points in instructions
  • E2: identifying that different types of texts might have different forms; for example, an expression of opinion might be in the form of a poster, email or brochure
  • E3: identifying the organisation and language features in texts such as narratives, recounts, information reports, simple procedures, expression of opinion and responses to texts (including poetry), and discuss their purposes
Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Identifying by topic only: Two texts can share a topic but have different purposes and structures.
  • Using one clue only: Check headings, order, sentence types and vocabulary together.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.
Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Use a fresh, code-aligned example. The student should show the exact word, sound, sentence, image feature, audience clue or text evidence that supports the answer.

Quick checks

  • What structure suits a procedure?
  • What feature helps organise facts?
  • Can two texts share a topic but differ in purpose?
  • Why do structures matter?

Mastery evidence

  • Identify purpose
  • Recognise story structure
  • Recognise procedure structure
  • Recognise information structure
Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Identify how stories, procedures and information texts are organised
  • Use the routine: Identify purpose → Notice structure → Find language features → Explain
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.
