Compare how texts are organised for a purpose AC9E2LA03

WORKED EXAMPLE

The same topic can have different jobs

Story: “A seed rolled into a dragon’s boot. The dragon shook it out and planted it.” Procedure: “Plant a seed. You need a pot, soil and a seed. Put soil in the pot. Place the seed in the soil. Water it.” Both concern a seed, but one tells imagined events and the other guides actions in order.

Learn the skill

1. Compare connected events and grouped information

Narratives often introduce characters and a setting before connected events. Recounts tell events in time order. Information texts group related facts. These are common patterns, not rigid tests.

Recount: “Yesterday we visited the garden. First we planted a seed. Then we watered it.” Information card, heading Seeds: “Some seeds grow inside fruit. A seed can grow into a plant.”

Try it · Which tells a past class event, and which groups facts about seeds? Give an order or language clue.

The recount uses Yesterday, we and ordered events. The information card groups seed facts under a heading. A text can mix purposes, so explain the actual evidence rather than sort by topic alone.

2. Connect organisation, language and form

A procedure orders actions; an opinion can state a choice and reason. The same purpose can appear in different forms, such as a poster, email or brochure.

Opinion message: “Please keep our reading corner quiet so everyone can enjoy a book.” Procedure: “Fold the paper. Draw a star. Open the paper.”

Try it · How would the order change if the supplied procedure cards were mixed? Could the opinion be sent in an email?

Keep fold before drawing on the folded paper, then open. Yes: an email could carry the same opinion and reason. A poster is a form, not automatic proof of persuasion.

3. Notice how a response refers to poetry

A response can name a feeling, quote or identify a feature, and explain the connection. Poetry may use repeated sounds and line breaks instead of a story problem.

Original poem: “Tip, tap, tiny rain; / Tip, tap, on the pane.” Response: “I enjoyed the repeated tip, tap because it reminds me of raindrops.”

Try it · What is the response doing that the poem itself is not?

It explains a reader’s reaction to a feature of the poem. The repeated words support that reason. Do not require all poems to rhyme or every story to have the same opening words.

Common mistakes

“Topic tells the text type.” Compare the job, order and actual language.

“One clue proves the purpose.” Use several clues from the complete text.

“All stories and poems follow one pattern.” Use common patterns thoughtfully and check the real example.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare and reorganise

Read the six complete samples in More examples. Choose two, explain a shared topic or feature and one organisational difference. Reorder the actual three paper-flag cards, then explain why the actions need that order.

Adjust the challenge Starter: compare two adult-read examples. Core: explain purpose, structure and language clues. Stretch: put the same opinion in a different form and explain what can stay the same.

Use the complete supplied samples or actual texts, including all relevant pictures. Adult reading supports comparison; no genre label by itself demonstrates comprehension.

Three tricky questions

1. Does numbered writing always give instructions?

No. Numbers can order events or facts. Read what the sentences do.

2. Must an opinion be a poster?

No. It can appear in an email, brochure or other suitable form.

3. Can a poem response include a reason?

Yes. A reader can connect a particular feature to a response.

FAQ

Are these fixed recipes for every text?

No. They are useful common patterns to compare with actual examples.

Why use texts from different learning areas?

The purpose changes: reporting science observations, explaining a maths method and telling imagined events can need different organisation and language.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Identifying by topic only: Two texts can share a topic but have different purposes and structures.
  • Using one clue only: Check headings, order, sentence types and vocabulary together.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Practice Thought & Formative Assessment

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Identify how stories, procedures and information texts are organised
  • Use the routine: Identify purpose → Notice structure → Find language features → Explain
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

Earlier worked model

Read: '1. Fold the paper. 2. Press the crease. 3. Open the paper.' Why are these instructions numbered?

Answer: To show the order of the actions.

Numbering guides the reader through a procedure in sequence.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LA03: identify how texts across the curriculum are organised differently and use language features depending on purposes

  • identifying the typical features of a text; for example, a typical introduction to a narrative or the use of dot points in instructions
  • identifying that different types of texts might have different forms; for example, an expression of opinion might be in the form of a poster, email or brochure
  • identifying the organisation and language features in texts such as narratives, recounts, information reports, simple procedures, expression of opinion and responses to texts (including poetry), and discuss their purposes
