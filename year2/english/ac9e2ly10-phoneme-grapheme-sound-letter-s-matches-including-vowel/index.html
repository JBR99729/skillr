YEAR 2 ENGLISH

Read and spell words using sound patterns AC9E2LY10

Learning intention: I can use sound–letter matches, silent letters and familiar word parts to read and spell words.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A spelling pattern can cross several letters

In night, n spells /n/, igh spells the long i sound and t spells /t/. Blend the three phonemes. In rain, r and n each spell a consonant sound, while ai spells the long a sound. Read the whole printed word, then check that it makes sense. A picture is not a replacement for reading its letters.

Learn the skill

1. Notice vowel spellings

A vowel digraph has two letters working together to spell a vowel sound. Some long vowel spellings use more letters. Learn a pattern in real words, then blend the complete word.

Read green, moon, rain, play and beach. They show ee, oo, ai, ay and ea. Night shows the less common igh spelling for long i; its final t still spells /t/. Patterns can have alternatives: oo differs in moon and book, and ea differs in beach and bread. An adult models unfamiliar pronunciations.

Try it · Read light. Which letters spell its long i sound, and which letter spells the last sound?

Igh spells the long i sound; t spells the final /t/. L spells the first sound. Blend all three phonemes to read light. Do not treat ight as one phoneme.

2. Keep cluster sounds; notice silent letters

In a consonant cluster you can hear each consonant phoneme. Some other spellings contain a letter that is not pronounced separately. Look carefully, blend the sounds and remember the written pattern.

In frog, /f/ and /r/ are separate sounds. In knee, k is not pronounced separately; kn spells /n/ and ee spells the vowel. In thumb, th spells one first sound and b is not pronounced separately at the end. We still include the silent letters when spelling.

Try it · Read crisp and knock. Which starts with two separate consonant sounds, and which has a silent initial k?

Crisp begins /k/, /r/: a consonant cluster. Knock begins /n/; the initial k is silent in this word. Read both words fully. When spelling knock, remember the kn pattern even though no separate /k/ is heard.

3. Blend longer words and familiar parts

Use known sound patterns through the whole word. A familiar compound can be split into two complete words, read and joined. Other longer words can be read in manageable syllables.

Raincoat combines rain and coat; both parts help us read and spell the whole word. Sunset combines sun and set. Rabbit has two spoken syllables, rab–bit; its two written b letters do not require two separate /b/ sounds. Join the parts smoothly and check the meaning.

Try it · Read lunchbox. Which two complete words help you, and how would you spell the word meaning a box for lunch?

Lunch and box combine to make lunchbox, written as one word here. Read both parts, join them and reread the whole word. Spelling it requires the letters in both parts, including the ch in lunch.

Common mistakes

Saying one sound for an entire cluster In frog, keep both /f/ and /r/. In sh, the two letters work together for one phoneme.

Leaving out a silent letter when writing Read knee with no separate /k/, but keep k in its conventional spelling.

Expecting a vowel spelling to have only one sound Compare the actual words: oo in moon differs from oo in book. Blend a plausible reading and check the meaning.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Read, sort and spell a fresh word

Read the supplied word cards in More examples. Sort by a feature you can explain; a word may belong in more than one group. Then turn the cards away. The adult dictates a different word from the closed adult card. The learner says its sounds, writes it, checks the spelling and rereads the written word. Copying an open card is practice; use the covered turn to check independent spelling.

Adjust the challenge Starter: read a few taught vowel-pattern words with sound boxes. Core: combine vowels, clusters and silent-letter words. Stretch: explain the parts of a two-syllable or compound word, then independently spell a fresh example.

Use words containing patterns already taught to the learner. Accept consistent local spoken forms. An adult checks actual reading and dictated spelling; choosing a label alone does not demonstrate either performance.

Three tricky questions

In night, do gh add two more spoken sounds?

No. Igh spells the long i vowel here. The word has three phonemes: /n/, long i, /t/.

Should the spelling of thumb leave out b because we cannot hear a separate /b/?

No. The conventional spelling still includes b. Notice and remember the mb pattern at the end.

Does seeing rain inside raincoat mean you may skip the other letters?

No. Read rain and coat, then join and check the complete word.

FAQ

Can a word fit two sorting groups?

Yes. Raincoat has a vowel digraph and is a compound. Explain the feature you used instead of forcing one label.

Are all two-syllable words compounds?

No. Sunset contains two complete words, sun and set. Rabbit has two syllables but does not split into two complete words with those meanings.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

A phoneme may be represented by one or more letters. Readers recognise common and less common patterns, preserve sounds in clusters and notice silent letters and word parts.

Big idea: Read and spell vowel patterns, clusters, silent letters and compound words

Learning routine: Say word → Identify sounds → Map graphemes → Notice pattern → Check meaning

Guided practice

  1. Sort and notice — Sort words by vowel digraph, consonant cluster, silent letter or compound word.
  2. Build or improve — Map phonemes to graphemes in one- and two-syllable words.
  3. Explain your choice — Use the words phoneme, grapheme, pattern to explain what you changed and why.

Complete reading and sorting cards

Read these cards: green, moon, book, rain, play, beach, bread, night, frog, crisp, knee, thumb, raincoat, sunset, rabbit. Point to the spelling that supports each reading. Sort by vowel pattern, consonant cluster, silent letter or compound. Some words can fit more than one group.

Check the sort and explain the overlaps

Examples: green has ee; moon and book have oo with different vowel sounds; rain has ai; play has ay; beach and bread have ea with different vowel sounds; night has igh for long i plus final /t/. Frog begins /f/ and /r/; crisp begins /k/ and /r/ and keeps /s/ and /p/ at the end. Knee has silent k; thumb has silent b. Raincoat and sunset are compounds. Rabbit has two syllables but is not built from two complete words.

Map, write and reread

Model rain as r | ai | n: three phonemes. Model thumb as th | u | mb: three phonemes in the usual pronunciation, with b not pronounced separately. Model robot by reading its two syllables and joining them. These are sound and spelling models, not three universal rules for every word.

Adult dictation card · hide all printed targets

Choose a word whose patterns the learner has been taught. Say “tail” in “The dog wagged its tail”; “bright” in “The lamp is bright”; or “sunhat” in “I packed my sunhat”. The learner says the word, maps its sounds, writes it and reads the spelling back. Say the target word and its sentence aloud. Keep the printed word hidden and do not name its letters or show its spelling until the learner has written it.

Check the writing

Tail uses ai; bright keeps the /b/ and /r/ cluster and igh for long i; sunhat combines sun and hat. Check the actual letters, not only whether the learner can select a printed answer.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • One letter equals one sound: Some sounds use digraphs, and some letters are silent.
  • Dropping a cluster sound: Stretch the word and preserve each consonant phoneme.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Quick-answer checks and transfer

Check the earlier quick questions

Ai often spells the long a vowel as in rain. K is silent in knee. Sun and set form sunset. A grapheme is a letter or group of letters used to represent a phoneme.

For a fresh reading check, read bright and sunhat without an adult reading the target first. Explain the igh spelling and the two complete words sun and hat. Then use a covered adult dictation turn from the earlier packet to check actual writing and rereading.

Check the reading explanation

Bright keeps /b/ and /r/ as separate initial sounds and igh spells long i; final t remains /t/. Sunhat joins sun and hat. Knowing parts helps, but the learner must still read the complete word.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LY10: use phoneme-grapheme (sound-letter/s) matches, including vowel digraphs, less common long vowel patterns, consonant clusters and silent letters when reading and writing words of one or more syllables, including compound words

  • reading words with digraphs where one letter is not pronounced; for example, “knife” and “thumb”, when reading and using them in writing
  • providing the sounds for less common letter-sound matches; for example, “ight”, and using them in writing
  • reading words with vowel digraphs (“ee”, “oo”, “ai”, “ay”, “ea”)
