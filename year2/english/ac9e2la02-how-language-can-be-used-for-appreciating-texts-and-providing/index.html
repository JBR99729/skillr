Explain what you appreciate in a text AC9E2LA02

Learning intention: I can name a response and support a preference with an actual text detail.

An adult can read directions and model the examples. Use a short turn or return to a section later.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A reason points to something the text does

Original lines: “Tip, tap, tiny rain; tip, tap, on the pane.” A response might be “I enjoyed the repeated tip, tap because it reminds me of little raindrops.” The reason connects a real sound pattern to a reader’s response. Another reader can prefer a different feature with a supported reason.

Learn the skill

1. Name a precise response

Use words that say what you liked, enjoyed or found surprising. Name the actual setting, character, image, word pattern or event.

Original story: “Lia’s paper boat tipped. She folded its sides again. At last it floated in a shallow tray.” “I liked Lia’s persistence” means I appreciated that she kept trying.

Try it · Give a response to the supplied story and point to a detail.

For example: “I enjoyed the ending because Lia’s next try works.” Accept another supported response, including dislike. Teach unfamiliar response words such as persistence before relying on them.

2. Explain a preference and compare it

A preference chooses one option over another. A relevant reason explains the choice, rather than simply repeating that it is good.

Text A: “The little boat bobbed beside the bank.” Text B: “The boat moved.” Both describe a boat. One reader prefers A because bobbed gives a particular up-and-down movement.

Try it · Which version do you prefer for a story, and why?

Accept A for its more particular movement or B for a reason suited to the reader’s purpose, such as a very simple caption. Liked, enjoyed and preferred do not form one fixed strength scale; preferred usually needs a comparison.

3. Appreciate words specific to Country and Place

Use the actual text and its supplied language guidance. Explain what a particular word adds to the setting, keeping its language and creators identified.

Adult supplies Cooee Mittigar by Jasmine Seymour and Leanne Mulgo Watson. Read a real spread and its glossary. In this Darug source, Nura refers to Country and yana to walking.

Try it · Choose a word or setting detail actually read. Explain how it helps you appreciate that particular Country or invitation.

Refer to the real page and glossary, and accept different supported responses. Do not replace the actual book with an invented cultural passage, assume a local translation, or judge the child’s pronunciation from printed words alone.

Common mistakes

“I liked it” explains everything. Add the feature and why it mattered to you.

“Everyone must prefer the same text.” Different supported preferences are valid.

“Any reason fits.” Check that the reason matches a real text detail.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Two responses, one real text

Read the original rain lines in the worked example. Each partner shares a preference or response, points to the relevant words and gives a reason. Compare what each noticed. For the cultural focus, use the actual prepared book and glossary.

Adjust the challenge Starter: choose between two response stems with an adult. Core: name a feature and relevant reason. Stretch: compare two texts or explain why a partner’s different response is also supported.

Adult read-aloud supports access but is not independent decoding evidence. Personal preferences are not right or wrong; check the link between response and actual text.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “because it was good” very specific?

It repeats a judgement. Explain which feature worked and why.

2. Must an excited response use the word exciting?

No. Explain the feeling and the text detail in natural words.

3. Can an unfamiliar cultural word be guessed from an illustration alone?

Use the authorised text, glossary and guidance; an image is not a reliable translation dictionary.

FAQ

Do I need a long review?

No. One clear response, relevant reason and actual detail can be useful.

Can we discuss a text we disliked?

Yes. Use respectful, specific language and evidence rather than judging another reader.

Learning goals

1. Outcome Overview & Core Concepts

A strong response names a preference, gives a reason and refers to a feature of the text such as a character, image, event, word choice or ending.

Big idea: Use opinion language, reasons and evidence when appreciating texts

Learning routine: State opinion → Give reason → Point to evidence → Conclude

Complete original texts and response cards

Story A: “Lia’s paper boat would not float. She folded a second boat. It tipped. She folded the sides again. At last it floated in a shallow tray.” Poem B: “Little drops tap on the pane. / Softly comes the evening rain. / Tip and tap, a tiny beat. / Puddles sparkle by my feet.” Response cards: 1 “I liked Story A.” 2 “I enjoyed Story A because Lia keeps trying before her boat floats.” 3 “I preferred Poem B because its returning tap sounds remind me of rain.” 4 “This book was good.” Sort opinion alone versus opinion with a relevant text reason. Improve card 4 using an actual supplied detail or a detail from an actual book you have read. Accept different supported preferences; excitement and satisfaction are reader responses, not facts every reader must share.

Read an actual Country-specific text with an adult

Supply a lawful copy of Cooee Mittigar by Jasmine Seymour and Leanne Mulgo Watson and use the publisher’s teacher notes and the book’s glossary. It is a Darug text: Nura refers to Country and yana to walking in this source. Read the actual pages and glossary together. Choose a word or setting detail actually present, explain it using that source, and refer to the real spread when discussing its effect. Nura concerns Country and relationships, not merely a generic location. Attribute the language and creators; do not assume these words are local everywhere, invent pronunciation, or reproduce cultural artwork. For local topic words, confirm a community-approved local source and its permitted use first.

Common Student Misconceptions

3. Common Student Misconceptions

  • Only stating a preference: Add because and explain a specific text feature.
  • Reason does not match evidence: Choose an example that proves the reason.
  • Answer without evidence: Point to the word, sentence, image, sound pattern or text feature that supports the answer.

Revision Notes

Revision Notes

  • Remember: Use opinion language, reasons and evidence when appreciating texts
  • Use the routine: State opinion → Give reason → Point to evidence → Conclude
  • Before answering: point to the clue in the spoken words, written sentence, image, page layout or text purpose.
  • Self-check: change the example slightly and see whether the same reasoning still works.

Earlier worked model

Read aloud: 'Tip, tap, tiny rain; tip, tap, on the pane.' Which reason supports liking its sound?

Answer: The repeated 'tip, tap' sounds like little raindrops.

The reader connects the repeated sound pattern with rain.

Curriculum alignment

AC9E2LA02: explore how language can be used for appreciating texts and providing reasons for preferences

  • exploring how language is used to appreciate texts using more precise vocabulary; for example, “I liked how the author described the setting because …”
  • exploring verbs used to express degree of preference; for example, “liked”, “preferred”, “enjoyed”
  • identifying First Nations Australian language words specific to Country/Place that help the reader to be specific when appreciating the setting in a text
