About This Year 2 Maths Topic

In this unit, children identify, describe and demonstrate different amounts of turn in familiar situations.

Children learn to recognise and perform:

  • a quarter turn;
  • a half turn;
  • a three-quarter turn;
  • a full turn.

They also describe the direction of a turn as:

  • clockwise;
  • anticlockwise.

Children investigate turns by:

  • observing taps, door handles, dials and switches;
  • turning their bodies during movement and dance;
  • following and giving directional instructions;
  • observing the movement of hands on an analog clock;
  • navigating pathways through grids;
  • programming simple robot movements;
  • drawing arrows to show the amount and direction of a turn.

These activities support Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 content descriptor AC9M2M05 and its associated elaborations.

The Big Idea for Parents

A turn changes the direction an object or person is facing.

The amount of turn describes how far the object rotates, while clockwise or anticlockwise describes the direction of rotation.

A full turn can be divided into equal parts:

  • a quarter turn is one of four equal parts of a full turn;
  • a half turn is two quarter turns;
  • a three-quarter turn is three quarter turns;
  • a full turn is four quarter turns.

After a full turn, a person or object faces the same direction as at the beginning.

After a half turn, the person or object faces the opposite direction.

Learning Intentions

Children will learn to:

  • identify objects that turn;
  • describe the starting and finishing direction of a turn;
  • recognise a quarter turn;
  • recognise a half turn;
  • recognise a three-quarter turn;
  • recognise a full turn;
  • demonstrate different amounts of turn;
  • identify clockwise movement;
  • identify anticlockwise movement;
  • draw arrows showing the direction of a turn;
  • give and follow turn instructions;
  • connect clock-hand movement with turns;
  • follow pathways through a grid;
  • use turn instructions to locate objects;
  • program a sequence of simple movements;
  • explain how a turn changes position or direction.

Teacher Guide for Parents

Teachers introduce turns through physical movement before asking children to interpret arrows, diagrams or grid instructions.

At home, follow this sequence: identify the starting direction, name the amount of turn, identify clockwise or anticlockwise movement, perform the turn and check the finishing direction.

1. Begin with a Starting Direction

A turn can only be described clearly when the starting direction is known.

Ask your child to stand facing a clear reference point such as:

  • a door;
  • a window;
  • a chair;
  • a wall;
  • an arrow placed on the floor.

State the starting direction before giving the turn instruction.

2. Identify Everyday Objects That Turn

Look around the home, classroom or playground for objects that rotate.

Examples include:

  • a tap handle;
  • a door handle;
  • a key in a lock;
  • a volume dial;
  • a switch;
  • a steering wheel;
  • a fan;
  • a wheel;
  • a clock hand;
  • a jar lid.

Ask children to describe how far the object turns and whether it turns clockwise or anticlockwise.

3. Understand a Quarter Turn

A quarter turn is one of four equal parts of a full turn.

When standing and facing forwards, a quarter turn changes the direction to one side.

For example:

  • a quarter turn clockwise may make the child face right;
  • a quarter turn anticlockwise may make the child face left.

The finishing direction depends on the starting direction.

4. Understand a Half Turn

A half turn is equal to two quarter turns.

After a half turn, the person or object faces the direction opposite to the starting direction.

A half turn can be completed clockwise or anticlockwise.

5. Understand a Three-Quarter Turn

A three-quarter turn is equal to three quarter turns.

It is also the same finishing position as making one quarter turn in the opposite direction.

For example:

A three-quarter turn clockwise finishes in the same direction as a quarter turn anticlockwise.

6. Understand a Full Turn

A full turn is equal to four quarter turns or two half turns.

After a full turn, the person or object returns to the original direction.

A full turn can be completed clockwise or anticlockwise.

7. Compare the Amounts of Turn

Use movement to compare the different turns.

  • A quarter turn is the smallest turn in this unit.
  • A half turn is larger than a quarter turn.
  • A three-quarter turn is larger than a half turn.
  • A full turn completes the entire rotation.

Encourage children to build larger turns from quarter turns.

8. Introduce Clockwise Movement

Clockwise means moving in the same direction as the hands on an analog clock.

Starting at the top of a clockface, clockwise movement travels:

12 → 3 → 6 → 9 → 12

Use a large floor clock or teaching clock to demonstrate the direction.

9. Introduce Anticlockwise Movement

Anticlockwise means moving in the direction opposite to the hands on an analog clock.

Starting at the top of a clockface, anticlockwise movement travels:

12 → 9 → 6 → 3 → 12

10. Avoid Relying Only on Left and Right

Clockwise does not always mean turn right, and anticlockwise does not always mean turn left when describing a longer turn.

The words clockwise and anticlockwise describe the direction of rotation around a central point.

Teacher tip

Always name both the amount and direction, such as “quarter turn clockwise” rather than simply “turn right”.

11. Demonstrate Turns with Body Movement

Ask your child to stand on a marked spot facing forwards.

Give instructions such as:

  • make a quarter turn clockwise;
  • make a half turn anticlockwise;
  • make a three-quarter turn clockwise;
  • make a full turn anticlockwise.

Ask the child to state the finishing direction.

12. Use Floor Arrows

Place arrows on the floor pointing in four directions.

Ask the child to begin on one arrow, perform a turn and identify the arrow they now face.

This makes the starting and finishing directions visible.

13. Build Turns from Quarter Turns

Use repeated quarter turns to develop understanding.

  • 1 quarter turn makes a quarter turn;
  • 2 quarter turns make a half turn;
  • 3 quarter turns make a three-quarter turn;
  • 4 quarter turns make a full turn.

Count each turn aloud while moving.

14. Draw Turn Arrows

Use a curved arrow to show rotation.

The arrow should clearly show:

  • the starting position;
  • the direction of rotation;
  • the finishing position;
  • the amount of turn.

Label the diagram with words such as “half turn clockwise”.

15. Describe Turns of Taps and Door Handles

Observe how a tap, door handle, lock or switch turns.

Ask:

  • What is the starting position?
  • How far did the object turn?
  • Which direction did it turn?
  • What is the finishing position?

16. Give and Follow Movement Instructions

Use short instructions first.

For example:

  1. Take two steps forwards.
  2. Make a quarter turn clockwise.
  3. Take one step forwards.
  4. Make a half turn.

Children should complete the steps in the stated order.

17. Create a Turning Dance

Create a short movement sequence using steps and turns.

For example:

  1. Clap twice.
  2. Make a quarter turn clockwise.
  3. Take two steps.
  4. Make a half turn anticlockwise.
  5. Make one full turn.

Ask children to describe each turn using precise mathematical language.

18. Reverse a Movement Sequence

After completing a pathway or dance, ask the child to return to the starting position.

Discuss which turns must be reversed and which direction should be used.

19. Connect Turns with Clock Hands

Begin with the minute hand pointing to 12.

  • Moving from 12 to 3 is a quarter turn clockwise.
  • Moving from 12 to 6 is a half turn clockwise.
  • Moving from 12 to 9 is a three-quarter turn clockwise.
  • Moving from 12 back to 12 is a full turn clockwise.

20. Connect Turns with Time

The minute hand completes one full turn in one hour.

  • A quarter turn represents one quarter-hour or 15 minutes.
  • A half turn represents one half-hour or 30 minutes.
  • A three-quarter turn represents 45 minutes.
  • A full turn represents one hour or 60 minutes.

21. Investigate Clockwise and Anticlockwise Clock Turns

Although clock hands normally move clockwise, children can move the hands anticlockwise on a teaching clock to explore earlier positions.

Ask:

  • Where will the hand finish after a quarter turn clockwise?
  • Where will it finish after a half turn?
  • What position is one quarter turn anticlockwise from 12?

22. Use Turns to Locate Objects

Place several objects around the room.

Ask the child to begin facing one object and follow an instruction such as:

“Make a quarter turn clockwise. Which object are you facing now?”

23. Follow a Grid Pathway

Draw a simple square grid and place a character or robot on one square.

Show the direction the character is facing.

Give instructions using:

  • move forwards;
  • move backwards;
  • quarter turn clockwise;
  • quarter turn anticlockwise;
  • half turn;
  • three-quarter turn;
  • full turn.

24. Distinguish Turning from Moving

A turn changes the direction a person or object faces, but it does not automatically move the person to a new square.

“Turn” and “move forwards” should be treated as separate instructions.

Important distinction

A robot that makes a quarter turn stays on the same grid square until it receives a movement instruction.

25. Program a Robot Pathway

Use a toy robot, classroom robot, counter or paper character.

Write a sequence of instructions to guide it from a starting point to a target.

For example:

  1. Move forwards two squares.
  2. Make a quarter turn clockwise.
  3. Move forwards three squares.
  4. Make a half turn.

26. Test and Correct Instructions

Follow the completed pathway exactly.

When the robot does not reach the target, identify the instruction that needs to be corrected.

This develops sequencing and problem-solving skills.

27. Describe the Finishing Direction

Reaching the correct square is not always enough.

Ask children to identify:

  • the finishing position;
  • the direction the character is facing;
  • the final turn completed;
  • whether another turn is required.

28. Compare Equivalent Turns

Some turns produce the same finishing direction.

  • Two quarter turns equal one half turn.
  • Four quarter turns equal one full turn.
  • Two half turns equal one full turn.
  • A three-quarter turn clockwise finishes like a quarter turn anticlockwise.

29. Explain a Turn Clearly

Encourage children to include three pieces of information:

  1. the starting direction;
  2. the amount and direction of turn;
  3. the finishing direction.

For example:

“I started facing the door. I made a half turn clockwise, so I finished facing the window.”

Recommended Teaching Sequence at Home

  1. Identify objects that turn.
  2. Establish a clear starting direction.
  3. Practise quarter turns.
  4. Combine quarter turns to make half turns.
  5. Practise three-quarter turns.
  6. Practise full turns.
  7. Introduce clockwise and anticlockwise movement.
  8. Draw and label turn arrows.
  9. Follow body-movement instructions.
  10. Create dance sequences using turns.
  11. Connect clock-hand movement with turns.
  12. Locate objects using turn instructions.
  13. Follow and create grid pathways.
  14. Explain starting and finishing directions.

Common Misconceptions and How Parents Can Help

Turning without identifying a starting direction

Mark the starting direction with an object, arrow or label before completing the turn.

Confusing a quarter turn with a half turn

Build each turn using repeated quarter turns and count them aloud.

Thinking a full turn changes the finishing direction

Demonstrate that four quarter turns return the person or object to the original direction.

Thinking a half turn returns to the starting direction

A half turn faces the opposite direction. Two half turns are needed to return to the starting direction.

Confusing clockwise and anticlockwise

Use an analog clock as a visual reference and trace the normal movement of its hands.

Assuming clockwise always means right

Clockwise describes circular rotation. Use curved arrows rather than relying only on left and right language.

Moving forwards while turning

Separate the instructions. First turn on the same spot, then move forwards when instructed.

Counting grid squares while completing the turn

A turn changes facing direction but does not change the grid square.

Forgetting the direction of the turn

Always use a complete phrase such as “half turn anticlockwise”.

Thinking three-quarter turn means three full turns

Divide one complete turn into four equal parts and identify three of those parts.

Drawing straight arrows for rotational movement

Use curved arrows around a central point to show the direction and amount of rotation.

Ignoring the finishing direction in a grid pathway

Ask children to state both the final square and the direction the character faces.

Simple Home Turn and Direction Activities

Activity 1: Turn Hunt

Find taps, handles, knobs, switches, lids and other objects that turn around the home.

Describe the amount and direction of each turn.

Focus: AC9M2M05_E1.

Activity 2: Body-Turn Challenge

Ask your child to begin facing a marked direction and perform quarter, half, three-quarter and full turns.

Focus: Demonstrating turns.

Activity 3: Clockwise or Anticlockwise?

Draw curved arrows showing different directions and ask your child to identify each as clockwise or anticlockwise.

Focus: Direction of rotation.

Activity 4: Turning Dance

Create a short dance containing quarter, half and full turns.

Ask your child to describe and perform the sequence.

Focus: AC9M2M05_E2.

Activity 5: Clock-Hand Turns

Start the minute hand at 12 and move it through quarter, half, three-quarter and full turns.

Connect each movement with the relevant amount of time.

Focus: AC9M2M05_E3.

Activity 6: Object Direction Game

Place four objects around a child standing in the centre.

Give turn instructions and ask which object the child faces after each turn.

Focus: Position and direction.

Activity 7: Floor Grid Path

Create a simple floor grid using tape or paper squares.

Guide your child through the grid using movement and turn instructions.

Focus: AC9M2M05_E4.

Activity 8: Program the Toy

Use a toy, counter or paper robot and write a sequence of steps and turns to reach a target.

Test and correct the program.

Focus: Sequencing and debugging.

Activity 9: Draw the Turn

Draw an object in its starting and finishing positions.

Add a curved arrow and label the amount and direction of turn.

Focus: Turn diagrams.

Activity 10: Turn Instruction Creator

Ask your child to create a pathway or movement sequence for another person to follow.

Check whether each instruction is clear and complete.

Focus: Mathematical communication.

Questions Parents Can Ask

  • What direction are you facing at the start?
  • What amount of turn will you make?
  • Will you turn clockwise or anticlockwise?
  • What direction are you facing now?
  • How many quarter turns make a half turn?
  • How many quarter turns make a full turn?
  • What happens after a half turn?
  • What happens after a full turn?
  • Is this turn larger or smaller than a half turn?
  • Can you make the same finishing position another way?
  • Which way do clock hands normally turn?
  • How far does the minute hand move in a quarter-hour?
  • Does the robot move to another square when it turns?
  • Which square will the robot reach?
  • Which direction will it face at the end?
  • Which instruction should come first?
  • Where did the pathway go wrong?
  • How can you correct the instruction?
  • How could you draw this turn?
  • How can you check your answer?

Supporting Different Learners at Home

Children Who Need More Support

  • use a large floor arrow to mark the starting direction;
  • begin with quarter and half turns;
  • practise clockwise movement separately;
  • practise anticlockwise movement separately;
  • count repeated quarter turns aloud;
  • use objects at the front, back, left and right;
  • use a large teaching clock as a reference;
  • provide one grid instruction at a time;
  • use a physical toy before using drawn grids;
  • allow oral explanations before written responses.

Children Ready for Extension

  • compare equivalent turns;
  • reverse multi-step pathways;
  • create pathways with obstacles;
  • find several routes to the same target;
  • use fewer instructions to improve a route;
  • predict finishing positions without physically turning;
  • combine turns and distances in longer sequences;
  • identify errors in turn diagrams;
  • design a grid challenge for another learner;
  • explain why two different turns can produce the same finish.

  1. Demonstrate a quarter turn clockwise.
  2. Demonstrate a half turn anticlockwise.
  3. Demonstrate a three-quarter turn.
  4. Demonstrate a full turn.
  5. Explain the difference between clockwise and anticlockwise.
  6. Identify one everyday object that turns.
  7. Draw and label a half-turn arrow.
  8. Move a clock hand through a quarter and half turn.
  9. Follow a short grid pathway.
  10. State the final position and facing direction.

Home Learning Summary

Understanding turns develops when children physically rotate, observe everyday turning objects and connect movement with arrows, clocks and pathways.

Use this simple routine:

  1. mark the starting direction;
  2. name the amount of turn;
  3. name clockwise or anticlockwise;
  4. complete the turn on the same spot;
  5. identify the finishing direction;
  6. draw or describe the movement;
  7. apply the turn to a clock or grid pathway;
  8. check the result.
