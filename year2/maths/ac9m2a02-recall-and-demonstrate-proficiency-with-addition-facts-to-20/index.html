Addition facts to 20 and related subtraction

Key concept: Build addition facts to 20 with doubles, ten-frames and known number bonds. Use the same whole and parts to work out related subtraction facts.

Learn the skill

1. Build and recall useful number bonds

Build totals with counters or two colours on a ten-frame. Name the whole and its two parts, then say the matching addition. Start with secure facts within 10 and build towards totals of 20. Adding zero leaves the amount unchanged.

Try it · Complete 6 + __ = 10 and __ + 6 = 10. What stays the same?

Both blanks are 4. The parts are 6 and 4 and the whole is 10 in both equations.

2. Count on and bridge through ten

Count on from an addend rather than starting the count at zero. For a small extra amount, a few counts may help. For a larger extra amount, making ten can be easier. Track every part once.

Try it · Solve 9 + 6 by making ten. State the two parts of 6.

Split 6 into 1 and 5. 9 + 1 = 10; 10 + 5 = 15.

3. Use an addition fact to subtract

Subtraction can find a missing part. If you know 8 + 7 = 15, you also know 15 − 8 = 7 and 15 − 7 = 8. Start with the whole, then name the part removed or the missing part.

Try it · Which addition fact helps you find 14 − 6? Give the subtraction answer and a check.

6 + 8 = 14, so 14 − 6 = 8. Check: 8 + 6 = 14.

Common mistakes

Starting the related subtraction with a part. Start with the whole, 15, then subtract a part.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Build a fact family

Make 8 and 7 with counters. Move two to bridge through ten, then write the two addition and two subtraction facts for 8, 7 and 15.

Adjust the challenge: Start with 6 and 4. Extend by checking 15 − 8 using the original model.

FAQ

How can 8 + 7 help with 15 − 7?

The addition shows that 15 has parts 8 and 7. Removing 7 leaves 8.

Learn the skill

Learn addition facts and related subtraction

Read, build or draw, then try the question. Open the answer after explaining your thinking.

The addends are the numbers added; the sum is their total. A double uses two equal addends. A fact family keeps the same whole and parts. Recall means knowing a familiar fact without counting every object.

Try a worked example together

Use the same parts to add and subtract

A jar holds 17 counters. Ten are blue and the rest are yellow. Find the yellow counters by thinking about the missing part.

  2. Write 17 − 10 = 7. Taking away the blue part leaves the yellow part.
  3. Check by putting the two parts back together: 10 + 7 = 17.

Try it: Eighteen pencils are in a tub. Eight are short and the rest are long. Write an addition and a subtraction sentence that show how many are long.

Check the answer

8 + 10 = 18 and 18 − 8 = 10. There are 10 long pencils.

1. Build and recall useful number bonds

Build totals with counters or two colours on a ten-frame. Name the whole and its two parts, then say the matching addition. Start with secure facts within 10 and build towards totals of 20. Adding zero leaves the amount unchanged.

The model shows 8 + 7 = 15 and 7 + 8 = 15. The order of the parts can change without changing the whole. Also read 15 = 8 + 7: the equals sign means the values match.

Try it: Complete 6 + __ = 10 and __ + 6 = 10. What stays the same?

Check the answer

Both blanks are 4. The parts are 6 and 4 and the whole is 10 in both equations.

2. Use doubles and near doubles

Work through it: 7 + 7 = 14 is a double. To find 7 + 8, use one extra: 7 + 7 + 1 = 15. Or start with 8 + 8 = 16 and remove one: 15. Explain which addend changed.

Try it: Use a known double to solve 6 + 7. Why do you adjust by one?

Check the answer

6 + 6 = 12, and 7 is one more than 6, so add one: 13. You could also use 7 + 7 = 14 and subtract one.

3. Count on and bridge through ten

Count on from an addend rather than starting the count at zero. For a small extra amount, a few counts may help. For a larger extra amount, making ten can be easier. Track every part once.

Work through it: For 8 + 7, split 7 into 2 and 5. 8 + 2 = 10, then 10 + 5 = 15. The two pieces must still total 7. Reaching 10 is an intermediate step, not the final answer.

Try it: Solve 9 + 6 by making ten. State the two parts of 6.

Check the answer

Split 6 into 1 and 5. 9 + 1 = 10; 10 + 5 = 15.

4. Use an addition fact to subtract

Subtraction can find a missing part. If you know 8 + 7 = 15, you also know 15 − 8 = 7 and 15 − 7 = 8. Start with the whole, then name the part removed or the missing part.

Work through it: For 16 − 9, ask “9 and what make 16?” Count up 1 to 10, then 6 to 16: 1 + 6 = 7. Check 9 + 7 = 16.

Try it: Which addition fact helps you find 14 − 6? Give the subtraction answer and a check.

Check the answer

6 + 8 = 14, so 14 − 6 = 8. Check: 8 + 6 = 14.

5. Connect families and record all partitions

Addend means a number being added; the sum is the total. A part-part-whole model shows two parts joined to make one whole. A ten-frame makes combinations to 10 visible.

If the parts are 8 and 7 and the whole is 15, the fact family is 8 + 7 = 15, 7 + 8 = 15, 15 − 8 = 7 and 15 − 7 = 8.

Record combinations in a useful order

Ask students to find every way to split 10 between two groups. Record 10 = 10 + 0, 9 + 1, 8 + 2, …, 0 + 10. Changing one part down by one while the other goes up by one proves no partition has been skipped. Discuss which pairs are reversals.

For ordered placements in two labelled groups, 0 + 10 and 10 + 0 are different arrangements. If you are listing pairs without order, count each reversed pair once. A double such as 7 + 7 = 14 has only one distinct addition sentence and one distinct subtraction sentence: do not invent four different facts.

Try it: Write the two addition and two subtraction facts for 5, 8 and 13. Then say how you could list all ordered pairs making 6.

Check the answer

5 + 8 = 13; 8 + 5 = 13; 13 − 5 = 8; 13 − 8 = 5. For ordered pairs making 6, start 0 + 6 and increase the first part by 1 as the second decreases by 1, ending 6 + 0. This includes 7 ordered pairs.

6. Choose a strategy and explain your check

Mix addition and related subtraction so you choose a method that suits the numbers. Recall a fact you know; derive one you do not yet remember. Fluency includes accurate, flexible thinking, rather than only finishing quickly.

Work through it: For 8 + 9, a near double is useful: 8 + 8 + 1 = 17. For 17 − 9, use the related fact to find 8. Both answers keep the same whole and parts.

Try it: A learner says 8 + 5 = 10 because 8 needs 2 to make ten. Explain the missing step and correct the answer.

Check the answer

Only 2 of the 5 has been used. There are 3 left: 8 + 2 + 3 = 13. Check: 13 − 5 = 8.

Teaching tips and common mistakes

Keep the learning focused: Keep the core addition facts and related subtractions within 20. A remembered fact and a clearly explained derived fact are both useful evidence.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Counting every item: prompt with “Which double or make-10 fact can help?”
  • Losing part of an addend: after splitting 7 into 2 and 5, check that 2 + 5 still equals 7.
  • Subtracting in the wrong direction: identify the whole first; subtraction starts with the whole.
  • Writing unrelated equations: keep the same two parts and whole in all four equations.
  • Repeating or missing partitions: change each part by one in opposite directions and mark the endpoints.

Adjust the support

  • Support: use two-colour counters, ten-frames and part-part-whole templates; keep totals within 10 before moving to 20.
  • Core: solve mixed addition facts to 20, name the strategy and derive both related subtraction facts.
  • Extend: find all partitions of a chosen whole to 20 and justify that the list is complete, without introducing formal algebra.
Quick learning check

Complete 12 + □ = 19 and 19 − 12 = □. Why do the blanks match?

Check the answer

Both are 7. The whole is 19 and the known part is 12, so both sentences find the same missing part.

An adult may read directions aloud. Start with objects or drawings, then reduce support as the learner becomes confident.
