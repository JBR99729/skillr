Learn the skill

Five steps to number confidence

Learning goal: I can show, read, locate and order numbers to 1000. Use blocks or a virtual model, digit cards and a pencil. Return to a model whenever an answer is correct but the explanation is unclear.

1. Build and read number models

Make groups of ten, then groups of a hundred. Name the unit before counting it. A flat is 100, a rod is 10, and a small square is 1.

Example 1: read 352

  1. Count 3 hundreds: 300.
  2. Count 5 tens: 50; then 2 ones.
  3. Combine 300 + 50 + 2. Write 352.

Example 2: build 704

  1. Use 7 hundred flats.
  2. Use no ten rods, so keep 0 in the tens place.
  3. Add 4 ones. The numeral is 704, not 740.

Ten hundred flats make 1000. Build it and count in hundreds to check. For quick drawings, use a square labelled 100, a line labelled 10 and a dot for 1. These symbols stand for different amounts.

Try together: Build 231. How many hundreds, tens and ones do you need?

Answer and teaching point

2 hundreds, 3 tens and 1 one. If the child counts all blocks as ones, name each unit and build again.

Assign: Matching printable pack: Practice 1-10, pages 8-11.

2. Match numbers and number names

A numeral is a number written with digits. A digit is one symbol from 0 to 9. Say the hundreds first, then the rest.

Example 1: numeral to words

  1. 352 has 3 hundreds.
  2. The remaining 52 is fifty-two.
  3. Say and write three hundred and fifty-two.

Example 2: words to numeral

  1. “Seven hundred and four” has 7 hundreds.
  2. No tens are named, so put 0 in the tens place.
  3. Put 4 in the ones place: 704.

“One thousand” is 1000. For teen endings, say the whole teen word: 612 is six hundred and twelve. Check number meaning separately from spelling.

Try together: Write “two hundred and seventeen” as a numeral.

Answer and teaching point

217: 200 and 17. Say seventeen as one teen word; it is not seventy.

Assign: Practice 11-20, pages 12-14.

3. Read and use number lines

Equal gaps show equal steps. Read the end labels and count the gaps, not the ticks. Move right for larger numbers.

Example 1: gaps of one

  1. From 420 to 430 is 10.
  2. There are 10 gaps, so each is worth 1.
  3. Move six gaps from 420 to place 426.

Example 2: gaps of ten and the halfway point

  1. From 400 to 500 is 100 over 10 gaps.
  2. Each gap is worth 10. Halfway is 450.
  3. 455 belongs halfway between 450 and 460. A different, unlabelled arrow near that position can be estimated as about 455; 490 would be too far right.

For an estimate, name the two surrounding labels and explain why the position fits. Do not pretend an approximate arrow gives an exact number.

Try together: On a line from 310 to 320 with 10 equal gaps, where does 318 go?

Answer and teaching point

Eight gaps after 310, or two before 320. Each gap is 1.

Assign: Practice 21-30, pages 15-17.

4. Compare and order numbers

Greater means a larger amount; less means a smaller amount; equal means the same amount. 1000 is greater than every three-digit number. For two three-digit numbers, compare hundreds first, then tens, then ones.

Example 1: compare 586 and 568

  1. Both have 5 hundreds.
  2. Compare tens: 8 tens is more than 6 tens.
  3. 586 is greater than 568. Stop at the first different place.

Example 2: order 807, 780 and 870

  1. 780 has only 7 hundreds, so it comes first.
  2. 807 and 870 both have 8 hundreds. Compare 0 tens with 7 tens.
  3. Smallest first: 780, 807, 870. Reverse this for largest first.

If every place matches, the numbers are equal. Keep all cards when ordering, including repeats. To make the smallest number from non-zero digit cards, choose the smallest available hundreds digit, then tens, then ones; use each card once. Reverse the choices for the largest number. To find every arrangement, fix one hundreds digit and swap the remaining two, then change the hundreds digit.

Try together: Order 631, 613 and 136, smallest first.

Answer and teaching point

136, 613, 631. Compare hundreds, then tens; do not judge by the last digit alone.

Assign: Practice 31-40, pages 18-20.

5. Use charts and cross boundaries

This fragment comes from a chart with ten numbers in every full row. Right within a row is 1 more; down one row is 10 more. Left is 1 less; up is 10 less.

Part of a ten-column number chart
146147148
156157158
166167168

Example 1: use two directions

  1. Start at 157. Move right: 158.
  2. Return to 157. Move down: 167.
  3. Move up from 167 to check: 157.

Example 2: cross a hundred

  1. Count back in ones: 401, 400, 399, 398.
  2. Continue counting when the hundred changes; do not restart at 1.
  3. Contrast tens: 185, 195, 205, 215. The ones stay 5. In hundreds: 126, 226, 326.

At a row end, the next counting number begins the next row. After 150 comes 151; directly below 150 is 160. These are different moves.

Check both ends: A group from 300 to 350, including both ends, accepts 300, 320 and 350. It excludes 299 and 351. “Including” means the end numbers belong too.

Try together: Count backwards: 601, 600, __, __. Does 350 belong in the group from 300 to 350, including both ends?

Answer and teaching point

599, 598. Yes, 350 is included. If a boundary is skipped, count slowly and check a chart.

Assign: Practice 41-50, pages 21-23.

Teacher and tutor guidance

AC9M2N01: recognise, represent and order numbers to at least 1000 using physical and virtual materials, numerals and number lines.

Begin with two-digit prerequisites where needed, then use three-digit quantities, zeros and 1000. The five sections are teaching focuses, not official curriculum strands. Broader four-digit work is an optional continuation; this guide reaches 1000. Non-standard regrouping belongs to the next place-value focus.

Demonstrate with physical collections as well as drawings. Ask the child to group objects and explain the total; a written numeral alone does not show that they can construct the model. Read instructions aloud or accept oral responses when writing masks number understanding.

In the matching printable pack, concepts are on pages 4-6 and vocabulary is on page 7. Teach all five focuses before assigning the separate Test, pages 24-28. Keep teacher keys, pages 29-37, separate. Record errors by model, notation, scale, order or chart direction and reteach that idea.

Quick recap: Why is 704 different from 740? How do you find the gap size on a line? What changes when you move down a ten-column chart?

Expected explanations

704 has 4 ones and no tens; 740 has 4 tens and no ones. Use labelled endpoints and count equal gaps to find their value. One row down adds 10. A confident answer should include the reason, not just a rule name.
