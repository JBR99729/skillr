Learn the idea

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Recognise, represent and order numbers to at least 1000 using materials, numerals and number lines. An adult can read the directions and accept a spoken, pointed, built or drawn explanation.

1. Build, read and represent numbers

A numeral is a number written with digits. A hundred flat represents 100, a ten rod represents 10, and a small cube represents 1. Name the unit before counting it.

Worked example: build and read 352

  1. Count the hundreds: 100, 200, 300.
  2. Count five tens to reach 350, then two ones to reach 352.
  3. Match the complete model to the numeral 352.

Build another: 704 uses seven hundreds, no tens and four ones. Keep the empty tens place in the numeral. Ten hundreds make 1000: build or sketch ten hundred groups and count to check.

Words support meaning: say “three hundred and fifty-two” as you point to 352 and its model. The goal is recognising the amount, rather than testing spelling.

Watch for: counting every piece as one ignores the units. A hundred flat and one cube represent different amounts.

Try it: Build or draw 231 with hundreds, tens and ones. Say what each part represents.

Check the answer

2 hundreds, 3 tens and 1 one. Count the units to check the total, then match the model to 231.

2. Read and locate numbers on number lines

Equal spaces on one number line show equal changes. Find two labelled marks and work out the value of each space. Count the spaces, rather than the marks.

Worked example: locate 650

  1. From 620 to 680 is a change of 60.
  2. Six equal spaces share that change: each is 10.
  3. Move three spaces from 620: 630, 640, 650.

A different scale: a line from 420 to 430 with ten equal spaces changes by 1 each time. The sixth space after 420 is 426. On a line marked in tens from 400 to 500, 450 is halfway; 455 is halfway between 450 and 460.

Watch for: the value of a space can change on a new line. Read its labels before deciding.

Try it: A number line from 900 to 1000 has five equal spaces. What is one space worth? What number is two spaces after 900?

Check the answer

One space is 20, because five equal steps of 20 make 100. Two spaces after 900 is 940. Draw the five spaces and count 900, 920, 940, 960, 980, 1000.

3. Compare and order complete numbers

Greater means a larger amount; less means a smaller amount; equal means the same amount. Compare the largest place first.

Make and compare: use the digit cards 2, 5 and 8 once each. The greatest number is 852: choose the largest hundreds digit first, then the largest remaining tens digit. The smallest is 258: choose the smallest hundreds digit first, then the smallest remaining tens digit. Explain how the place of each digit changes its value.

Worked example: compare 586 and 568

5865 hundreds · 8 tens · 6 ones5685 hundreds · 6 tens · 8 ones586 > 568Eight tens is more than six tens.
  1. The hundreds match.
  2. The tens differ: 8 tens is more than 6 tens.
  3. Stop at that first different place: 586 is greater.

Order a set: 807, 780 and 870 become 780, 807, 870 from least to greatest. Reverse the order for greatest to least. Keep repeated cards: 780, 807, 807, 870 is a valid ordered set. If all places match, the numbers are equal.

At the boundary: 1000 is greater than every three-digit number. On a number line, the number farther right is greater.

Watch for: comparing the final digit first can give the wrong answer.

Try it: Order 136, 631 and 613 from least to greatest. Explain your first comparison.

Check the answer

136, 613, 631. One hundred is less than six hundreds. For the remaining pair, one ten is less than three tens.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • 704 becomes 740: build each number and point to the ones and tens.
  • Counting tick marks: trace the spaces between neighbouring marks.
  • Skipping a boundary: count 401, 400, 399, 398 while pointing to consecutive positions. After 599 comes 600.

Optional chart support: on a chart with ten numbers per row, moving down one full row adds 10. From 157, one place to the right is 158 and one row down is 167. Use this as a support for number order; it is not a separate focus.

Ready to try it independently?

Ask the child to construct a fresh number model, place a numeral on an equally spaced number line, and order a small set that includes a number close to 1000. Ask “How do you know?” A selected answer alone does not demonstrate constructing a model or explaining the scale.

Begin with two-digit numbers if needed, then return to three-digit numbers and 1000. Keep non-standard regrouping for the next place-value topic.
