Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Read, build or draw, then try the question. Open the answer after explaining your thinking.

Equal groups contain the same number. An array has equal rows and columns. To partition is to split into useful parts. Division can find the number in each group or the number of groups.

1. Build and label equal groups

First check that every group has the same number. Name how many groups and how many in each group separately. Rearranging counters does not change the total; adding or removing a counter does.

Work through it: There are 4 groups, with 6 counters in each. The total is 24 counters. Four groups with counts 6, 6, 6 and 5 would not be four equal groups of six.

Try it: Make 3 groups with 4 counters in each. How many groups? How many in each? How many altogether?

Check the answer

3 groups; 4 counters in each; 12 counters altogether. Each group must contain exactly 4.

2. Connect repeated addition and multiplication

Multiplication describes repeated equal quantities. If there are 4 trays with 6 biscuits on each tray, the repeated addition is 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24.

Students should say what the numbers mean: 4 groups of 6 make 24. The groups must be equal; a collection split into unequal groups does not model multiplication in this way.

Write 4 × 6 = 24 to match “four groups of six”. Four equal jumps of 6 land on 24. Each repeated addend or jump stands for one whole group; 4 + 6 would count a different relationship.

Try it: Five bags hold 3 shells each. Write repeated addition and a multiplication sentence.

Check the answer

3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15, and 5 × 3 = 15. There are 15 shells, not 8.

3. Read, make and turn arrays

An array organises objects into equal rows and equal columns. Twelve objects can be arranged as 3 rows of 4, 4 rows of 3, 2 rows of 6 or 6 rows of 2.

The same array can be viewed in two directions. A 3-by-4 array shows both 3 groups of 4 and 4 groups of 3; both have a total of 12.

Rotate or redraw arrays so students focus on the equal-group structure rather than the picture orientation.

Try it: Draw 2 rows of 5 dots. Turn the drawing. How many rows and columns can you see now? Did any dots disappear?

Check the answer

The original has 2 rows of 5: 10 dots. Turned through a quarter-turn it has 5 rows of 2. The total remains 10.

4. Split an array into helpful parts

Students do not need to count every item in a large array. They can split it into familiar parts.

For a 3-by-5 array, a student might see 2 groups of 5 as 10, then one more group of 5: 10 + 5 = 15.

For 6 groups of 4, they might use 5 groups of 4 = 20, then add one more 4 to make 24.

The split above separates 3 rows of 2 from 3 rows of 3. The parts contain 6 and 9 counters, so 6 + 9 = 15. Every counter belongs to one part: do not leave a gap or count a column twice.

Try it: Split 4 rows of 6 into 4 rows of 5 and 4 rows of 1. Find each part and the total.

Check the answer

4 × 5 = 20 and 4 × 1 = 4, so 20 + 4 = 24. The row widths 5 and 1 still make 6.

5. Divide by sharing equally

When the number of groups is known, sharing finds how many belong in each group. Share one object to each group in turn, then check that the shares are equal and every object is used.

Work through it: Share 12 counters equally between 3 children. Each child gets 4 counters: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. The answer counts counters per child, not children.

Show one sharing problem in several ways

A Think Board can show the same problem in several connected forms: the story, materials, a drawing/bar model, skip counting and a number sentence.

Example: $36 is shared equally between 4 people. Students can make 4 equal groups, draw 4 equal bars and skip count 4 groups of 9 to confirm that each person receives $9.

The unit matters: the answer is 9 dollars per person, not 9 people.

Try it: Share 20 counters equally among 4 groups. What does 5 mean in your answer?

Check the answer

20 ÷ 4 = 5. It means 5 counters in each group. Check: 4 groups of 5 use all 20 counters.

6. Divide into groups and connect the facts

When the size of each group is known, grouping finds how many groups can be made. Put 12 counters into groups of 3. There are 4 groups: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. Here 4 counts groups; in a sharing story it could count counters per person.

Whole and equal parts

A multiplication bar model shows several equal parts making one whole. A division bar model can show either an unknown part size or an unknown number of equal parts.

For 4 groups of 6, draw 4 equal bars labelled 6. The whole is 24. For 24 shared between 4 groups, the whole is known and each equal bar is unknown.

A Think Board can also show a grouping story, grouped drawing, repeated jumps and number sentence. Keep the answer unit visible. For 24 ÷ 6, a useful split is 12 ÷ 6 plus 12 ÷ 6: 2 groups plus 2 groups make 4 groups.

Try it: There are 18 pencils. Put 3 pencils into each pot. How many pots are needed? Give a multiplication check.

Check the answer

6 pots: 18 ÷ 3 = 6. Check: 6 × 3 = 18 pencils. Sharing 18 pencils among 3 pots would instead give 6 pencils per pot.

Teaching tips and common mistakes

Keep the learning focused: Use manageable whole-number results and one-digit multipliers or divisors. Long division, general times-table speed tests and fractional answers are not needed here.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Unequal groups: multiplication and equal-sharing models need equal group sizes.
  • Counting every object in an array: prompt students to use rows, columns, doubles or known facts.
  • Mixing up number of groups and group size: ask what each number represents in the story.
  • Assuming all division means sharing: contrast “share into 3 groups” with “make groups of 3”.
  • Writing a correct number without a unit: identify whether the answer is people, dollars, groups, objects or another quantity.

Adjust the support

  • Support: use counters, cups, egg cartons, grid paper and pre-drawn bar models.
  • Core: move between an array, repeated addition, a bar model and a spoken equal-group statement.
  • Extension: ask students to find several different arrays for the same total and explain why each is valid.

Learning check: Choose a fresh example, show a model or useful calculation, and explain why your answer fits. Revisit the matching focus if you cannot yet explain it.

An adult may read directions aloud. Start with objects or drawings, then reduce support as the learner becomes confident.
