About This Year 2 Maths Topic

In this unit, children acquire data about categorical variables through surveys, observation, experiments and familiar digital tools.

Categorical data places responses or objects into named groups. Examples include:

  • favourite fruits;
  • favourite sports teams;
  • ways students travel to school;
  • types of classroom rubbish;
  • weather observations;
  • types of animals observed;
  • yes or no survey responses.

Children learn to:

  • ask a clear question;
  • decide what data is needed;
  • collect responses or observations;
  • sort information into relevant categories;
  • record data in a list or table;
  • count the number of responses in each category;
  • identify the most and least common responses;
  • decide whether the collected data answers the question;
  • review and improve survey questions;
  • explore ways data can be observed, collected, sorted and recorded.

These activities support Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 content descriptor AC9M2ST01 and its associated elaborations.

The Big Idea for Parents

A useful data investigation begins with a clear question.

The question determines what information should be collected, which categories are needed and how the results should be recorded.

The basic Year 2 data investigation process is:

  1. Ask a question.
  2. Decide what data is needed.
  3. Choose a collection method.
  4. Collect responses or observations.
  5. Sort the data into categories.
  6. Record the data in a list or table.
  7. Count and compare the categories.
  8. Use the results to answer the original question.

Children should understand that a table is not only for displaying results. It also helps organise information so that patterns and comparisons are easier to see.

Teacher Guide for Parents

Teachers help children understand that data investigations are planned processes. Children should know why they are collecting information before they begin recording responses.

At home, follow this sequence: ask, plan, collect, sort, record, count, compare and answer.

1. Introduce Categorical Data

Categorical data groups responses by a name, description or type.

Examples include:

  • apple, banana, orange or pear;
  • walk, car, bus or bicycle;
  • recycle, reuse or throw away;
  • sunny, cloudy, rainy or windy;
  • yes or no.

The categories describe qualities or groups rather than measurements such as height or mass.

2. Begin with a Clear Question

A survey question should be easy to understand and should produce responses that can be sorted.

Suitable Year 2 questions include:

  • What is your favourite fruit?
  • How do students travel to school?
  • Which day of the week is most popular?
  • Which playground activity do children prefer?
  • Which materials in our rubbish can be recycled?

Questions that are too broad or unclear may produce responses that are difficult to classify.

3. Decide Whether the Question Can Be Answered with Data

Ask children:

  • Can we collect information to answer this question?
  • Who or what should we observe?
  • What responses might we receive?
  • How will we record the information?

A question such as “What is the best fruit?” is based on personal opinion. A more useful survey question is “Which fruit is chosen as the favourite by the most students?”

4. Choose Relevant Categories

Categories should match the question and allow each response to be recorded clearly.

For a travel-to-school investigation, categories might be:

  • walk;
  • car;
  • bus;
  • bicycle or scooter;
  • other.

An “other” category can be useful when an unexpected response is possible.

5. Avoid Overlapping Categories

A response should usually fit clearly into one category.

For example, using separate categories called “car” and “vehicle” would create overlap because a car is a vehicle.

Revise the categories before collecting data if they are confusing.

6. Conduct a Favourite Things Survey

Ask a small group of people the same question.

Record each response immediately so that no answer is forgotten or counted twice.

Children can begin with a simple list:

  • Alex – apple;
  • Mia – banana;
  • Sam – apple;
  • Leo – orange.

7. Convert a Response List into Categories

After collecting responses, group matching answers together.

For example:

  • apple: Alex, Sam;
  • banana: Mia;
  • orange: Leo.

This makes the frequency of each response easier to count.

8. Record Data in a Table

A simple data table should include clear headings.

Favourite fruit Number of responses
Apple 2
Banana 1
Orange 1

Children should check that every response from the original list appears in the table.

9. Count Responses Accurately

Use one-to-one counting so that every response contributes one count.

Children can:

  • cross off each response after recording it;
  • move a counter for each response;
  • use tally marks if familiar;
  • recount each category;
  • compare the category total with the number surveyed.

10. Check the Overall Total

Add the number of responses in all categories.

The total should equal the number of people surveyed or objects observed.

Useful checking strategy

If 12 people answered the survey, the frequencies in the table should add to 12.

11. Identify the Most Common Response

The most common or most popular category has the greatest frequency.

Ask children to compare the numbers rather than relying only on the order of the rows.

12. Identify the Least Common Response

The least common or least popular category has the smallest frequency.

There may be more than one least common category when two or more categories have the same smallest result.

13. Recognise Equal Frequencies

Two categories can have the same number of responses.

For example, if three students choose apples and three choose bananas, the two categories are equally popular.

14. Answer the Original Question

Children should use the data to write or state a conclusion.

For example:

“Apple was the most popular fruit because 6 students chose it. Pear was the least popular because only 1 student chose it.”

15. Decide Whether the Data Is Sufficient

Ask whether enough information was collected to answer the question.

Consider:

  • Was the correct group surveyed?
  • Were enough people asked?
  • Were all responses recorded?
  • Were the categories suitable?
  • Did the question match the investigation?

16. Investigate Rubbish, Recycling and Reuse

Collect a safe sample of clean classroom or household items.

Sort them into categories such as:

  • throw away;
  • recycle;
  • reuse.

Record the number of objects in each category.

17. Use Clear Rules for Waste Categories

Before sorting, discuss what each category means.

  • Throw away: an item that cannot be reused or placed in the available recycling system.
  • Recycle: an item accepted for recycling in the relevant local system.
  • Reuse: an item that can be used again without being remade.

Local recycling rules can vary, so adults should help children confirm which category is suitable.

18. Answer “How Much Rubbish Is Really Rubbish?”

Compare the number of items in each waste category.

Ask:

  • How many objects must be thrown away?
  • How many can be recycled?
  • How many can be reused?
  • Which category is largest?
  • Does the data answer the investigation question?

19. Collect Data Through Observation

Observation means watching events or objects and recording what occurs.

Children might observe:

  • ways students arrive at school;
  • types of birds visiting a garden;
  • weather conditions during a week;
  • colours of passing cars;
  • playground activities chosen at lunchtime.

20. Observe Before Finalising Categories

Sometimes children need to watch the event first before they know which categories are relevant.

For example, an initial travel-to-school observation may reveal categories such as walking, car, bus, bicycle, scooter and mixed travel.

21. Record Travel-to-School Data

Create a table with one row for each travel category.

Travel method Number of students
Walk _____
Car _____
Bus _____
Bicycle or scooter _____
Other _____

22. Collect Data Through a Simple Experiment

A simple experiment can generate categorical results.

Examples include:

  • spinning a colour spinner several times;
  • drawing coloured counters from a bag;
  • testing whether objects roll, slide or do both;
  • sorting materials by whether they absorb water;
  • observing whether seeds germinate or do not germinate.

Record the category for every trial.

23. Create a Digital Survey

Familiar software can be used to create a simple survey.

The survey should include:

  • a clear title;
  • one question at a time;
  • simple response choices;
  • categories that do not overlap;
  • an “other” option when needed.

24. Use AI Tools Responsibly

A teacher or parent may use a generative artificial intelligence tool to suggest survey questions or possible response categories.

Children should still review the suggestions and decide:

  • whether the question is clear;
  • whether the language is suitable;
  • whether the response choices match the question;
  • whether any categories overlap;
  • whether personal information is being requested.

Digital safety reminder

Children should use digital and AI tools with adult supervision and should not enter private identifying information into public tools.

25. Interpret Digital Survey Responses

Digital survey tools may automatically group responses.

Children should still check:

  • how many responses were collected;
  • whether every response fits a category;
  • which category is most common;
  • which category is least common;
  • whether the total is correct;
  • whether the data answers the question.

26. Review and Improve a Survey

After using a survey, ask whether any changes are needed.

A question may need improvement when:

  • people interpret it in different ways;
  • important response choices are missing;
  • two categories mean nearly the same thing;
  • respondents can choose more than one answer when only one is needed;
  • the responses do not answer the original investigation question.

27. Explore First Nations Australian Data Practices Respectfully

First Nations Australian communities have longstanding ways of observing, recording and sharing information about Country, seasons, plants, animals, weather and community activities.

Learning activities should use locally appropriate, community-approved sources wherever possible and avoid treating all First Nations Australian knowledge systems as identical.

Children may explore examples such as:

  • recording seasonal environmental observations;
  • sorting plants or animals by observable features;
  • noticing patterns in weather or animal behaviour;
  • organising observations about changes on Country;
  • using symbols, oral records, diagrams or other approved representations.

28. Distinguish Data from an Opinion

Data is collected information. An opinion is what someone thinks or prefers.

A favourite-fruit survey collects data about opinions, but the conclusion should describe what the responses show rather than claim that one fruit is objectively best.

29. Compare Two Categories

Ask children to use comparison language.

For example:

  • more than;
  • fewer than;
  • the same as;
  • most common;
  • least common;
  • difference between.

Example:

“Four more students travelled by car than by bus.”

30. Explain a Data Conclusion

A complete Year 2 data conclusion should refer to evidence from the table.

Encourage children to state:

  1. what the investigation asked;
  2. which category had the most responses;
  3. which category had the least responses;
  4. any equal results;
  5. whether the question was answered.

Recommended Teaching Sequence at Home

  1. Recognise categorical data.
  2. Pose a clear question.
  3. Predict possible categories.
  4. Choose a survey, observation or experiment.
  5. Collect and record responses.
  6. Sort the responses into categories.
  7. Create a list or table.
  8. Count each category.
  9. Check the overall total.
  10. Identify the most and least common categories.
  11. Compare category frequencies.
  12. Answer the original question.
  13. Decide whether the data was sufficient.
  14. Review and improve the investigation.

Common Misconceptions and How Parents Can Help

Beginning to collect data without a question

State the investigation question first so children know which information is relevant.

Using categories that overlap

Check that each response can be placed clearly into one category or explain when multiple selections are allowed.

Leaving out unexpected responses

Include an “other” category when the complete range of responses is not known.

Counting the same response twice

Cross off or mark each response after transferring it into the table.

Missing a response

Compare the table total with the number of people surveyed or objects observed.

Choosing the first category as the most popular

Compare the frequencies. The order of categories in a table does not determine popularity.

Thinking there can be only one least common category

Two or more categories can share the smallest frequency.

Confusing a category name with its frequency

The category describes the group, while the frequency tells how many responses belong to it.

Writing a conclusion not supported by the data

Ask children to point to the numbers in the table that support their statement.

Assuming all collected data answers the question

Return to the original question and check whether the categories and responses provide the required information.

Accepting digital survey results without checking

Check totals, missing responses, categories and whether the question was interpreted consistently.

Treating First Nations Australian practices as one uniform system

Use locally relevant and community-approved sources and recognise the diversity of First Nations communities, Countries and knowledge practices.

Simple Home Data Activities

Activity 1: Family Favourite Fruit Survey

Ask family members to choose their favourite fruit. Record each response, organise the results in a table and identify the most and least common choices.

Focus: AC9M2ST01_E1.

Activity 2: Favourite Day Survey

Survey a small group about their favourite day of the week. Compare the frequencies and identify any equal results.

Focus: Surveys and category comparison.

Activity 3: Recycle, Reuse or Throw Away

Sort a safe collection of clean household items into recycle, reuse and throw-away categories.

Record the number of items in each category.

Focus: AC9M2ST01_E2.

Activity 4: Travel Observation

Observe how people travel past a chosen location for a short period. Create suitable categories and record each observation.

Focus: AC9M2ST01_E4.

Activity 5: Weather List and Table

Record the weather each day for one week using categories such as sunny, cloudy, rainy and windy.

Count how often each category occurs.

Focus: Observation data.

Activity 6: Colour Spinner Experiment

Spin a colour spinner 20 times and record the result of each spin.

Sort the results by colour and display them in a table.

Focus: Experimental categorical data.

Activity 7: Digital Survey

With adult supervision, create a short digital survey with clear response choices.

Review whether the question and categories worked well.

Focus: AC9M2ST01_E3.

Activity 8: Improve the Question

Examine an unclear question such as “What food do you like?” and rewrite it as a question that produces easier-to-sort responses.

Focus: Survey design.

Activity 9: Garden Observation

Observe birds, insects, leaves or flowers in a garden and create categories after noticing which types appear.

Focus: Observation and classification.

Activity 10: Data Detective

Give your child a completed table and ask them to identify the most common, least common and equal categories before writing a conclusion.

Focus: Interpreting lists and tables.

Questions Parents Can Ask

  • What question are you trying to answer?
  • What data do you need to collect?
  • Will you use a survey, observation or experiment?
  • Who or what will provide the data?
  • What categories will you use?
  • Do any categories overlap?
  • Do you need an “other” category?
  • How will you record each response?
  • Have you counted every response once?
  • What is the total number of responses?
  • Does the table total match the number collected?
  • Which category is most common?
  • Which category is least common?
  • Are any categories equal?
  • How many more responses are in one category?
  • What does the data show?
  • Does the data answer the original question?
  • Was any information missing?
  • How could the question be improved?
  • How can you check your conclusion?

Supporting Different Learners at Home

Children Who Need More Support

  • begin with a yes-or-no question;
  • use only two or three categories;
  • use picture cards to represent categories;
  • collect a small number of responses;
  • record names or objects before creating a table;
  • move one counter for each response;
  • colour-code categories;
  • cross off each response after counting it;
  • compare only the most and least common categories;
  • allow oral conclusions before written explanations.

Children Ready for Extension

  • design surveys with four or more categories;
  • compare data collected from two groups;
  • identify the difference between category frequencies;
  • create a survey containing an “other” response;
  • review surveys for overlapping categories;
  • collect data on two different days;
  • compare observation and survey methods;
  • design a digital survey and revise it after testing;
  • write several conclusions supported by the same table;
  • explain why a dataset may not answer the question.

Quick Parent Check

Use a short survey or observation activity to check whether your child understands the main ideas.

  1. Pose a clear question that can be answered with categorical data.
  2. Suggest suitable response categories.
  3. Collect at least five responses or observations.
  4. Record the raw data in a list.
  5. Sort the information into categories.
  6. Display the category frequencies in a table.
  7. Check that the total is correct.
  8. Identify the most and least common categories.
  9. State one comparison between categories.
  10. Explain whether the data answers the question.

A child shows secure understanding when they can plan a simple investigation, organise categorical data accurately and use the results to answer the original question.

Quick Exit Ticket

  1. Write one question that can be answered with categorical data.
  2. Name three suitable response categories.
  3. Explain how a table helps organise the results.

Home Learning Summary

Data skills develop when children investigate meaningful questions and take part in the complete process of collecting, sorting, recording and interpreting information.

Use this simple routine:

  1. ask a clear question;
  2. choose relevant categories;
  3. collect responses or observations;
  4. record every item once;
  5. sort the data into categories;
  6. display the results in a list or table;
  7. check the total;
  8. compare the category frequencies;
  9. answer the investigation question;
  10. review how the investigation could be improved.
