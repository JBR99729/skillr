Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Read, build or draw, then try the question. Open the answer after explaining your thinking.

A model is a drawing, collection, table or number sentence that represents a situation. The cost is the price to pay; change is money returned. A solution includes what the answer means.

1. Understand the problem and choose a model
  1. Understand: What is happening? What do we know? What do we need to find?
  2. Represent: Draw, model or organise the information so the relationships are visible.
  3. Choose: Select an operation and calculation strategy that fits the situation.
  4. Solve: Carry out the calculation and keep track of the units.
  5. Check: Does the answer make sense? Is it too large, too small or impossible?
  6. Communicate: Write a sentence that answers the actual question.

Students should become comfortable moving backwards if needed. If a calculation gives an unreasonable answer, return to the representation or operation choice and revise the model.

Look for the relationship

Words such as “more”, “left” or “each” can appear in different kinds of problems, so operation choice should come from the mathematical relationship.

  • Addition: combine two or more parts to find a total.
  • Subtraction: find a missing part, an amount left or a difference.
  • Multiplication: combine repeated equal groups.
  • Division: share a total equally or find how many equal groups can be made.

Ask students to explain: “I chose ___ because ___.”

Try it: There are 14 children on a bus. Some get off and 9 remain. What is unknown? Write a matching number sentence.

Check the answer

The number who got off is unknown. 14 − __ = 9 or 9 + __ = 14. Five children got off. A labelled drawing can show 14 as the whole, 9 as one part and 5 as the other.

2. Solve joining, taking-away and comparison stories

Ask what each number counts. Joining finds a whole; taking away finds what remains; comparing finds the difference. A missing starting amount may need addition even if the story describes something being taken away.

Work through it: A class has 17 red counters and 8 blue counters. 17 + 3 = 20, then 20 + 5 = 25 counters. To compare 25 counters with 18, count up: 18 → 20 is 2 and 20 → 25 is 5. The difference is 7 counters.

Try it: After giving away 6 stickers, Ava has 15. How many did she have at the start?

Check the answer

The starting amount is the whole: 15 + 6 = 21 stickers. Check: 21 − 6 = 15.

3. Model equal groups and equal shares

Six students each contribute $5 to a class project. The situation has 6 equal groups of $5, so multiplication or repeated addition is suitable:

5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = $30, so 6 groups of $5 make $30.

Students can represent the problem using 6 equal bars, an array of 6 groups, skip counting by 5s or a number sentence.

Choose multiplication when equal groups are combined. Choose division when you know the total and need a group size or a group count. Draw or build the groups before calculating if the story is unclear.

Try it: A teacher shares 24 counters equally among 6 learners. What is the answer and what does it count?

Check the answer

24 ÷ 6 = 4 counters per learner. Six shares of 4 use all 24 counters.

4. Work with whole-dollar money

A student buys a book for $12 and a game for $8. To find the total cost, combine the two parts: 12 + 8 = $20.

If the student pays with $25, the missing part is the change: 25 − 20 = $5.

A suitable model is a bar showing the $20 cost and the $5 change making the $25 payment. The final answer must include dollars.

Work through it: A notebook costs $4 and a pencil case costs $7. Their total is $11. Pay with a $20 note. Count from 11 to 20, or calculate 20 − 11: the change is $9. Three identical $4 notebooks cost $12.

Australian coins and notes have different values. Two $2 coins and one $1 coin make $5; five $1 coins also make $5. Count the value, not only the number or size of coins. Use drawings labelled with real Australian denominations.

Try it: Four identical markers cost $12 altogether. What does one marker cost? Could you buy all four with $10?

Check the answer

12 ÷ 4 = $3 per marker. $10 is not enough for all four; you need $2 more.

5. Plan more than one step

Work through it: There are 3 packs with 5 cards in each, plus 4 loose cards. First find the packs: 3 × 5 = 15. Then add the loose cards: 15 + 4 = 19 cards. Say why each step belongs in the plan.

Use a calendar as your model

A calendar can act as the mathematical model when a problem asks how many days remain until an event or until the end of the year.

Students can count the remaining days in the current month, add the days in complete future months, then add any final partial month. The representation should make clear which days are included and which date is the endpoint.

For example, to count days after 28 April up to and including 3 May, count 29 and 30 April, then 1, 2 and 3 May: 5 days. For days left in a year, use a real calendar, agree whether today is counted, and record the remaining month parts before adding. This is a modelling investigation, not a requirement to memorise month lengths.

Try it: You have $20. Buy 2 books at $6 each. How much money remains? Show both steps.

Check the answer

Two books cost 2 × $6 = $12. Then $20 − $12 = $8 remains. $12 is the cost, not the final answer to “how much remains”.

6. Check, explain and adapt the solution

A bare number is usually incomplete in a modelling task. Students should name what the number represents.

  • “The class collected $30 altogether.”
  • “There are 4 full teams and 2 students left.”
  • “Each person receives $9.”

Checking the unit is a simple way to catch many modelling errors.

Explain leftovers in the situation

Practical division problems often require students to interpret what happens when the total cannot be divided exactly.

Example: 22 students are placed into teams of 5. Four full teams use 20 students and 2 students remain. The calculation alone is not the final answer; students must explain what should happen with the 2 remaining students in the context.

Possible real-world decisions might include changing the team sizes or forming an additional smaller group. The mathematical model supports the decision but does not automatically choose it.

Sometimes information is missing. “A child buys a toy and pays $10” does not give enough information to find change: we need the toy’s price. If an answer looks wrong, check the model, quantities, calculation and units.

Try it: There are 17 children. Each car can take at most 4 children. Are 4 cars enough? Explain.

Check the answer

No. Four cars take 16 children, so one child would have no place. Five cars are needed; the fifth need not be full. This is a decision about the situation, without needing a formal remainder algorithm.

Teaching tips and common mistakes

Keep the learning focused: Keep stories short and use whole Australian dollars. Explain leftover objects in everyday language; formal remainder notation and complex algebra are not needed.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Choosing an operation from one keyword: ask students to describe the relationship between the quantities first.
  • Calculating before representing: a quick diagram or bar model often reveals what the problem is actually asking.
  • Ignoring the unit: a correct numerical result can still be an incomplete answer.
  • Forcing a remainder to disappear: practical division sometimes needs a decision about leftover people or objects.
  • Not checking reasonableness: compare the answer with the original quantities before accepting it.

Adjust the support

  • Support: provide a six-step modelling checklist and partially completed bar models or Think Boards.
  • Core: give mixed everyday problems where students must choose among all four operations and justify the choice.
  • Extension: ask students to change one condition in a problem, predict how the model changes and solve the new version.

For First Nations Australian learning contexts, use appropriately authorised local or curriculum resources and follow their guidance. Do not invent cultural stories, rules or meanings. Invite learners to show the relevant pattern, grouping or additive relationship with materials and a number sentence.

Choose an appropriate game context

The curriculum includes modelling equal grouping and sharing through First Nations Australian children’s instructive games, including Yangamini from the Tiwi Island Peoples. When using this elaboration, use curriculum-authorised or community-appropriate source material and avoid inventing cultural details. Keep the mathematical focus on the equal-group relationships, the number sentence and the interpretation of the solution within the source context.

Learning check: Choose a fresh example, show a model or useful calculation, and explain why your answer fits. Revisit the matching focus if you cannot yet explain it.

An adult may read directions aloud. Start with objects or drawings, then reduce support as the learner becomes confident.
