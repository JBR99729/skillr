Fractions in everyday life

Learning intention: Represent equal parts of shapes, objects, measures, distances and events.

Worked example

Two unequal pieces are not halves.

Learn the skill

1. Equal parts and folding

Name the whole first. Halves, quarters and eighths must be equal parts of that same whole. Fold a paper strip in half, halve every part again, then repeat: 2, 4, 8 equal parts. Count regions, not crease lines.

Try it · Fold a strip into two equal parts. What fraction is one part?

One half.

2. Everyday objects and measures

Use a paper sandwich model to show equal servings. For capacity, two half-cup measures or four quarter-cup measures fill the same full cup. Use the same full measure as the whole. Half the height in an irregular bottle does not necessarily mean half the capacity.

Try it · Split a sandwich into four equal parts. What are two parts together?

Two quarters, which is one half.

3. Lengths, positions and events

Mark both endpoints of the whole distance. Partition the distance into four equal intervals: the second division is halfway. A game with four equal playing periods has four quarters; breaks are separate. Half of a 60-minute hour is 30 minutes; a quarter is 15 minutes. These are duration models, rather than clock-reading drills.

Try it · Mark one quarter of a strip with eight equal spaces. How many spaces do you mark?

Two spaces.

Common mistakes

Using unequal pieces. Compare the parts against each other before naming the fraction.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Open one focus at a time. Explain the model, try the question and check the reasoning.

1. Equal parts and folding

Name the whole first. Halves, quarters and eighths must be equal parts of that same whole. Fold a paper strip in half, halve every part again, then repeat: 2, 4, 8 equal parts. Count regions, not crease lines.

Try it: What fraction is shaded?

Check the answer

One eighth: 1 of 8 equal parts of the whole strip. Two unequal pieces are not halves.

2. Everyday objects and measures

Use a paper sandwich model to show equal servings. For capacity, two half-cup measures or four quarter-cup measures fill the same full cup. Use the same full measure as the whole. Half the height in an irregular bottle does not necessarily mean half the capacity.

Try it: How many quarter-cups make a half-cup?

Check the answer

Two quarter-cups. Compare them with the same full-cup whole.

3. Lengths, positions and events

Mark both endpoints of the whole distance. Partition the distance into four equal intervals: the second division is halfway. A game with four equal playing periods has four quarters; breaks are separate. Half of a 60-minute hour is 30 minutes; a quarter is 15 minutes. These are duration models, rather than clock-reading drills.

Try it: A track is split into four equal stages. What fraction is completed after two stages?

Check the answer

Two quarters, which is one half of the whole track.

Try it together
  1. Fold and prove: fold paper into halves, then quarters, then eighths. Open it and check that all parts are equal.
  2. Fair-share challenge: draw two same-sized paper sandwiches, one split into equal halves and one into unequal pieces. Explain which shows fair halves.
  3. Measure the whole: fill one cup using halves and quarters and record how many equal parts make the whole.
  4. Human number line: mark the start, halfway point and finish of a path, then discuss what “halfway” means.
  5. Time connection: connect 15 minutes to quarter of an hour and 30 minutes to half an hour.

Support: Use real objects, fewer choices and an adult read-aloud. Core: Explain and record the model independently. Extend: Create a new example or repair an error while staying within this skill.

Teaching tips and common mistakes
  • Two pieces automatically mean halves. No: the 2 pieces must be equal.
  • A larger denominator means a larger piece. For the same whole, eighths are smaller than quarters because the whole is split into more equal parts.
  • The whole can change halfway through a comparison. Fractions can only be compared fairly when the same-sized whole is being considered.
  • Fractions only describe shapes. They also describe measures, positions, distances, durations and events.

Ask the learner to explain what each mark, unit or instruction means. Rebuild the model when an answer is correct by guessing. A written answer alone does not establish practical or digital performance.

Curriculum code and learning goal

AC9M2M02: identify common uses and represent halves, quarters and eighths in relation to shapes, objects and events.

  • E1: food items divided into equal halves, quarters and eighths
  • E2: half- and quarter-cup/spoon measures
  • E3: paper folding into equal halves and quarters
  • E4: halves and quarters describing lengths, positions and distances
  • E5: halves and quarters describing durations and sporting-event sections
