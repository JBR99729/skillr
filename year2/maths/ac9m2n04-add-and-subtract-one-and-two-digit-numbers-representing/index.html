A whole is the total; parts combine to make it. A difference tells how far apart two amounts are. A number sentence uses numbers and signs to show a relationship.

Try a worked example together

Use tens and ones on an open number line

Find 36 + 28. Start at 36 and split 28 into 20 and 8. Break the 8 into 4 and 4 so you can land on a friendly ten.

  1. Start at 36 and jump forward 20 to 56.
  2. Jump 4 to reach 60, then jump the remaining 4 to reach 64.
  3. Check the jumps: 20 + 4 + 4 = 28, so you added the full 28.

Try it: Use an open number line to work out 71 − 16. Show where you start and how you split the amount you take away.

Check the answer

One way is 71 − 10 = 61, then 61 − 1 = 60 and 60 − 5 = 55. The backward jumps total 16. Check: 55 + 16 = 71.

1. Show the whole, parts and missing amount

Part-part-whole models help students see how addition and subtraction are connected. If 24 and 13 are the parts, then 37 is the whole:

24 + 13 = 37, 13 + 24 = 37, 37 − 24 = 13 and 37 − 13 = 24.

Ask students to name the whole and the two parts before writing the number sentence. This prevents students from choosing an operation only because of a keyword.

Match a story to a number sentence

For each problem, students should identify what is known, what is unknown and how the quantities are connected.

Example: Mia has 28 stickers and gets 16 more. A bar model shows 28 and 16 as two parts of an unknown whole. The matching number sentence is 28 + 16 = ?.

Example: A box held 45 pencils and 17 were used. The whole is 45, one part is 17 and the missing part is the number left. The number sentence is 45 − 17 = ?.

The equals sign means has the same value as. Both 24 + 13 = 37 and 37 = 24 + 13 are true. A word such as “more” does not tell you the operation by itself.

Try it: Luca has 18 cards. He has 7 more than Mia. How many cards does Mia have? Name the whole and the missing part.

Check the answer

18 is the larger amount. Mia’s cards and the difference of 7 make 18: __ + 7 = 18. Mia has 11 cards. Check: 11 + 7 = 18.

2. Add with known facts, tens and ones

Regroup useful parts

For 6 + 4 + 7, join 6 and 4 first to make 10, then add 7: 17. The same three amounts are used once each. This can be easier than counting all the objects.

Use a known double

Known doubles can be adjusted by one when the addends are close.

7 + 8 = 7 + 7 + 1 = 14 + 1 = 15.

Students should explain why this works: 8 has been partitioned into 7 and 1, so the total has not changed.

Bridge through a ten

Split one addend so the first jump lands on a friendly ten.

7 + 8: split 8 into 3 and 5. Then 7 + 3 = 10 and 10 + 5 = 15.

47 + 6: split 6 into 3 and 3. Then 47 + 3 = 50 and 50 + 3 = 53.

For subtraction, bridge through a ten in the opposite direction; for example, 52 − 7 can be seen as 52 − 2 − 5.

Keep the value when you split

Separate two-digit numbers into tens and ones, then recombine the partial results.

34 + 28: 30 + 20 = 50 and 4 + 8 = 12, so 50 + 12 = 62.

56 + 37: 50 + 30 = 80 and 6 + 7 = 13, so 80 + 13 = 93.

Students may jot down the partial amounts, but should still be able to explain where each number came from.

Zero and a useful check

Zero ones do not change the amount. For example, 34 + 20 = 54 because 30 + 20 = 50 and 4 + 0 = 4.

Addition and subtraction can also check each other. If 63 − 27 = 36, then 36 + 27 should return to 63.

Work through it: For 38 + 24, add 20 to reach 58, then split 4 into 2 and 2. 58 + 2 = 60; 60 + 2 = 62. On a hundreds chart with ten numbers in a row, adding 20 moves down two rows; adding 4 means counting forward four numbers, wrapping to the next row if needed.

Try it: Solve 46 + 7. Show how you reach the next ten without losing part of 7.

Check the answer

Split 7 into 4 and 3. 46 + 4 = 50; 50 + 3 = 53. The parts 4 and 3 still make 7.

3. Subtract and check with addition

Count back or count up

On a number line, make efficient jumps in tens and ones rather than counting every number.

To calculate 52 − 38, count up from 38: +2 to 40, then +12 to 52. The total increase is 14, so 52 − 38 = 14.

On a hundreds chart, moving down one row adds 10, moving up one row subtracts 10, moving right within a row adds 1 and moving left within a row subtracts 1. At a row end, continue counting at the start of the next row; do not wrap a horizontal move as though it were a move down.

Work through it: For 52 − 7, subtract 2 to reach 50, then subtract the remaining 5: 45. Check: 45 + 7 = 52. For 61 − 58, counting up is short: 58 → 60 is 2, then 60 → 61 is 1, so the difference is 3.

When using tens and ones, exchange one ten for ten ones if you need more ones. For 42 − 18, rename 42 as 3 tens and 12 ones. Remove 1 ten and 8 ones: 2 tens and 4 ones remain, so the answer is 24. Do not subtract whichever digit is smaller from whichever is larger.

Try it: Find 63 − 8. Then check with addition.

Check the answer

63 − 3 = 60; 60 − 5 = 55. Check: 55 + 8 = 63. A different correct strategy is welcome if you can explain it.

Teaching tips and common mistakes

Keep the learning focused: Stay with one- and two-digit operands. Use flexible mental methods, drawings and jottings; a formal column algorithm is not required.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Counting by ones when a larger jump is available: prompt students to look for tens, doubles or a friendly benchmark.
  • Changing the value while partitioning: every split must recombine to the original number.
  • Writing a number sentence that does not match the story: identify the whole and parts first.
  • Forgetting the second part of a bridge: after reaching 10 or the next ten, the leftover amount still has to be added or subtracted.
  • Ignoring place value: 4 ones + 8 ones makes 12 ones, which is 1 ten and 2 ones.
  • Treating subtraction as only take-away: subtraction can also compare quantities or find a missing part/difference.

Adjust the support

  • Support: use counters, ten frames, base-ten blocks, open number lines and completed bar-model templates.
  • Core: ask students to solve the same calculation with two strategies and choose the more efficient method.
  • Extension: give missing-number equations such as 38 + □ = 65 or □ − 27 = 41 and ask students to justify their strategy.

For First Nations Australian learning contexts, use appropriately authorised local or curriculum resources and follow their guidance. Do not invent cultural stories, rules or meanings. Invite learners to show the relevant pattern, grouping or additive relationship with materials and a number sentence.

Quick learning check

A box started with 65 cards. There are now 49. Find how many were taken and show a short way to check.

Check the answer

16 cards. Count up from 49 to 50 (+1), then to 65 (+15). Check: 65 − 16 = 49.

An adult may read directions aloud. Start with objects or drawings, then reduce support as the learner becomes confident.
