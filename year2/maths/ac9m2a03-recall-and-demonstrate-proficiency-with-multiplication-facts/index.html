Twos facts, doubling and halving

Core idea: Connect the two times table with doubling and related division facts. Double 7 is 14, so 2 × 7 = 14 and sharing 14 equally into 2 groups gives 7 in each group.

Learn the skill

1. Double: join two equal groups

Double means two equal amounts together. Double 5 is 5 + 5 = 10. It does not mean add 2.

Try it · What is double 8?

16, because 8 + 8 = 16.

2. Halve: share fairly into two groups

Share twelve counters equally between two children. Each child gets six. Half of 12 is 6, so 12 ÷ 2 = 6 counters each.

Try it · Share 18 counters equally between two groups. How many are in each?

9, because 9 + 9 = 18.

3. Connect facts and choose an operation

Two equal groups of eight make sixteen. The whole is 16; the equal parts are 8 and 8.

Try it · What two facts link double 6 and half of 12?

Double 6 is 12, and half of 12 is 6.

Common mistakes

Halving by removing two. Share the whole into two equal groups and count one group.

Learn the skill

Key concept: small steps, clear examples

Read, build or draw, then try the question. Open the answer after explaining your thinking.

Double means two equal amounts joined. Half is one of two equal parts of a whole. A pair is a group of two. “Twice as many” means two times the amount.

1. Double: join two equal groups

Double means two equal amounts together. Double 5 is 5 + 5 = 10. It does not mean add 2.

Use a double you know

To find double 6, add one counter to each group of five. The total increases by 2: 10 + 2 = 12. To find double 4, remove one from each group: 10 − 2 = 8.

Try with support: Start from double 6 = 12. What is double 7? Explain how both groups change.

Try independently: Use double 5 = 10 to find double 4.

Check your reasoning

Double 7 is 14: add one to each of two groups, so add 2 altogether. Double 4 is 8: remove one from each group, so subtract 2 altogether.

2. Multiply: count equal groups

Five tables each have two pencils. There are five groups of two: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10, so 5 × 2 = 10. Count 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.

Same total, different grouping

Two groups of six give 2 × 6 = 12. Six groups of two give 6 × 2 = 12. Rearrange the same twelve counters to compare them. The total stays the same; the number and size of the groups change.

Try with support: Count six pairs, then say 6 × 2 without counting again.

Try independently: What are 2 × 7 and 2 × 0? Explain the zero fact.

Check your reasoning

Six pairs make 12. 2 × 7 = 14. 2 × 0 = 0: two empty groups contain no objects.

3. Halve: share fairly into two groups

Share twelve counters equally between two children. Each child gets six. Half of 12 is 6, so 12 ÷ 2 = 6 counters each.

Check by doubling: 6 + 6 = 12. Both parts must be equal. Subtracting 2 from twelve gives ten; that is not halving.

Try with support: Share 16 counters between two children. Complete 16 ÷ 2 = □ counters each.

Try independently: Find half of 18, then check by doubling.

Check your reasoning

16 ÷ 2 = 8 counters each. Half of 18 is 9; 9 + 9 = 18.

4. Divide: make groups of two

Now put the same twelve counters into pairs. Each group contains two counters. Count the groups: there are six pairs, so 12 ÷ 2 = 6 pairs.

Compare: Sharing asks “How many in each of two groups?” Making pairs asks “How many groups of two?” Both use 12 ÷ 2 = 6, but the answer describes a different quantity.

Try with support: Draw pairs for ten socks. How many pairs?

Try independently: Fourteen students work in pairs. How many pairs can you make?

Check your reasoning

Ten socks make 5 pairs: 10 ÷ 2 = 5. Fourteen students make 7 pairs: 14 ÷ 2 = 7. The answers count pairs, not students in each pair.

5. Connect facts and choose an operation

Two equal groups of eight make sixteen. The whole is 16; the equal parts are 8 and 8.

Related facts: 2 × 8 = 16; 8 × 2 = 16; 16 ÷ 2 = 8; 16 ÷ 8 = 2. Sharing sixteen into two equal groups gives eight each. Grouping sixteen into groups of eight gives two groups.

Missing number: 2 × □ = 14. Half of fourteen is seven, so the missing number is 7. Check: 2 × 7 = 14.

Try independently: Half of a collection is 9. How many objects are in the whole collection?

Check your reasoning

The whole has 18 objects. Double the known half: 9 + 9 = 18. Halving 9 again would not recover the whole.

Double or halve?

Choose the operation that reaches the target: start at 6, target 12; start at 16, target 8. Explain your choice before calculating. Make more rounds using even starts when halving and keeping whole-number results within 20.

Check your reasoning

Double 6 to reach 12. Halve 16 to reach 8. Doubling makes two equal amounts together; halving finds one of two equal parts.

Teaching tips and common mistakes

Keep the learning focused: Use twos facts, doubling and halving, with core fluency within 20. General tables, odd-number halving into fractions and formal division algorithms are not required.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Counting by ones — Use pairs and skip count by twos.
  • Double and half mixed — Double joins two equal parts; half splits into two equal parts.
  • Unequal shares — When sharing into two groups, check they contain the same number.
  • Sharing and grouping confused — Division by 2 can ask for the size of each of two equal shares or the number of pairs. Name what the answer counts.
  • Double means add two — Double joins two equal amounts. Double 7 is 14, not 9.
  • Neighbouring doubles change by one — Each of two groups changes by one, so the total changes by two.
  • Half means subtract two — Half of 12 is 6, not 10. Check by doubling.
  • The half is halved again — When the half is known and the whole is missing, double the half.

Learning check: Choose a fresh example, show a model or useful calculation, and explain why your answer fits. Revisit the matching focus if you cannot yet explain it.

An adult may read directions aloud. Start with objects or drawings, then reduce support as the learner becomes confident.
