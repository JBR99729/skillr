About This Year 2 Maths Topic

In this unit, children recognise, compare and classify shapes by referring to their features.

Children use spatial terms such as:

  • side;
  • vertex and vertices;
  • straight;
  • curved;
  • equal;
  • opposite;
  • parallel.

They sort shapes in different ways and explain the rule used for each classification.

Children also learn that turning, rotating or moving a shape does not change its name or defining features.

They investigate shapes in sporting fields and courts and use digital tools to create, move, rotate and change regular and irregular shapes.

These activities support Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 content descriptor AC9M2SP01 and its associated elaborations.

The Big Idea for Parents

Shapes are classified by their features, not by their size, colour or orientation.

A triangle remains a triangle when it is turned sideways, rotated, made larger, made smaller or coloured differently, because it still has three straight sides.

Children should describe shapes using observable features rather than relying only on the shape’s name.

For example:

“This shape is a quadrilateral because it has four straight sides. It has two pairs of opposite sides, and each pair is parallel.”

A strong shape description may include:

  • the number of sides;
  • whether the sides are straight or curved;
  • whether any sides are equal in length;
  • whether sides are opposite;
  • whether opposite sides are parallel;
  • the number of vertices;
  • whether the shape is regular or irregular.

Learning Intentions

Children will learn to:

  • recognise common two-dimensional shapes;
  • count sides accurately;
  • count vertices accurately;
  • identify straight and curved boundaries;
  • identify equal sides;
  • identify opposite sides;
  • identify parallel sides;
  • sort shapes using different rules;
  • explain the rule used to classify shapes;
  • recognise triangles and other polygons;
  • recognise shapes in different orientations;
  • explain why rotation does not change a shape’s name;
  • identify shapes and lines in sporting fields;
  • use spatial language to describe courts and fields;
  • create regular shapes using digital tools;
  • change regular shapes into irregular shapes;
  • observe how moving vertices changes shape features;
  • compare shapes before and after manipulation.

Teacher Guide for Parents

Teachers help children move beyond naming familiar shapes by asking them to describe and justify the features that make each shape belong to a group.

At home, follow this sequence: look, count, compare, classify, rotate and explain.

1. Begin with Observable Features

Show a collection of different shapes and ask children what they notice.

Encourage observations about:

  • number of sides;
  • number of vertices;
  • straight and curved boundaries;
  • equal and unequal sides;
  • opposite sides;
  • parallel sides.

Avoid beginning with colour or size, because these features do not usually determine the shape’s classification.

2. Count Sides Carefully

A side is part of the boundary of a shape.

For polygons, the sides are straight line segments.

Ask children to trace around the boundary and count each side once.

Mark the starting side to avoid counting it twice.

3. Count Vertices

A vertex is a point where two straight sides meet.

The plural of vertex is vertices.

For common polygons, the number of sides and vertices is the same.

  • A triangle has 3 sides and 3 vertices.
  • A quadrilateral has 4 sides and 4 vertices.
  • A pentagon has 5 sides and 5 vertices.
  • A hexagon has 6 sides and 6 vertices.

4. Identify Straight and Curved Boundaries

Ask children to trace each part of a shape’s boundary.

Decide whether the boundary is:

  • entirely straight;
  • entirely curved;
  • a combination of straight and curved sections.

Polygons are closed two-dimensional shapes made from straight sides.

5. Recognise Triangles

A triangle is any closed two-dimensional shape with three straight sides.

Triangles may:

  • have equal or unequal sides;
  • be wide or narrow;
  • point upwards, downwards or sideways;
  • appear in different orientations.

The defining feature is that every triangle has three straight sides.

6. Recognise Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is a closed two-dimensional shape with four straight sides.

Squares, rectangles, parallelograms and many irregular four-sided shapes are all quadrilaterals.

Children can sort quadrilaterals further by examining:

  • equal side lengths;
  • opposite sides;
  • parallel sides;
  • regular and irregular features.

7. Identify Equal Sides

Equal sides have the same length.

Children can compare side lengths by:

  • using a ruler;
  • using string;
  • placing matching sides together;
  • using equal-length marks on a diagram.

Do not rely only on appearance, especially when shapes are rotated or drawn at different sizes.

8. Understand Opposite Sides

Opposite sides are across from each other and do not share a vertex.

In a four-sided shape, each side has one opposite side.

Use matching colours to mark pairs of opposite sides.

9. Understand Adjacent Sides

Adjacent sides are next to each other and share a vertex.

Comparing opposite and adjacent sides helps children use spatial language accurately.

10. Identify Parallel Sides

Parallel lines remain the same distance apart and do not meet when extended.

Use examples such as:

  • railway tracks;
  • opposite sides of a rectangle;
  • court sidelines;
  • lines on ruled paper.

Children can place rulers along the sides to help judge whether the lines travel in the same direction.

11. Compare Opposite and Parallel Sides

Opposite and parallel do not mean exactly the same thing.

“Opposite” describes where the sides are located in relation to each other.

“Parallel” describes the direction of the lines and the fact that they do not meet.

In a rectangle, each pair of opposite sides is also parallel.

12. Sort Shapes by Number of Sides

Provide a mixed collection of polygons.

Sort them into groups such as:

  • 3 sides;
  • 4 sides;
  • 5 sides;
  • 6 sides;
  • more than 6 sides.

Ask children to explain the sorting rule.

13. Sort Shapes by Equal Sides

Use groups such as:

  • all sides equal;
  • some sides equal;
  • no sides equal.

A shape may belong to more than one classification system depending on the rule being used.

14. Sort Shapes by Parallel Sides

Create groups such as:

  • no parallel sides;
  • one pair of parallel sides;
  • two pairs of parallel sides.

Ask children to mark the parallel sides before sorting.

15. Use More Than One Sorting Rule

Re-sort the same collection several times.

For example, first sort by the number of sides, then sort by equal sides and finally sort by parallel sides.

This helps children understand that classification depends on the chosen feature.

16. Ask Children to Justify Their Classification

Encourage complete explanations.

For example:

“I placed this shape in the four-sided group because it has four straight sides and four vertices.”

17. Rotate Familiar Shapes

Cut out a square, triangle, rectangle and other shapes.

Turn each shape:

  • sideways;
  • upside down;
  • through a quarter turn;
  • through a half turn.

Ask whether the number of sides, vertices or equal sides has changed.

18. Explain Why Orientation Does Not Change a Shape

Rotating a shape changes the direction it faces, but it does not change its defining features.

A square rotated to look like a diamond is still a square if it still has four equal straight sides and the same other defining properties.

Important Year 2 understanding

A shape should not need to sit in a familiar upright position before it can be recognised.

19. Compare Shapes in Different Orientations

Show several copies of the same shape in different orientations.

Ask children to identify the shared features.

For example:

  • same number of sides;
  • same number of vertices;
  • same pattern of equal sides;
  • same pairs of parallel sides.

20. Avoid Prototype-Only Thinking

Children may recognise only a typical triangle pointing upwards or a rectangle resting on its longest side.

Include:

  • narrow triangles;
  • wide triangles;
  • rotated rectangles;
  • irregular quadrilaterals;
  • shapes of different sizes.

21. Investigate Sporting Fields

Look at diagrams of sporting fields and courts.

Identify shapes such as:

  • rectangles;
  • circles;
  • semicircles;
  • squares;
  • other marked regions.

22. Describe Basketball Court Features

On a basketball court diagram, children may identify:

  • straight sidelines;
  • straight end lines;
  • a centre circle;
  • rectangular marked areas;
  • opposite sidelines;
  • parallel boundary lines.

23. Use Spatial Terms with Sporting Fields

Ask children to describe court and field features using complete sentences.

For example:

  • “The two sidelines are opposite and parallel.”
  • “The centre circle has a curved boundary.”
  • “The end lines are straight.”
  • “The goal square has four straight sides.”

24. Compare Different Sporting Fields

Compare diagrams of two or more fields or courts.

Ask:

  • Which features are the same?
  • Which shapes are different?
  • Which lines are parallel?
  • Which boundaries are curved?
  • Where are opposite sides located?

25. Create Regular Shapes

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal.

At Year 2 level, children can observe regular shapes by comparing equal side lengths and the overall repeated pattern.

Use digital drawing or geometry tools to create shapes such as:

  • an equilateral triangle;
  • a square;
  • a regular pentagon;
  • a regular hexagon.

26. Change a Regular Shape into an Irregular Shape

Drag or push one vertex of a regular shape.

Observe what changes.

  • Some side lengths may become unequal.
  • The shape may no longer look balanced.
  • The number of sides may remain the same.
  • The number of vertices may remain the same.

For example, moving one vertex of a regular quadrilateral may create an irregular quadrilateral.

27. Observe Features That Stay the Same

When a vertex is moved, some features may change while others remain unchanged.

Ask children to compare:

  • number of sides before and after;
  • number of vertices before and after;
  • equal sides before and after;
  • parallel sides before and after;
  • regularity before and after.

28. Move and Rotate Digital Shapes

Moving a shape changes its position.

Rotating a shape changes its orientation.

Neither action changes the shape’s defining features.

Reshaping by moving a vertex, however, may change some of its features.

29. Distinguish Moving from Reshaping

Children should understand the difference between:

  • moving a whole shape without changing it;
  • rotating a whole shape without changing it;
  • dragging a vertex and changing the shape itself.

30. Record Before-and-After Observations

Use a simple comparison table.

Recommended Teaching Sequence at Home

  1. Identify straight and curved boundaries.
  2. Count sides and vertices.
  3. Sort shapes by number of sides.
  4. Identify equal and unequal sides.
  5. Identify opposite sides.
  6. Identify parallel sides.
  7. Re-sort shapes using different features.
  8. Rotate shapes into different orientations.
  9. Explain why orientation does not change classification.
  10. Investigate shapes and lines in sporting fields.
  11. Create regular shapes using digital tools.
  12. Move and rotate digital shapes.
  13. Drag vertices to create irregular shapes.
  14. Compare features before and after manipulation.

Common Misconceptions and How Parents Can Help

Classifying shapes by colour or size

Ask children to focus on sides, vertices, straight and curved boundaries, equality and parallel lines.

Thinking a rotated shape has a different name

Rotate the shape and recount its sides and vertices to show that its defining features remain unchanged.

Calling a rotated square a diamond

Explain that “diamond” describes its appearance, while the mathematical classification depends on its features.

Recognising only one type of triangle

Show triangles of different sizes, widths and orientations. Emphasise that all have three straight sides.

Counting a vertex twice

Mark the starting vertex and move around the boundary in one direction.

Confusing sides and vertices

Trace each side with a finger and tap each vertex where two sides meet.

Thinking opposite sides are beside each other

Use different colours to mark sides across from each other and compare them with adjacent sides.

Thinking all opposite sides are automatically parallel

Explain that opposite describes location, while parallel describes the direction of the lines.

Thinking parallel sides must be horizontal

Rotate examples to show that parallel sides can slant or appear vertical.

Thinking all four-sided shapes are rectangles

Introduce quadrilateral as the larger group and compare the features of different four-sided shapes.

Thinking moving a shape changes its features

Compare the side and vertex counts before and after moving or rotating it.

Thinking dragging a vertex only moves the shape

Compare moving the entire shape with moving one vertex. Moving a vertex can change side lengths and parallel sides.

Simple Home Shape Classification Activities

Activity 1: Shape Feature Hunt

Find shapes around the home and describe their sides, vertices, straight lines and curved boundaries.

Focus: Recognising shape features.

Activity 2: Sort by Number of Sides

Cut out or draw a collection of polygons and sort them into groups according to the number of sides.

Focus: AC9M2SP01_E1.

Activity 3: Equal-Side Sort

Sort shapes into groups with all sides equal, some sides equal or no sides equal.

Use a ruler or string to check.

Focus: Comparing side lengths.

Activity 4: Parallel-Side Search

Look for pairs of parallel lines in books, furniture, windows, floorboards and shape diagrams.

Focus: Parallel and opposite sides.

Activity 5: Rotate and Recognise

Cut out several shapes and rotate each one into different orientations.

Ask your child to name the shape and explain which features stayed the same.

Focus: AC9M2SP01_E2.

Activity 6: Shape Mystery Bag

Place cardboard shapes in a bag. Ask your child to feel one shape, count its sides and vertices and describe it before looking.

Focus: Feature-based classification.

Activity 7: Sporting Court Investigation

Examine a diagram of a basketball court or another sporting field and label straight, curved, opposite and parallel features.

Focus: AC9M2SP01_E3.

Activity 8: Build and Classify Shapes

Use craft sticks, straws or matchsticks to build polygons.

Classify each shape by its number of sides, equal sides and parallel sides.

Focus: Constructing and classifying shapes.

Activity 9: Digital Shape Change

Create a regular shape using a digital drawing or geometry tool and drag one vertex.

Describe which features changed and which stayed the same.

Focus: AC9M2SP01_E4.

Activity 10: Shape Sorting Rule Challenge

Sort the same collection of shapes in three different ways and explain each sorting rule.

Focus: Flexible classification.

Questions Parents Can Ask

  • How many sides does this shape have?
  • How many vertices does it have?
  • Are the boundaries straight, curved or both?
  • Which sides are equal?
  • Which sides are opposite?
  • Which sides are adjacent?
  • Are any sides parallel?
  • How do you know the lines are parallel?
  • What rule did you use to sort the shapes?
  • Could the shape belong to another group?
  • What features do all the shapes in this group share?
  • Does rotating the shape change its name?
  • Which features stayed the same after rotation?
  • What shapes can you see in the sporting field?
  • Which lines are opposite and parallel?
  • What happened when you moved the whole shape?
  • What happened when you dragged one vertex?
  • Is the shape regular or irregular?
  • Which features changed?
  • How can you check your classification?

Supporting Different Learners at Home

Children Who Need More Support

  • begin with triangles and four-sided shapes;
  • use large cut-out shapes;
  • trace each side while counting;
  • mark the starting vertex;
  • colour-code equal sides;
  • colour-code opposite sides;
  • use rulers to compare parallel directions;
  • rotate one shape at a time;
  • use physical shapes before digital tools;
  • allow oral explanations before written responses.

Children Ready for Extension

  • create sorting rules using two features at once;
  • classify unfamiliar and irregular polygons;
  • use Venn diagrams to sort shapes;
  • identify shapes belonging to more than one category;
  • compare several types of quadrilaterals;
  • design a sporting field using specified shapes;
  • create regular and irregular versions of a polygon;
  • predict how dragging a vertex will change features;
  • write clues for a mystery shape;
  • design a shape-classification challenge for another learner.

Quick Parent Check

Use a mixed collection of shape cards to check whether your child understands the main ideas.

  1. Sort shapes by the number of sides.
  2. Identify a shape with all sides equal.
  3. Identify a pair of opposite sides.
  4. Identify a pair of parallel sides.
  5. Describe one shape using at least three features.
  6. Recognise a rotated triangle or quadrilateral.
  7. Explain why a rotated shape keeps the same name.
  8. Identify shapes and lines in a sporting court.
  9. Compare a regular and irregular shape.
  10. Explain what changed after a vertex was moved.

A child shows secure understanding when they can classify shapes using their features, recognise them in different orientations and explain their reasoning with accurate spatial vocabulary.

Home Learning Summary

Shape understanding develops when children examine features, sort the same shapes in different ways and explain why each shape belongs to a category.

Use this simple routine:

  1. trace the boundary;
  2. count sides and vertices;
  3. identify straight and curved sections;
  4. compare side lengths;
  5. identify opposite and parallel sides;
  6. classify the shape;
  7. rotate it and check the features again;
  8. explain the classification using spatial vocabulary.
