Learn the idea

Small steps, clear examples

Learning goal: Partition, rearrange, regroup and rename two- and three-digit numbers while keeping their value, and explain zero in place-value notation. An adult can read the directions and accept a spoken, pointed, built or drawn explanation.

1. Partition and rename numbers

Partition means split a number into parts. Rename means describe the same amount in another equal way. The usual hundreds, tens and ones grouping is called standard grouping.

Start with two digits: build 62 as 6 tens and 2 ones. Open one ten into 10 ones to show 5 tens and 12 ones. Both groups still make 62. Keep the unit names attached as you describe the parts.

Worked example: partition 384

  1. Keep the value of each whole part: 300, 80 and 4.
  2. Write 384 = 300 + 80 + 4.
  3. Rearrange the parts: 384 = 4 + 300 + 80. The total stays the same.

Another name: three hundreds contain thirty tens. Together with eight more tens, 384 is 38 tens and 4 ones. It is also 3 hundreds and 84 ones.

Watch for: rearranging whole parts is different from swapping digits. 300 + 80 + 4 and 4 + 300 + 80 are equal; 384 and 834 are different numbers.

Try it: Rename 246 in two different ways. Include the unit names.

Check the answer

For example, 2 hundreds + 4 tens + 6 ones; 24 tens + 6 ones; or 2 hundreds + 46 ones. Accept other groupings that keep the total at 246.

2. Make equal exchanges in both directions

Exchange means replace a group with another group worth the same amount: 10 ones ↔ 1 ten, and 10 tens ↔ 1 hundred. Build both sides to check.

Worked example: rename 243

  1. Start with 2 hundreds, 4 tens and 3 ones.
  2. Open one hundred into 10 tens. One hundred remains.
  3. Combine those 10 tens with the original 4 tens: 243 = 1 hundred + 14 tens + 3 ones.
  4. Collect 10 of the 14 tens into a hundred to return to 2 hundreds, 4 tens and 3 ones.

Open a ten: 62 is 6 tens and 2 ones. Exchange one ten for 10 ones: 62 = 5 tens + 12 ones.

One trade at a time: 1 hundred + 9 tens + 14 ones becomes 1 hundred + 10 tens + 4 ones, then 2 hundreds + 0 tens + 4 ones: 204.

Watch for: adding ten tens without removing one hundred changes the total.

Try it: Someone says 243 = 2 hundreds + 14 tens + 3 ones. Is that equal? Repair the grouping.

Check the answer

No. That grouping is 343, which is 100 too much. Use 1 hundred + 14 tens + 3 ones, or return to 2 hundreds + 4 tens + 3 ones. The trade must remove one hundred when it adds ten tens.

3. Explain the role of zero

A zero digit records an empty place in the usual grouping. It does not mean the whole number has no amount.

Worked example: show 407

  1. Place 4 in the hundreds column, 0 in tens, and 7 in ones.
  2. The zero keeps 7 in the ones place: 407 = 400 + 0 + 7.
  3. Removing the zero makes 47, a different amount.

The tens digit and all complete tens are different: 407 has a tens digit of 0, but its four hundreds can be exchanged for 40 complete tens, with 7 ones left.

Zero at the end: 560 is 5 hundreds, 6 tens and 0 loose ones, or 56 tens. Two zeros: 700 is 7 hundreds, 0 loose tens and 0 loose ones, or 70 tens.

Watch for: say “no loose tens in the standard grouping”, rather than “there are no tens anywhere in 407”.

Try it: Show 508 using hundreds, tens and ones. Then rename it using tens and ones only. What does its zero tell you?

Check the answer

5 hundreds, 0 loose tens and 8 ones; or 50 tens and 8 ones. The zero is the tens digit in the usual grouping. Fifty complete tens are still contained in the five hundreds.

Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Reading a digit as its value: in 352, the 5 means 5 tens, or 50.
  • Changing the amount during a trade: build the old and new groups side by side and check both totals.
  • Thinking only one partition is correct: ask for another equal name, then check each unit.
  • Writing 24 tens as 24: 24 tens is 240. Keep the unit name attached.
Ready to try it independently?

Ask the child to build a two- or three-digit number, show a second equal grouping, reverse an exchange, and explain a zero digit. Accept a spoken, drawn or built explanation that names the units and preserves the total.

Keep materials visible when support is needed. For a challenge, find three different equal groupings within two- and three-digit numbers. Formal addition and subtraction algorithms are not part of this guide.
