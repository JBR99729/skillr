Collect, sort and record data

Learning intention: Plan a question, collect categorical data and check labelled lists and tables.

Practice on app ↗Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Worked example

Group the records without losing or counting any twice.

Learn the skill

1. Plan a question and choose categories

A categorical question asks about kinds or groups, such as one preferred playground game. State one response per person and provide suitable non-overlapping choices, with another choice when needed. Decide whether asking, observing or conducting a simple experiment answers the question.

Try it · Is favourite colour a category question?

Yes. Each answer can be sorted into a named colour group.

2. Collect by survey, observation and experiment

For a survey, record each person’s answer once. For an observation, define what and when you will watch, then record what actually happens. For an experiment, record one outcome for each trial, such as which side of a two-sided counter faces up. Predictions are not observed results; no probability calculation is needed.

Try it · You want to know which drinks classmates prefer. Would a survey or a weather observation answer that?

A survey. Ask classmates to choose a drink.

3. Organise lists, tallies and tables; check digital records

Example survey responses: tag, swings, tag, ball, swings, tag. Group the records without losing or counting any twice.

Try it · Your tally counts are 3 and 4. How many responses should the original list contain?

Seven responses.

Common mistakes

Recording a response twice. Make one tally per response and check the total against the number collected.

10-MINUTE HOME ACTIVITY

Collect a small set of responses

Ask five people which of three snacks they prefer. Record one response per person with a tally. Transfer the counts to a table and check the total.

Adjust the challenge: Start with two choices. Extend by comparing the tally and a digital table.

FAQ

What is the total if the categories have counts 2, 1 and 2?

Five responses.

Learn the skill

Small steps, clear examples

Open one focus at a time. Explain the model, try the question and check the reasoning.

1. Plan a question and choose categories

A categorical question asks about kinds or groups, such as one preferred playground game. State one response per person and provide suitable non-overlapping choices, with another choice when needed. Decide whether asking, observing or conducting a simple experiment answers the question.

Try it: Can counting arriving bikes tell us everyone’s favourite way to travel?

Check the answer

No. Observation records actual travel; preference needs a survey question.

2. Collect by survey, observation and experiment

For a survey, record each person’s answer once. For an observation, define what and when you will watch, then record what actually happens. For an experiment, record one outcome for each trial, such as which side of a two-sided counter faces up. Predictions are not observed results; no probability calculation is needed.

Try it: Six counter trials give A, B, A, A, B, A. What belongs in the record?

Check the answer

Six results: A appears 4 times and B appears 2 times. Record actual outcomes, including repeated outcomes.

3. Organise lists, tallies and tables; check digital records

Example survey responses: tag, swings, tag, ball, swings, tag. Group the records without losing or counting any twice.

GameCount
Tag3
Swings2
Ball1
Climbing0

The counts total 6, matching the six responses. Zero means no response in that category, rather than a missing record. With adult support, enter the same categories in a simple form or spreadsheet. Check duplicate submissions, blanks, headings and totals against the originals; software does not guarantee correct data. Revise unclear choices before repeating the survey.

Try it: Do three category names mean that only three people responded?

Check the answer

No. Count responses, not category headings. This example has six responses.

Tally check: Group the first five tally strokes as four upright strokes crossed by the fifth. A group of five plus one extra tally represents 6 responses. Recount against the raw list before transferring totals into a table.

Support: Use real objects, fewer choices and an adult read-aloud. Core: Explain and record the model independently. Extend: Create a new example or repair an error while staying within this skill.

Teaching tips and common mistakes
  • Unclear question — Use a question everyone can answer the same way.
  • Double counting — Record each person or item once.
  • Overlapping categories — Make categories clear so each response has one place.

Ask the learner to explain what each mark, unit or instruction means. Rebuild the model when an answer is correct by guessing. A written answer alone does not establish practical or digital performance.

Curriculum code and learning goal

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: acquire data for categorical variables through surveys, observation, experiment and using digital tools; sort data into relevant categories and display data using lists and tables
  • E1: posing a question of interest about favourite things; for example, asking classmates what are their favourite types of fruit, football teams, days of the week, and recording responses using a table; using counting strategies to determine the number of different responses and the most popular and least popular responses
  • E2: investigating questions, such as “How much rubbish is really rubbish?” by gathering data about objects in categories; for example, throw away, recycle and reuse; deciding whether the data answers the question
  • E3: using familiar software or generative artificial intelligence tools, to construct a survey to collect class data, sorting and interpreting responses, and considering the questions asked and whether they need to be modified to reuse the survey
  • E4: observing events and using the observations to design a table or list to record data; for example, observing students arriving at school prior to deciding the appropriate data categories for investigating the different ways students get to school
  • E5: exploring the ways First Nations Australians observe, collect, sort and record data (teaching context)

Use a reliable, community-approved source and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does.
