About This Year 2 Maths Topic

This topic builds on children’s understanding that the position of a digit determines its value. Children learn that a number can be broken apart, regrouped and renamed in several correct ways without changing the total value.

For example, the number 245 can be represented in its usual place-value form as:

  • 2 hundreds, 4 tens and 5 ones;
  • 200 + 40 + 5.

It can also be regrouped and renamed in non-standard ways:

  • 24 tens and 5 ones;
  • 2 hundreds and 45 ones;
  • 1 hundred, 14 tens and 5 ones;
  • 245 ones.

Each representation looks different, but every representation still has a total value of 245.

Children also investigate the role of zero in numbers such as 304. The zero shows that there are no tens and keeps the hundreds and ones digits in their correct positions.

These activities support Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 content descriptor AC9M2N02 and its associated elaborations.

The Big Idea for Parents

The most important message to give your child is:

Change the grouping, but keep the total the same.

Regrouping does not make a number larger or smaller. It only changes how the same quantity is organised and described.

Think about money. One ten-dollar note can be exchanged for ten one-dollar coins. The appearance and grouping change, but the value remains ten dollars.

Place-value materials work in the same way:

  • 1 hundred can be exchanged for 10 tens;
  • 1 ten can be exchanged for 10 ones;
  • 10 tens can be regrouped as 1 hundred;
  • 10 ones can be regrouped as 1 ten.

Learning Intentions

Children will learn to:

  • represent two- and three-digit numbers using materials;
  • identify hundreds, tens and ones in a number;
  • explain the value of each digit;
  • partition a number into place-value parts;
  • rearrange number parts without changing the total;
  • regroup 1 hundred as 10 tens;
  • regroup 1 ten as 10 ones;
  • rename numbers using standard groupings;
  • rename numbers using non-standard groupings;
  • check that a renamed number has the same value;
  • recognise zero as a place-value placeholder;
  • explain why removing a zero can change a number.

Teacher Guide for Parents

Teachers usually introduce this concept with materials before asking children to complete written regrouping questions.

At home, follow this order: build it, describe it, exchange it, rename it and check it.

1. Begin with Base-10 Materials

Use base-10 blocks if available:

  • a small cube represents 1 one;
  • a rod represents 1 ten;
  • a flat represents 1 hundred.

You can also use bundled sticks:

  • single sticks represent ones;
  • a bundle of 10 sticks represents 1 ten;
  • 10 bundles represent 1 hundred.

Build a number such as 326 using 3 hundreds, 2 tens and 6 ones.

What to ask

“What does the 3 represent? What does the 2 represent? What does the 6 represent?”

2. Connect Each Digit to the Materials

Write the number beside the materials and point to one digit at a time.

For 326:

  • 3 represents 3 hundreds, which is 300;
  • 2 represents 2 tens, which is 20;
  • 6 represents 6 ones, which is 6.

Show the expanded form:

326 = 300 + 20 + 6

Teacher tip

Ask the child to touch the matching material while explaining each digit. This connects the written symbol to the actual quantity.

3. Explain Partitioning

Partitioning means breaking a number into parts.

The standard partition of 472 is:

  • 4 hundreds;
  • 7 tens;
  • 2 ones;
  • 400 + 70 + 2.

Ask your child to build the number, draw it and write its expanded form.

What to say

“We are breaking the number into place-value parts, but all the parts still combine to make the original number.”

4. Explain Regrouping with an Exchange

Build 245 using:

  • 2 hundreds;
  • 4 tens;
  • 5 ones.

Exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens. The materials now show:

  • 1 hundred;
  • 14 tens;
  • 5 ones.

The grouping changed, but the total remains 245.

Show the calculation:

200 + 40 + 5 = 100 + 140 + 5

Check the value

Ask: “Did we add anything? Did we remove anything? Is the total still 245?”

5. Rename a Number Using Tens and Ones

Begin with the standard name:

245 = 2 hundreds, 4 tens and 5 ones

Exchange both hundreds for 20 tens. Add the existing 4 tens:

20 tens + 4 tens = 24 tens

Therefore:

245 = 24 tens and 5 ones

Important explanation

Twenty-four tens means 24 groups of 10. It does not mean the number 24.

6. Rename a Number Using Hundreds and Ones

Build 245 again. Keep the 2 hundreds and exchange the 4 tens for 40 ones.

Add the original 5 ones:

40 ones + 5 ones = 45 ones

Therefore:

245 = 2 hundreds and 45 ones

Encourage your child to check:

200 + 45 = 245

7. Compare Standard and Non-Standard Groupings

A standard grouping uses the usual hundreds, tens and ones arrangement.

For example:

372 = 3 hundreds, 7 tens and 2 ones

Non-standard groupings rename some or all of the place-value groups:

  • 2 hundreds, 17 tens and 2 ones;
  • 3 hundreds and 72 ones;
  • 37 tens and 2 ones;
  • 372 ones.

Teacher tip

Ask your child to prove each representation by calculating the value of every group.

8. Explain the Role of Zero

Write the number 304 in a place-value chart:

  • 3 hundreds;
  • 0 tens;
  • 4 ones.

Explain that the zero keeps the tens place empty. Without the zero, 304 would become 34, which has a completely different value.

Compare:

  • 304 = 3 hundreds, 0 tens and 4 ones;
  • 34 = 0 hundreds, 3 tens and 4 ones.

What to say

“Zero shows that there are no groups in this place, but the place still needs to be held.”

9. Check That the Total Is Preserved

After every regrouping, ask your child to check the value.

For example:

  • 3 hundreds, 6 tens and 4 ones = 364;
  • 2 hundreds, 16 tens and 4 ones = 364;
  • 36 tens and 4 ones = 364;
  • 3 hundreds and 64 ones = 364.

Children should not simply memorise the renamed form. They should be able to explain why the total has stayed the same.

Recommended Teaching Sequence at Home

  1. Build a number using hundreds, tens and ones.
  2. Read and explain the value of every digit.
  3. Write the standard partition.
  4. Exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens.
  5. Describe the new grouping.
  6. Exchange 1 ten for 10 ones.
  7. Rename the number in another way.
  8. Write an equation to check the total.
  9. Practise examples containing zero.
  10. Explain why every representation has the same value.

Common Misconceptions and How Parents Can Help

Treating digits as separate numbers

A child may say that the 4 in 245 is worth 4. Use materials to show that the 4 is in the tens place and represents 4 tens, or 40.

Thinking 24 tens means 24

Build 24 groups of 10 or calculate 24 × 10. Explain that 24 tens have a value of 240.

Thinking regrouping changes the number

Physically exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens without adding or removing materials. Count the total before and after.

Believing a number has only one correct name

Ask the child to build the same number in at least three ways and prove that each representation has the same value.

Regrouping but losing a group

When exchanging 1 hundred for 10 tens, cross out or remove the hundred before adding the 10 tens. Then recount the complete value.

Dropping zero from a number

Compare 406 and 46 using a place-value chart. Show that 406 has 4 hundreds, while 46 has no hundreds.

Thinking zero has no purpose

Explain that zero shows an empty place. It keeps every other digit in the correct place-value column.

Writing a renamed number without checking

Ask the child to calculate the value of each group and add the parts to confirm the total.

Simple Home Activities for Place Value and Regrouping

Activity 1: Build the Number

Say a two- or three-digit number. Ask your child to build it using base-10 blocks, bundles of sticks or drawings.

Ask them to explain the value of each digit.

Focus: AC9M2N02_E1.

Activity 2: Place-Value Match

Write a numeral on one card and a place-value description on another card.

For example, match 428 with 4 hundreds, 2 tens and 8 ones.

Focus: Connecting digits and materials.

Activity 3: Exchange a Hundred

Build a number using hundreds, tens and ones. Ask your child to exchange 1 hundred for 10 tens.

Record the number before and after the exchange.

Focus: Regrouping without changing value.

Activity 4: Exchange a Ten

Build a number such as 146. Exchange 1 ten for 10 ones.

Rename the number as 1 hundred, 3 tens and 16 ones.

Focus: Flexible place-value groupings.

Activity 5: How Many Tens?

Write a number such as 340 and ask: “How many tens are in this number?”

Ask your child to prove that 340 is 34 tens.

Focus: Renaming hundreds as tens.

Activity 6: Rename 245

Build 245 and ask your child to rename it in as many correct ways as possible.

Record every representation and check its value.

Focus: AC9M2N02_E2.

Activity 7: Zero Placeholder Detective

Compare numbers such as 305, 350, 503 and 530.

Ask your child to identify the empty place in each number and explain what the zero is doing.

Focus: Role of zero.

Activity 8: True or False?

Read statements such as:

  • 326 is 32 tens and 6 ones;
  • 245 is 2 hundreds and 45 ones;
  • 304 is the same as 34;
  • 18 tens have a value of 180.

Ask your child to prove whether each statement is true or false.

Focus: Reasoning and error analysis.

Activity 9: Money Exchange

Use pretend money to exchange one ten-dollar note for ten one-dollar coins.

Connect the exchange to changing 1 ten into 10 ones.

Focus: Understanding equivalent value.

Activity 10: Make My Number

Give a clue such as: “My number is 2 hundreds and 36 ones.”

Ask your child to identify the number, build it and rename it using hundreds, tens and ones.

Focus: Moving between non-standard and standard forms.

Questions Parents Can Ask

  • What does each digit represent?
  • How many hundreds are in the number?
  • How many tens are in the number?
  • How many ones are in the number?
  • Can you build the number using materials?
  • Can you write the number in expanded form?
  • Can you partition the number another way?
  • What can you exchange?
  • What is 1 hundred worth in tens?
  • What is 1 ten worth in ones?
  • Can you rename the number using only tens and ones?
  • Can you rename the number using hundreds and ones?
  • Has the total changed?
  • How can you prove the value stayed the same?
  • What does the zero show?
  • What would happen if the zero were removed?
  • Is this a standard or non-standard grouping?
  • Can you find another correct representation?

Supporting Different Learners at Home

Children Who Need More Support

  • begin with two-digit numbers;
  • use physical materials for every example;
  • use a labelled hundreds, tens and ones chart;
  • practise exchanging 1 ten for 10 ones first;
  • work with one regrouping step at a time;
  • use numbers without zero before introducing zero;
  • record the value of every group;
  • use drawings to match the materials;
  • ask the child to explain orally before writing;
  • check the total after every exchange.

Children Ready for Extension

  • find every possible regrouping of a number;
  • rename numbers using only tens or only ones;
  • work with numbers beyond 1000;
  • solve missing-group questions;
  • create true and false place-value statements;
  • correct an incorrect regrouping;
  • compare two non-standard representations;
  • represent numbers containing more than one zero;
  • explain regrouping without using materials;
  • create place-value riddles for another person.

Quick Parent Check

Use these questions to check whether your child understands the main ideas.

  1. Build 374 using hundreds, tens and ones.
  2. Explain what the 7 represents in 374.
  3. Partition 526 into standard place-value parts.
  4. Rename 245 as tens and ones.
  5. Rename 245 as hundreds and ones.
  6. Show 326 as 2 hundreds, some tens and 6 ones.
  7. Write the number represented by 27 tens and 4 ones.
  8. Explain why 24 tens and 5 ones equal 245.
  9. Explain the role of zero in 408.
  10. Correct the statement: “304 is the same as 34.”

A child shows secure understanding when they can find the answer, model it and explain why the total remains unchanged.

Home Learning Summary

Short practical activities are more effective than asking a child to complete a long page of abstract regrouping questions without support.

Build one number, exchange one group and ask the child to explain what changed and what stayed the same.

Repeat the same routine with different numbers:

  1. build the number;
  2. identify every digit;
  3. exchange one place-value group;
  4. rename the new grouping;
  5. check that the total is unchanged.
