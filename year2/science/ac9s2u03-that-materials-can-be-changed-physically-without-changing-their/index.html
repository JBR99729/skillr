YEAR 2 SCIENCE · AC9S2U03

Change the shape, keep the material

Learning intention: Explore bending, twisting, stretching and breaking materials. Explain what changes and what can stay the same.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A torn sheet is still paper

A whole paper sheet is torn into smaller pieces. Its shape and the number of pieces change, but each piece is still paper. Some physical changes are easy to undo; others are not. Difficulty putting it back together does not by itself show that a new material formed.

Learn the skill

1. Name the material first

Identify what the object is made from before describing the action. “Paper” names a material; “sheet” names one possible form.

ObjectMaterial
Paper stripPaper
Dough ballModelling dough
Try it · If a paper strip is bent, has it automatically become a new material?

No. It can remain paper even though its shape changes.

2. Describe the action and effect

Bending makes a curve or fold; twisting turns parts around; stretching makes something longer; breaking divides it into pieces. Materials respond differently.

ActionPossible observation
Stretch suitable dough gentlyIt becomes longer
Break a dry paper stripIt separates into smaller pieces
Try it · Must every material stretch like dough?

No. A material may bend, break or barely change under the same gentle action.

3. Check what stayed the same

Ask whether the material is still the same, rather than deciding only by appearance. Unfolding may be easy; joining torn paper perfectly is difficult.

Compare two changes

Fold paper: still paper. Tear paper: still paper, even when the tear is hard to undo.

Try it · Is every physical change easy to reverse?

No. Tearing paper is a physical change that is difficult to undo.

Common mistakes

“New shape means new material.” Describe shape and material separately.

“More pieces means more material was created.” If all pieces are kept, splitting a sheet does not create extra paper.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make a change-and-material record

2 minutes: an adult chooses safe paper and suitable modelling dough. 5 minutes: bend, twist or gently stretch a sample, then tear a paper strip. 3 minutes: name the action, visible change and material afterwards.

Adjust the challenge Support: use action picture cards. Extend: compare two materials under one gentle action and explain why the results may differ.

Use adult-approved materials. Avoid sharp fragments, glass, heat and unknown substances. Do not eat modelling dough. An adult handles any ice or water demonstration.

Three tricky questions

1. A sheet becomes four pieces. Have four new materials been made?

No. There are more pieces, but they can all remain paper.

2. A strip keeps a crease after unfolding. Does that prove a new substance formed?

No. A lasting change in shape can still be a physical change.

3. Must a material return to its old shape when the force stops?

No. Some keep their changed shape.

FAQ

What does physical change mean here?

A change such as shape or size while the material remains the same.

What about the ice in the illustration?

Melting changes solid ice into liquid water. Both are water; changing state is another physical change.

What students learn in AC9S2U03

A material can change shape or size without becoming a different material. Bending, twisting, stretching and breaking into smaller pieces are physical changes.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: recognise that materials can be changed physically without changing their material composition and explore the effect of different actions on materials including bending, twisting, stretching and breaking into smaller pieces
  • E1: exploring how materials can be physically changed to suit a particular purpose, such as twisting strands of cotton or wool together to make the thread stronger, or folding paper to make it fly
  • E2: manipulating materials such as paper or fabric, and determining ways they can be physically changed by scrunching, twisting or bending, or broken into smaller pieces by cutting, tearing or crushing
  • E3: crushing a stick of chalk into a powder, comparing the properties of the stick and the powder, and discussing whether it is still the same material
  • E4: exploring how First Nations Australians make physical changes to natural materials to produce objects such as bowls, baskets and various fibre crafts (teaching context)
  • E5: creating an ‘odd one out’ game by providing samples of the same material that has been physically changed in different ways, and one sample of a different material, and challenging other students to identify the odd one out

Quick questions

If an answer is uncertain, return to the model and explain what each part represents before calculating or drawing a conclusion.

Common misconceptions
  • Shape change means new material — A bent paper strip is still paper.
  • Action and effect mixed — Name the action first, then describe the result.
  • Unsafe breaking or stretching — Only use teacher-approved materials and procedures.
