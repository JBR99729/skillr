YEAR 2 SCIENCE · AC9S2I05

Compare honestly. Improve the test.

Learning intention: Compare observations with predictions and others’ records. With guidance, consider fairness and ask a useful further question.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The prediction partly matched

Ava predicted that a folded paper strip would hold exactly 4 counters before bending down. It held 3, while her flat strip held 1. “Folded holds more” matched her expectation, but the exact prediction of 4 did not. Another group recorded 5 on a folded strip; they compare setups before explaining the difference.

Ava’s exact prediction: folded strip4
Ava’s observed folded strip3

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Compare the actual claim

Read the whole prediction. A result can support one part of an idea without matching an exact-number prediction.

Two comparisons

Folded versus flat: 3 is more than 1. Exact prediction versus result: 4 is not 3.

Try it · Should Ava rewrite her original prediction as 3?

No. Keep both the original prediction and the measured result.

2. Compare methods before blaming

Different observations may come from different materials, gaps, counter sizes or methods, as well as real variation.

Our groupOther groupWhat to check
3 counters5 countersSame paper, gap and counter size?
Try it · Does a result of 5 prove the other group measured badly?

No. Check their setup and records before drawing that conclusion.

3. Ask a connected next question

To compare folding fairly, keep paper size and type, gap, counter type and placement similar. Change the intended feature and repeat with guidance.

Next question

Will a folded strip still hold more than a flat strip if we repeat the comparison using the same paper and gap?

Try it · Is “Which music do you like?” a useful follow-up to these paper results?

It is not connected to this evidence. Ask about the paper setup or result.

Common mistakes

“A prediction must match or the experiment is useless.” A different result can help improve the next question.

“Fair test only means taking turns.” Taking turns is fair behaviour. A fair comparison also needs comparable test conditions.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Keep both records and compare

2 minutes: read Ava’s table. 5 minutes: explain what matched and what differed. 3 minutes: suggest one setup check and one connected further question.

Adjust the challenge Support: use prediction/result cards. Extend: explain why the exact-number prediction and the broader folded-versus-flat idea need separate judgements.

Use supplied records. Any real test needs adult-approved supports, suitable counters and a low workspace.

Three tricky questions

1. Both groups used paper, but their gaps were different. Could that matter?

Yes. The gap may affect how many counters a strip holds.

2. Should a surprising result be erased?

No. Keep the observation and investigate possible reasons.

3. Does one repetition proving different settle the explanation?

No. Compare methods and gather more evidence with guidance.

FAQ

What does compare mean?

Describe similarities and differences using the records.

What is a useful further question?

One connected to what happened and suitable for another observation or safe investigation.

What students learn in AC9S2I05

After an investigation, students compare predictions with observations, compare records with others, decide whether the procedure was fair and identify what could be explored next.

Learning sequence: Compare → Check fairness → Explain difference → Ask next question

  • Compare observations
  • Evaluate fairness
  • Explain differences
  • Pose further questions

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: compare observations with predictions and others’ observations, consider if investigations are fair and identify further questions with guidance
  • E1: comparing their observations of changing shadow length across the day with their predictions and the patterns observed by others
  • E2: comparing observations of sounds with those of others and considering if we all sense sound in the same way
  • E3: proposing ways to ensure that the same sound is produced in an investigation in order to keep the investigation fair
  • E4: comparing findings from investigations about physically changing a material, such as cutting and folding, and exploring questions that investigate similar changes to different materials

Key idea: After an investigation, students compare predictions with observations, compare records with others, decide whether the procedure was fair and identify what could be explored next.

Quick questions

  • What if a prediction is not supported?
  • Name one fair-test feature.
  • Why compare with others?
  • Give a useful next question.

If an answer is uncertain, return to the model and explain what each part represents before calculating or drawing a conclusion.

Common misconceptions
  • Changing the result to match prediction — Record what actually happened.
  • Calling every difference an error — Differences can come from method, measurement or real variation.
  • Fairness checked too late — Plan fairness before testing and review it afterward.
