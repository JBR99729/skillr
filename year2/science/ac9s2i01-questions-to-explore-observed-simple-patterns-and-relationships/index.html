YEAR 2 SCIENCE · AC9S2I01

Notice a pattern, ask a question

Learning intention: Turn observed patterns into questions that can be explored. Make a prediction with a reason based on experience.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Which paper strip will hold more counters?

A class compared equal paper strips bridging the same gap. In two earlier trials, the folded strip held more identical counters before bending down than the flat strip. The class asks whether that pattern will happen in another similar trial, and predicts the folded strip may hold more.

Earlier trialFlat stripFolded strip
11 counter before bending down3 counters before bending down
21 counter before bending down4 counters before bending down

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Say what was observed

An observation reports what happened. Read the records before deciding what to investigate.

What the records show

The folded strip held more counters in both recorded trials.

Try it · Did the folded strip hold exactly the same number in both trials?

No. It held 3 in the first and 4 in the second.

2. Ask about something observable

A useful question identifies what to compare and what to observe. An opinion question may need to be changed for this investigation.

QuestionWhat it asks
Which strip holds more identical counters before bending down?An observable comparison
Which strip is nicest?A preference
Try it · Could “Which colour do I like?” answer the paper-strength question?

No. It asks about preference rather than the number held.

3. Predict with a reason

Make the prediction before the new trial. Use the pattern, and allow the result to differ.

A reasoned prediction

I predict the folded strip may hold more counters because it held more in both earlier trials.

Try it · Can we predict the next folded strip will hold exactly 4 counters with certainty?

No. Earlier counts differed, and a new trial may differ too.

Common mistakes

“A question and prediction are the same.” The question asks what to find out; the prediction says what may happen.

“My prediction is my result.” Keep the before-trial prediction separate from the observed result.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a question from evidence

2 minutes: read the paper-strip table. 5 minutes: write or dictate one observation, one question and one prediction with a reason. 3 minutes: point to the record that supports your reason.

Adjust the challenge Support: choose between two questions. Extend: explain what the variation between 3 and 4 tells you about an exact-number prediction.

The activity uses supplied records. If testing later, an adult must check supports and age-appropriate counters in a low, clear workspace.

Three tricky questions

1. Is “It will hold 10 because I want it to” supported by these records?

No. A wish is not relevant evidence.

2. Can a question be worthwhile before we know the answer?

Yes. It guides the next observation or investigation.

3. If the next result differs, should we pretend the earlier pattern never happened?

No. Keep all records and consider what changed.

FAQ

Must a question be answered by an experiment?

No. Some questions are explored through careful repeated observations.

Does a prediction have to be correct?

No. It must be made honestly before checking and supported by a relevant reason.

What students learn in AC9S2I01

A useful scientific question can be explored by observing or testing. A prediction states what may happen and gives a reason based on experience or a pattern.

Learning sequence: Notice → Ask → Predict → Give reason → Investigate

Success looks like

  • Notice patterns
  • Pose investigable questions
  • Make predictions
  • Give evidence-based reasons
  • Keep questions open
Concept model and worked application

Turn an observation into a testable question

Ice melts faster in sun→Which place melts ice faster?→I predict the sunny place

The question identifies what will be compared.

Write a prediction with a reason

I predict...because...based on...

A reason connects the prediction to previous evidence or experience.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: pose questions to explore observed simple patterns and relationships and make predictions based on experiences
  • E1: posing questions about how to make sounds with different instruments, such as: ‘If I do this, will it always produce a higher pitched sound?’
  • E2: posing questions about the appearance or position of celestial objects in space across time, such as: ‘I wonder if the moon will look the same tomorrow or next week, as it does today?’
  • E3: making predictions about what might occur when materials such as playdough or tissue paper are pulled with different strengths
  • E4: making predictions about the relationship between vibration and sound, such as: ‘I think that if a ruler is twanged harder, it will make a louder sound’
  • E5: making predictions about future appearances of phenomena in the sky at certain times of the week, month or year, such as the moon or satellites

Revision Notes

Key idea: A useful scientific question can be explored by observing or testing. A prediction states what may happen and gives a reason based on experience or a pattern.

Remember

  • Notice patterns
  • Pose investigable questions
  • Make predictions
  • Give evidence-based reasons
  • Keep questions open

Quick questions

  • What makes a question investigable?
  • Write a prediction using because.
  • Turn one observation into a question.
  • Why is a prediction different from a result?

If an answer is uncertain, return to the model and explain what each part represents before calculating or drawing a conclusion.

Common misconceptions
  • Question cannot be explored — Ask about something observable or testable.
  • Prediction without reason — Use because to connect the prediction to evidence.
  • Question already gives the answer — Keep the question open until evidence is collected.
