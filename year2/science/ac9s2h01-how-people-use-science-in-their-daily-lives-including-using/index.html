YEAR 2 SCIENCE · AC9S2H01

Use a pattern to make a useful choice

Learning intention: Describe everyday uses of science. Explain how observations and patterns can support predictions and decisions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Predict before choosing where ice will stay solid

In two earlier comparisons with similar ice pieces and the same containers, ice near a sunny window had less solid ice left than ice in a cooler place after the same time. A family uses that pattern to predict a cooler place may slow melting in a similar trial. They check what happens instead of treating the prediction as certain.

Earlier comparisonSunny windowCooler place
Trial 1, after equal timeLess solid ice leftMore solid ice left
Trial 2, after equal timeLess solid ice leftMore solid ice left

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Connect science to a need

People use knowledge of materials, sound, weather and living things to solve everyday problems.

NeedUseful scientific idea
Make a bendable modelMaterials respond differently to forces
Choose a quiet activitySounds differ in loudness
Keep ice solid longerWarmer surroundings can make ice melt faster
Try it · Is science only used by people wearing lab coats?

No. Families, gardeners, designers and many other people use it.

2. Give a relevant reason

A prediction should connect to the observed pattern. A favourite colour is not evidence about melting.

Pattern → prediction

Less ice remained in the warmer place before, so it may happen again under similar conditions.

Try it · Does saying “maybe” make a random guess evidence-based?

No. Give a relevant observation or experience as the reason.

3. Check and update

New observations can strengthen a prediction or make us revise it. Ask whether conditions changed before applying an old pattern.

New conditionQuestion to ask
A much larger ice pieceIs the comparison still similar?
A different containerCould the container affect the result?
Try it · Must the family ignore a new result that disagrees with its prediction?

No. Keep the result and use the new evidence to improve a later prediction.

Common mistakes

“A useful prediction is a promise.” A pattern helps us predict; it does not guarantee the outcome.

“One rule works in every place.” Check the situation and learn from appropriate local knowledge and experts.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain one everyday science choice

2 minutes: choose an everyday need with an adult. 5 minutes: draw the need, a relevant observation and a prediction. 3 minutes: explain the decision and what evidence could change it.

Adjust the challenge Support: use “I noticed… so I predict…”. Extend: name a condition that could make an earlier pattern less useful.

Use supplied records or safe household observations. An adult makes decisions about food storage, weather hazards and tools.

Three tricky questions

1. A prediction was useful yesterday. Must it be correct today?

No. Check whether the conditions and new evidence support it.

2. Is “I like the blue box” evidence that ice melts more slowly in it?

No. Compare observations under suitable conditions.

3. Can local sky patterns help people plan activities?

Yes. People can use repeated observations and appropriate local knowledge to make plans, without treating them as guarantees.

FAQ

What makes a scientific prediction useful?

A clear claim about what may happen, supported by relevant experience or a pattern.

How should First Nations knowledge be used?

Respect cultural authority and diverse, place-specific knowledge. Use appropriate community-approved sources.

What students learn in AC9S2H01

People use science to solve problems, make choices and predict what may happen. Predictions are stronger when they are based on an observed pattern or previous evidence.

Learning sequence: Notice need → Use science → Observe pattern → Predict → Check

Success looks like

  • Identify daily science uses
  • Recognise patterns
  • Make evidence-based predictions
  • Check predictions
  • Explain science decisions

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: describe how people use science in their daily lives, including using patterns to make scientific predictions
  • E1: learning how First Nations Australians use observations of the night sky to assist with navigation (teaching context)
  • E2: recognising that astronomers use patterns of movement of celestial bodies in the sky, such as stars and comets, to make predictions about future appearances
  • E3: listening to music and learning from musicians about how music can be understood as patterns of sounds and how they use their body or instruments to create music
  • E4: discussing how we manage sound at home to ensure that we do not disturb each other or our neighbours, such as quietly closing doors, turning down the volume, taking off shoes on wooden floors or using headphones
  • E5: investigating toys and digital tools that are voice activated, and engaging in guided discussion about how some devices use voice patterns to recognise the unique features of an individual’s voice
  • E6: exploring how sound-activated and voice-activated tools help people manage daily activities such as turning on lights and communicating with others
  • E7: learning from people who work with materials, such as woodworkers, product designers or artists such as fibre artists or sculptors, about how they learn about properties of materials and how they use creativity when manipulating materials
  • E8: exploring how physically changing materials helps us to re-use them in a variety of ways, and decrease waste
  • E9: considering how First Nations Australians use scientific practices such as sorting, classification and estimation to make predictions (teaching context)
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use a reliable, community-approved source and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges and practices as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation or reproducing cultural material.

Common misconceptions
  • Prediction means random guess — Give a reason based on a pattern or previous observation.
  • Science only happens in laboratories — Science is used in homes, schools, farms, hospitals and many jobs.
  • One observation proves a pattern — Look for repeated evidence before claiming a pattern.
