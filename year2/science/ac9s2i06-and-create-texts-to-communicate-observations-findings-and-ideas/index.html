YEAR 2 SCIENCE · AC9S2I06

Make the evidence clear to a reader

Learning intention: Create texts that communicate observations, findings and ideas. Use clear labels and accurate everyday and scientific vocabulary.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A report needs the unit and the comparison

Supplied records show a flat strip held 1 counter and a folded strip held 3 before bending down. A useful finding says: “In our test, the folded strip held more counters than the flat strip.” A clear report also names the paper, gap and unit. It does not claim every folded material is always stronger.

StripCounters held before bending down
Flat paper strip1
Folded paper strip3

Artwork provides context. Read the supplied records for exact counts and measurements; do not measure the illustration.

Learn the skill

1. Separate the parts of the report

A question says what was investigated. Observations report what happened. A finding explains what the evidence shows.

PartExample
QuestionWhich strip holds more counters?
ObservationFolded: 3 counters; flat: 1 counter
FindingThe folded strip held more in this test
Try it · Is “Will thicker paper change the result?” an observation?

No. It is a further question.

2. Use helpful words and labels

Name the object, action and unit. Everyday words are useful; scientific words such as material and observation help when used accurately.

UnclearClearer
It did betterThe folded paper strip held more counters
It held 3It held 3 identical counters before bending down
Try it · Must a report use long science words to be clear?

No. Use accurate words the audience understands.

3. Choose a format and check it

Use a short report, labelled drawing or table. A partner should be able to find the question, results and finding. Digital audio or video may help when suitable.

Reader check

Can someone tell which strip was tested, what was counted and what the records show?

Try it · May a neat drawing replace the count of 3 with 4 to look more balanced?

No. Every text and visual must match the observation record.

Common mistakes

“A finding can go beyond every recorded condition.” Limit the claim to the evidence from the actual test.

“A picture does not need labels.” Labels and a caption can explain objects, actions, units and results.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a mini investigation report

2 minutes: read the supplied results. 5 minutes: create a labelled drawing, table or three-sentence report. 3 minutes: ask a partner to identify the observation and finding, then improve an unclear word.

Adjust the challenge Support: dictate sentences and add labels. Extend: add a clearly labelled further question without presenting it as a finding.

Use supplied records. Share photographs, audio and video only with adult guidance and permission from people recorded.

Three tricky questions

1. Can a finding be correct without saying “always”?

Yes. A finding limited to the actual test is often more accurate.

2. The table says 3, but the report says 4. Which needs checking?

Check the original observation record and correct the mismatch.

3. Can a report include a prediction as well as a finding?

Yes. Label them so the reader knows what was expected and what was observed.

FAQ

What is scientific vocabulary?

Words used accurately to describe scientific ideas and evidence, such as material, vibration and observation.

Are spoken explanations useful?

Yes. This code also asks students to write and create texts; a spoken explanation can support a labelled drawing or dictated report.

What students learn in AC9S2I06

Scientific communication helps another person understand what was investigated, what was observed and what the evidence suggests. Labels, tables, diagrams and precise vocabulary improve clarity.

Curriculum coverage and elaborations

The content description and elaborations below show the curriculum ideas taught in this unit. Items marked as teaching context support lesson planning.

  • Content description: write and create texts to communicate observations, findings and ideas, using everyday and scientific vocabulary
  • E1: using learnt scientific vocabulary and structuring texts to sequence events, processes or ideas
  • E2: creating and narrating a short animation to show the changing position of the sun across the day and using terms such as ‘sunrise’, ‘sunset’ and ‘horizon’
  • E3: creating a class model of the solar system and naming the sun and planets
  • E4: making a collage to represent and display all the ways a material can be physically changed
  • E5: presenting and sharing musical instruments, through dance and song, to show what is vibrating to make the sound
  • E6: presenting findings of investigations using charts, read-alouds, slideshows or displays using everyday and scientific vocabulary
  • E7: acknowledging and learning about First Nations Australians’ ways of sharing astronomical knowledge across generations through oral traditions that include cultural accounts, stories, song and dance (teaching context)
Cultural safety for First Nations elaborations

Use a reliable, community-approved source and identify the specific Nation, People or community where the source does. Avoid presenting diverse First Nations knowledges and practices as one generic tradition. Follow local cultural and intellectual property protocols before inviting community participation or reproducing cultural material.

Quick questions

  • What belongs in a finding?
  • Why label a diagram?
  • Name one scientific word.
  • How can you check whether a report is clear?

If an answer is uncertain, return to the model and explain what each part represents before calculating or drawing a conclusion.

Common misconceptions
  • Finding without evidence — Refer to an observation or measurement.
  • Too many vague words — Use precise everyday and scientific vocabulary.
  • Diagram without labels — Labels show what each part represents.
