Newton’s Laws of Motion: Force, Mass and Acceleration

Learn how forces change motion, how to calculate net force and acceleration, and how Newton’s three laws explain everyday motion, sport and vehicle safety.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

Newton’s first law describes inertia: an object remains at rest or continues at constant velocity unless a non-zero net force acts. Newton’s second law connects net force, mass and acceleration: acceleration increases with net force and decreases as mass increases. Newton’s third law says forces arise in interaction pairs: if object A pushes object B, B pushes A with an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction.

The key Year 10 skill is to identify forces on one object, calculate the net force, then use the evidence to explain or predict its acceleration.

Newton’s laws cheat sheet

First law — inertia

Net force = 0 → acceleration = 0.

The object may be stationary or moving at constant velocity.

Second law

Fnet = ma

a = Fnet/m

m = Fnet/a

1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Third law

Interaction forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but act on different objects.

Problem-solving order

1. Choose the object.

2. Identify forces on it.

3. Choose a positive direction.

4. Calculate net force.

5. Use Fnet = ma.

6. State direction and units.

10 important worked examples

1. Balanced forces

A book has 12 N weight downward and a 12 N normal force upward.

Fnet = 12 − 12 = 0 N, so acceleration is 0. This does not mean there are no forces; it means the forces balance.

2. Find net force

A trolley is pushed 50 N right while friction is 18 N left.

Take right as positive: Fnet = 50 − 18 = 32 N right.

3. Calculate acceleration

A 1200 kg car experiences a net force of 3600 N forward.

a = Fnet/m = 3600/1200 = 3.0 m/s² forward.

4. Calculate force

A 6 kg object accelerates at 2.5 m/s².

Fnet = ma = 6 × 2.5 = 15 N in the direction of acceleration.

5. Same force, different mass

A 40 N net force acts on 5 kg and 10 kg carts.

5 kg: a = 40/5 = 8 m/s². 10 kg: a = 40/10 = 4 m/s².

For the same net force, doubling mass halves acceleration.

6. First law and constant velocity

A cyclist travels in a straight line at constant velocity. The driving force is 90 N forward.

Because acceleration is 0, net force is 0; resistive forces must total 90 N backward.

7. Third-law pair

A swimmer pushes water backward. The water pushes the swimmer forward with an equal-magnitude force.

These forces do not cancel because they act on different objects.

8. Seatbelts and stopping

When a car stops suddenly, a passenger’s body tends to continue moving because of inertia. The seatbelt provides the force that changes the passenger’s velocity.

Increasing the stopping time reduces the average acceleration magnitude and therefore reduces the average force on the passenger.

9. Motion data

A cart’s velocity changes from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s.

a = Δv/Δt = (8 − 2)/3 = 2 m/s². If mass is 4 kg, Fnet = 4 × 2 = 8 N.

10. Sport application

A 0.15 kg ball accelerates at 120 m/s² while being struck.

Fnet = 0.15 × 120 = 18 N.

This links measurable mass and acceleration to the net force during the interaction; real impacts may involve changing force over time.

Common mistakes
  • “A moving object needs a forward force to keep moving.” Not if velocity is constant; zero net force is sufficient.
  • Using one force instead of net force in F = ma. Add forces with direction first.
  • Thinking action and reaction cancel. They act on different objects; forces cancel only when they act on the same object and sum to zero.
  • Confusing speed with acceleration. Acceleration is change in velocity, not simply “moving fast”.
  • Forgetting direction. Force and acceleration are vectors; state the direction or use a sign convention.
  • Using mass in grams. Convert to kilograms before using F = ma.
10 important questions to solve
  1. A box has 80 N right and 35 N left acting on it. Find the net force.
  2. A 10 kg cart experiences 25 N net force. Find its acceleration.
  3. What net force is required to accelerate 1500 kg at 2 m/s²?
  4. A car moves at constant velocity while its engine provides 700 N forward. What total resistive force acts?
  5. Explain why a passenger moves forward relative to a car when the car brakes suddenly.
  6. A 2 kg cart and an 8 kg cart each experience 16 N net force. Compare their accelerations.
  7. A person pushes a wall with 100 N. State the Newton’s third-law partner force.
  8. A 0.5 kg ball changes velocity from 4 m/s to 10 m/s in 0.2 s. Find average acceleration and net force.
  9. Why do action–reaction forces not cancel when analysing one object?
  10. A truck has mass 6000 kg and acceleration 0.75 m/s². If resistive forces total 1500 N backward, what forward driving force is required?
Solutions with explanations
  1. 45 N right. 80 − 35 = 45.
  2. 2.5 m/s². a = 25/10.
  3. 3000 N. F = ma = 1500 × 2.
  4. 700 N backward. Constant velocity means net force is zero.
  5. Inertia. The passenger tends to maintain their previous velocity until the seatbelt or another force changes it.
  6. 8 m/s² and 2 m/s². a = F/m; the larger mass accelerates less for the same force.
  7. The wall pushes the person with 100 N in the opposite direction. The two forces act on different objects.
  8. 30 m/s² and 15 N. a = (10 − 4)/0.2 = 30; F = 0.5 × 30.
  9. Because the partner force acts on the other object. A free-body analysis includes only forces acting on the chosen object.
  10. 6000 N forward. Required net force = 6000 × 0.75 = 4500 N forward. Driving force must overcome 1500 N resistance as well: 4500 + 1500 = 6000 N.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S10U05: investigate Newton’s laws of motion and quantitatively analyse the relationship between force, mass and acceleration of objects.

  • E1 — motion relationships: investigate moving objects and connect distance, time, speed, force and acceleration. Examples 3 and 9 show the quantitative chain from motion data to acceleration and force.
  • E2 — mathematical representations: use graphs and algebraic formulas to relate force, speed, acceleration and mass. The cheat sheet and Examples 3–5 model Fnet = ma.
  • E3 — First Nations technologies: investigate how First Nations Australians use technologies such as spearthrowers and bows to increase projectile speed and impact. This should be taught respectfully as an applied investigation of force and motion, not reduced to a token calculation.
  • E4 — vehicle safety: model how changes in force affect motion and relate this to seatbelts, airbags and crumple zones. Example 8 explains the mechanism.
  • E5 — sport: investigate Newton’s laws in performance and safety. Example 10 provides a quantitative sport context.
  • E6 — evidence-based argument: use motion/force data to construct an argument about safety decisions such as lower speed limits near schools or for heavy vehicles.
  • E7 — automated vehicles: investigate how driverless vehicles use motion data and Newtonian relationships when braking; students should connect sensing, acceleration and required stopping force.
