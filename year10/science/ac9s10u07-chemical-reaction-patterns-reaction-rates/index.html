WORKED EXAMPLE

Balance atoms, then measure a rate with its units

The synthesis equation 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO has two magnesium atoms and two oxygen atoms on each side. Coefficients change the amounts represented without changing chemical formulas. In a separate fictional gas-producing reaction, measured volume is 0, 12, 20 and 26 cm³ at 0, 10, 20 and 30 s. Average interval rates are 1.2, 0.8 and 0.6 cm³/s, while the overall average is 26/30 ≈ 0.87 cm³/s. The intervals show slowing in this supplied record; none is an instantaneous rate.

Learn the skill

1. Classify and conserve

A synthesis combines reactants into a product; decomposition breaks a reactant into simpler products; single displacement replaces one element in a compound. Count each atom on both sides and adjust coefficients. Conserving atoms is necessary for a balanced equation but does not by itself establish that a proposed reaction will occur.

PatternBalanced example
Synthesis2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
Decomposition2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
Single displacementZn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
Try it · Why is changing MgO to MgO₂ an invalid way to balance magnesium oxide formation?

It changes the substance represented. Keep the formula MgO and adjust coefficients to 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Each side then contains two Mg and two O atoms.

2. Explain factors using collisions

Successful reacting encounters require suitable energy and orientation. Higher temperature can increase the fraction with enough energy, as well as collision frequency. Greater concentration can increase encounter frequency in suitable systems; greater exposed surface area can increase access to reacting solid. These are conditional explanations, not universal linear laws for all reactions.

Fictional matched tests reach the same 20 cm³ gas endpoint. Lump times: 40, 41, 39 s. Powder times: 20, 21, 19 s; starting reactant amounts and other stated conditions match.
Try it · Compare the mean times and the 1/mean-time rate proxy.

Means are 40 s and 20 s. The proxies are 0.025 s⁻¹ and 0.050 s⁻¹, so the powder proxy is twice as large for this fixed endpoint. This is not a molar rate or proof that every powder reaction is exactly twice as fast.

3. Distinguish rate from energy change and yield

A catalyst provides a lower-activation-energy pathway and is regenerated overall. Reactant and product energy levels remain the same for the same overall reaction, so the reaction energy change is unchanged. Faster reaction does not universally mean a larger final yield; amounts, equilibrium and other conditions can matter.

Two pathways have the same starting and ending energy levels; one has a lower highest barrier.
Try it · Which pathway is catalysed, and what can the described pathways alone not tell you?

The lower-barrier pathway represents catalysis. The description does not supply a numerical rate, a universal final yield increase or a practical recipe. Use the same reactant/product levels when comparing the overall energy change.

Common mistakes

“Balance by changing subscripts.” Subscripts specify substances; balance using coefficients.

“Every collision reacts.” Energy and orientation matter.

“A catalyst raises product energy or guarantees more product.” It changes the pathway barrier; distinguish rate from energy change and yield.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Join an equation check to a data check

Count Mg and O atoms in the synthesis example. Calculate the three interval gas rates and describe their pattern. Finally compare lump and powder endpoint proxies, keeping cm³/s and s⁻¹ clearly separate.

Adjust the challenge Support: count each element in its own row. Core: divide change in volume by change in time. Extend: explain why an overall average hides changes during a reaction.

These are paper equations and fictional records, not practical instructions. Do not heat magnesium, mix chemicals or generate gases for this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is 1/time always a reaction rate in mol/s?

No. For a fixed comparable endpoint it is a relative rate proxy with units s⁻¹.

2. Can a catalyst be involved in intermediate steps?

Yes. It can participate and then be regenerated in the overall process.

3. Is the 0–10 s interval rate equal to the 0–30 s average here?

No. They are 1.2 and about 0.87 cm³/s respectively; the average depends on the interval.

FAQ

Why repeat trials?

Repeats reveal variation and support a more reliable comparison, though repetition alone does not remove a systematic bias.

What does activation energy describe?

The energy barrier associated with a reaction pathway, distinct from the overall difference between reactant and product energy levels.

Learning goals

Reaction types show recurring rearrangements, while collision conditions affect how quickly reactions occur.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Synthesis, decomposition and displacement reactions have recognisable reactant–product patterns.
  • Balanced representations conserve atoms.
  • Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions and can be changed by key factors.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should read word and balanced chemical equations, understand conservation of mass and recognise reactants and products. Familiarity with collision ideas helps with reaction-rate explanations.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster than equal-mass chips with acid.
  2. Powder has more exposed surface area, so acid particles can make more successful collisions with the solid each second.
  3. Both samples have the same theoretical yield when the starting reactant amounts are the same.
Common misconceptions
  • A catalyst increases the amount of product: A catalyst provides a lower-activation-energy pathway. It changes how quickly the reaction proceeds.
  • Hotter particles become larger: Model increased speed and collision frequency, not particle size.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not introduce equilibrium kinetics or rate laws as the target. Do not confuse faster reaction with greater final yield.

Guided practice
  1. Use particle cards to classify one synthesis, decomposition and displacement pattern.
  2. Then change one collision condition and predict its effect on rate.

Work through the sequence Recognise the pattern → Represent the reaction → Model collisions → Test one rate factor, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Independent practice
  1. Classify Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Identify what replaces what.
  2. Explain why increasing temperature usually increases reaction rate using particle collisions.
  3. Explain why powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster than the same mass of large chips under otherwise identical conditions.
  4. Describe how a catalyst changes the reaction pathway and explain what it does not change about the overall reaction.
Check your answers
  1. Displacement. Zinc replaces copper in copper sulfate, leaving copper metal.
  2. Particles move faster at higher temperature. A larger fraction of collisions have enough energy to react.
  3. Powder exposes more surface area to the acid, so more successful collisions occur per second.
  4. A catalyst provides a pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated. The overall balanced equation stays the same.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A powdered solid reacts faster than the same mass in large chunks. Explain the result using particle collisions, then identify a variable that must be controlled to make the comparison valid.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Reaction types show recurring rearrangements, while collision conditions affect how quickly reactions occur.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Recognise the pattern → Represent the reaction → Model collisions → Test one rate factor. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
A catalyst increases the amount of product: Separate faster pathway from equilibrium amount in the selected reaction.
Practice and review
  1. Design a fair investigation to test the effect of concentration on reaction rate, identifying the independent, dependent and controlled variables and how rate will be measured.
  2. A student increases temperature and surface area at the same time and concludes that temperature caused the faster reaction. Evaluate the investigation and redesign it to support a valid conclusion.
  3. Compare the effects of temperature, concentration, surface area and a catalyst on reaction rate, explaining each using an appropriate particle or activation-energy mechanism.
Check your reasoning
  1. Change the acid concentration and measure the volume of carbon dioxide at regular intervals. Compare the initial slopes of the volume–time graphs. Keep the acid volume, temperature, calcium carbonate mass and chip size the same.
  2. Either change could have increased the rate. Repeat the experiment with the same chip size and mass, acid concentration and acid volume. Change only temperature and compare the initial rates.
  3. Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so a larger fraction of collisions meet the activation-energy requirement. Higher concentration increases collision frequency. Greater surface area exposes more solid to collisions. A catalyst provides a pathway with lower activation energy.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Keep reaction classification and reaction rate distinct. For rate explanations require a mechanism: how a changed condition alters the frequency or effectiveness of particle collisions.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to explain why crushing a solid can speed up a reaction without changing the amount of substance present.
