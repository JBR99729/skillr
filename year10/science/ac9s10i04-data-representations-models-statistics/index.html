YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Choose a representation that reveals the evidence AC9S10I04

Learning intention: Construct appropriate tables, graphs, descriptive summaries and supplied mathematical models while preserving units, unusual values and model limits.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A close-looking line can hide a systematic mismatch

The fictional calibration record below uses arbitrary load and response units. Two supplied candidate relationships are L = 6x − 2 and Q = x² + 2x. They are models to compare, not regression formulas students must derive. Calculate each predicted response, then residual = observed − predicted. The L residuals are positive at both ends and negative in the middle, revealing a structured mismatch. Q is closer for these supplied observations, but an improved fit does not establish its physical mechanism or validity beyond the tested range 0–4.

Learn the skill

1. Choose and construct for the data and purpose

A scatterplot shows paired numerical variables; a time plot preserves time order; bars compare categories. Label variables and units, use numerical spacing, and make raw versus processed values clear. A trend model should be distinguished from connections between individual readings. An axis need not always start at zero, but its scale must not create a false magnitude comparison.

Two treatment means are 10 and 11 cm³ of gas at the same endpoint. A described bar chart starts its vertical scale at 9 cm³, so the visible bar heights are 1 and 2 units.
Try it · What misleading impression could arise, and how would you repair it?

The visible bars can suggest twice the measured gas volume, although the means differ by 1 cm³, or 10% relative to 10 cm³. Use an appropriate zero baseline for this bar-length comparison and label the quantities and endpoint; retain the underlying readings.

2. Choose summaries without hiding the raw record

Mean, median and range answer different questions. At one fixed calibration load, repeated responses 8.0, 8.1, 8.2, 8.3 and 11.4 have mean 8.8, median 8.2 and range 3.4 response units. The unusual reading affects the mean and range strongly. It might reflect a recording problem or a real changed condition; do not silently delete it.

At different loads, the response changes substantially. A student pools all load levels and calls their overall range “sensor repeatability”.
Try it · Why is that summary poorly matched to the question?

It mixes the intended response to changing load with repeat variation. Compare repeat readings at the same load, retain load and trial identifiers, and investigate the unusual value using original records and conditions.

3. Connect equations, residuals and limits

The residual sign records whether an observation lies above or below a prediction. For the toy models, L residuals are 2.1, −1.0, −1.8, −1.1 and 2.1; Q residuals are 0.1, 0.0, 0.2, −0.1 and 0.1. Inspect structure as well as size. A supplied equation can be evaluated outside the observed range without its prediction becoming experimentally supported.

Q predicts 6² + 2×6 = 48 at x = 6. The supplied observations stop at x = 4.
Try it · What can and cannot be concluded about 48?

It is the equation’s calculated prediction in arbitrary response units. It is an extrapolation outside the supplied calibration range, not a validated measurement or proven future response. Collect suitable evidence before relying on the model there.

Common mistakes

“A graphing default is automatically appropriate.” Check axis assignments, numeric spacing, scales and missing values.

“The best summary replaces the raw data.” Keep raw observations and explain the processing.

“A better fit proves the mechanism.” Model agreement and explanatory evidence are different tests.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Construct and challenge a model display

Plot the five paired observations on labelled axes and add the two supplied candidate models with a legend. Calculate one residual for each model and explain the pattern. Then compare mean and median for the repeat dataset, keeping the unusual reading visible.

Adjust the challenge Support: calculate predictions in a separate table first. Core: label arbitrary units and distinguish observations from models. Extend: explain why an extra model parameter can improve fit without proving a mechanism.

Use paper or an appropriate spreadsheet with the supplied fictional data. Check formulas, filters and chart defaults; no instrument operation or software result is claimed by the page.

Three tricky questions

1. What is L’s residual at x = 2?

8.2 − 10 = −1.8 response units: the observation is below the model prediction.

2. Which repeat summary is less influenced by 11.4 here?

The median remains 8.2, while the mean is 8.8. Report context and spread rather than declaring one summary universally best.

3. Should irregular numerical x intervals be drawn equally spaced?

No. Preserve their numerical distances; equal category spacing can distort the relationship.

FAQ

Why show a residual plot or table?

Removing the dominant modelled pattern can expose smaller systematic differences that an overlay conceals.

Does a negative model prediction prove the observed sensor gave a negative reading?

No. L predicts −2 at x = 0 while the supplied observation is +0.1. Distinguish model output from recorded data.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Tables, graphs, statistics, models and equations should reveal the relevant relationship and communicate uncertainty honestly.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Representation choice is purpose- and data-dependent.
  • Conventions and scale affect interpretation.
  • Statistics and models summarise evidence but have limitations.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should know graph axes, units, variable types and basic descriptive statistics such as mean, median, range and percentage where appropriate.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A force–acceleration graph tests F = ma for constant mass.
  2. A fitted relationship and uncertainty show whether data support proportionality.
  3. Starting an axis away from zero may be defensible but must not hide scale or imply a false effect.
Common misconceptions
  • The representation with the most features is best: Choose the simplest form that answers the question without hiding uncertainty.
  • Changing an axis only changes appearance: Compare how scale changes perceived magnitude and slope.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not use visual effects that distort magnitude. Do not report statistics without units, context or distribution where relevant.

Guided practice
  1. Redesign the described misleading graph and calculate one useful descriptive statistic.
  2. Explain how interpretation changes.

Work through the sequence Define the message → Select the form → Apply conventions → Test interpretation, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Fictional repeated gas volumes at the same 30 s endpoint: A = 9, 10, 11 cm³; B = 10, 11, 12 cm³. A described misleading bar chart plots their means from a baseline of 9 cm³ to 11 cm³, making visible heights 1 and 2 units. Its vertical label omits the unit.

Check the supplied case

Means are 10 and 11 cm³, a difference of 1 cm³, not a doubling of measured volume. Redraw with a zero baseline for this bar-length comparison, label volume in cm³ and the common endpoint, and retain repeats/spread. A truncated scale must not be presented as proportional total volume.

Independent practice
  1. Choose an appropriate representation for categorical counts and justify your choice.
  2. Choose an appropriate graph for two continuous variables and explain what relationship it can reveal.
  3. State when mean, median and range can each provide useful information about a dataset.
  4. Explain why graph axes, units and scale choices can change how easily a pattern is interpreted.
Check your answers
  1. Use a bar chart with labelled categories and a count axis.
  2. Plot paired numerical measurements on a scatter plot to look for direction and strength of association.
  3. The mean uses every value, the median is less sensitive to extremes, and the range shows the distance between the smallest and largest values.
  4. Labels and units tell readers what values mean. A narrow scale can make small differences look large.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Given the same dataset, choose between a table, line graph, scatter plot and bar chart. Justify which representation best answers the investigation question and what information another representation would hide.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Question: how does temperature change with time in this fictional record? Time = 0, 1, 2, 3 min; temperature = 20, 23, 25, 26 °C.

Check the supplied case

A labelled time plot with numerical spacing makes change over time clear; a table preserves exact readings. A bar chart treats times as categories and is less useful for the requested temporal pattern. A scatterplot can show paired numerical values, but explain time ordering and any chosen connections.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Tables, graphs, statistics, models and equations should reveal the relevant relationship and communicate uncertainty honestly.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Define the message → Select the form → Apply conventions → Test interpretation. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
The representation with the most features is best: Choose the simplest form that answers the question without hiding uncertainty.
Practice and review
  1. A dataset contains time and temperature measurements collected every minute. Select and construct the most appropriate representation, then justify your choice over two alternatives.
  2. A student uses a pie chart to show the relationship between force and acceleration. Evaluate the representation and propose a better one.
  3. Given a dataset with an outlier, choose suitable descriptive statistics and explain how the outlier affects your representation and summary.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

For the time/temperature task, use t = 0, 1, 2, 3 min and T = 20, 23, 25, 26 °C. For the unusual-value task, use repeats at one fixed calibration load: 8.0, 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 11.4 arbitrary response units.

Check the supplied case

Plot temperature against time with labelled units and proportional spacing. For the repeats, mean = 8.8, median = 8.2 and range = 3.4 units; retain and investigate 11.4 before exclusion. These summaries do not automatically describe all load levels or prove the unusual reading is wrong.

Check understanding
  • I can explain and apply: Representation choice is purpose- and data-dependent.
  • I can explain and apply: Conventions and scale affect interpretation.
  • I can explain and apply: Statistics and models summarise evidence but have limitations.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Representation choice should follow the variable types and question. Do not let graphing become a formatting exercise detached from interpretation.

For parents and carers

Show your child a small dataset and ask which graph type would make the pattern easiest to see and why.
