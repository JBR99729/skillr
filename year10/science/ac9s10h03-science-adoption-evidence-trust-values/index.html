YEAR 9 SCIENCE

Explain why evidence and adoption can differ AC9S10H03

Learning intention: Analyse how evidence, trust, cost, access, infrastructure, values and policy interact when society adopts scientific knowledge and practices.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A temperature result and an adoption rate answer different questions

In a fictional insulation test, matched 100 mL samples begin at 50 °C in a room at 22 °C. After 12 minutes, uninsulated temperature decreases are 9, 10 and 11 °C; insulated decreases are 4, 5 and 6 °C. Mean decreases are 10 and 5 °C: 50% less temperature decrease in this test. This is not a 50% reduction in household bills. In a separate fictional programme, 60 of 100 invited households adopt an insulation option. Among the 40 remaining, reported primary barriers are cost (20), rental permission (12) and scheduling (8). These are supplied teaching records, not real community measurements.

Mean decrease: 10 → 5 °C(10 − 5)/10 = 50%Reduced temperature decrease for this test
60 of 100 invited households adopt60/100 = 60%Programme uptake among invitees
40 non-adopters report one primary barrier20 + 12 + 8 = 40Reported reasons; other factors may interact

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Separate scientific validity from uptake

A controlled performance comparison asks whether evidence supports a specific claim. Adoption asks whether people use a practice. High uptake cannot prove a claim scientifically valid; low uptake cannot by itself refute a sound measurement. Check the methods for the first question and social and practical conditions for the second.

Claim: “Because only 60% adopted, the 50% temperature result must be wrong.”
Try it · What is the reasoning error?

It treats programme uptake as a test of physical performance. The two percentages have different denominators and meanings. Evaluate the temperature methods and adoption barriers separately, then explain their possible connections.

2. Analyse interacting factors

Costs, infrastructure, permissions, convenience, trust and values can interact. A household might understand the evidence but be unable to alter rented property. Communication may help understanding while leaving money or access unchanged. One reported primary barrier is a useful lead, not proof of a sole cause.

A proposed response sends a scientific factsheet to all 40 non-adopters but offers no help with cost or rental permission.
Try it · What might it address, and what might remain?

It can improve access to information, but may not resolve affordability or permission. Investigate feasible assistance and appropriate decision-makers, listening to other concerns rather than assuming non-adopters reject science.

3. Evaluate a response fairly

Compare a proposed action with the actual barriers, affected stakeholders and possible burdens. A programme could offer accessible information, permission support or feasible financial assistance, then monitor uptake and performance. Policy can change opportunities, but authority does not replace evidence. A before/after change alone may have other causes.

After a fictional assistance offer, uptake rises from 60 to 75 of the original 100 invitees; no comparison group or detailed follow-up is available.
Try it · What can be stated, and what cannot?

Uptake rose by 15 percentage points among the invitees. The record alone cannot show that assistance caused every new adoption, that all barriers disappeared or that household bills fell by 50%. Gather relevant follow-up and comparison evidence.

Common mistakes

“Good evidence guarantees immediate uptake.” Practical and social barriers can remain.

“Popularity determines scientific truth.” Validity depends on relevant evidence and methods.

“One message fixes every barrier.” Different interacting constraints may need different responses.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Choose a response and an evaluation

Verify the two percentages and label their denominators. Choose one reported barrier, explain how it could interact with another, and propose a feasible response. Name an outcome and a comparison that would help evaluate it.

Adjust the challenge Support: sort performance and uptake statements first. Core: connect factors using because. Extend: distinguish a 15 percentage-point increase from a 25% relative increase in uptake.

Use the fictional paper records. No hot-water experiment, survey of identifiable households or current legal or financial recommendation is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Why is the later relative uptake increase 25%?

The increase is 15 on an initial 60: 15/60 = 25%. It is also 15 percentage points on the 100-household denominator.

2. Does one primary barrier mean no other barrier exists?

No. Reporting one main reason can conceal interacting conditions; follow up respectfully.

3. Can the same scientific evidence lead to different decisions?

Yes. Needs, costs, access and available alternatives can differ without changing whether the evidence itself is accurate.

FAQ

Is trust the same as unquestioning acceptance?

No. Trust can include confidence in competence, transparency and responsiveness while acknowledging uncertainty and asking for evidence.

What should an evaluation measure?

Measures matched to its goals: actual uptake, relevant performance, accessibility, costs and unintended effects, with clear limits on causal conclusions.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Evidence, trust, access, values, policy and perceived benefit influence whether science-related practices are adopted.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Adoption is influenced by scientific and social factors.
  • Evidence quality is essential but not sufficient.
  • Adoption patterns can vary among groups and over time.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should be able to distinguish evidence quality from popularity and identify factors such as trust, cost, risk, communication, values and access.

Key concept

Learning sequence: Define the practice → Evaluate evidence → Map adoption factors → Explain variation

Core teaching

  • Adoption is influenced by scientific and social factors.
  • Evidence quality is essential but not sufficient.
  • Adoption patterns can vary among groups and over time.

Key vocabulary and ideas

  • evidence strength
  • benefit and risk
  • trust
  • access and cost
  • values and policy
Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Genetic screening may identify inherited risk.
  2. Adoption depends on test validity, counselling, privacy, cost, values and possible discrimination.
  3. A responsible explanation separates scientific capability from social acceptability.
Common misconceptions
  • Strong evidence guarantees rapid adoption: Add trust, access, incentives, regulation and values to the model.
  • Low adoption proves the science is weak: Test several adoption factors before inferring the cause.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not label communities as anti-science without evidence. Do not treat adoption as proof that a claim is scientifically valid.

Guided practice
  1. Use a stakeholder map for genetic screening or low-emission transport.
  2. Annotate one adoption enabler and barrier for each group.

Work through the sequence Define the practice → Evaluate evidence → Map adoption factors → Explain variation, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Independent practice
  1. List four factors, other than evidence quality, that can affect whether a scientific practice is adopted by society.
  2. Explain how trust in institutions can affect adoption even when the underlying evidence is strong.
  3. Compare the effects of cost and accessibility on adoption of a new health technology.
  4. Explain why popularity of a claim is not the same as scientific support for the claim.
Check your answers
  1. Examples include trust, cost, access and policy.
  2. Trust affects whether people regard the advice as credible and applicable.
  3. Cost affects affordability. Accessibility includes whether people can reach, obtain or use the technology.
  4. Popularity describes what people believe or do. Scientific support depends on the quality of evidence and reasoning.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Two communities receive the same scientific recommendation but adoption differs. Analyse at least three factors other than “knowing the science” that could explain the difference.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Evidence, trust, access, values, policy and perceived benefit influence whether science-related practices are adopted.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Define the practice → Evaluate evidence → Map adoption factors → Explain variation. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Strong evidence guarantees rapid adoption: Add trust, access, incentives, regulation and values to the model.
Practice and review
  1. Two regions receive the same evidence-based health recommendation, but uptake is very different. Analyse at least three plausible factors that could explain the difference.
  2. A scientifically supported technology is expensive and difficult to access. Evaluate how evidence, equity, cost and trust could influence its broader adoption.
  3. Explain why effective science communication can influence adoption without changing the scientific evidence itself.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Check understanding
  • I can explain and apply: Adoption is influenced by scientific and social factors.
  • I can explain and apply: Evidence quality is essential but not sufficient.
  • I can explain and apply: Adoption patterns can vary among groups and over time.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Separate whether a claim is scientifically well supported from whether society adopts a practice. Adoption can depend on trust, feasibility, values, policy, cost and communication.

For parents and carers

Discuss a public-health or environmental recommendation and ask why good evidence does not always lead to immediate widespread adoption.
