Explain how energy balance affects climate AC9S10U04

Learning intention: Use energy-flow and matter-cycle models across Earth’s four spheres to explain climate patterns, feedbacks and the limits of a dataset.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A small imbalance changes stored energy

At the outer boundary of a fictional whole-Earth model, 100 energy units arrive per time step. Thirty are reflected and 70 absorbed. If 70 leave as infrared radiation, net storage is zero. If outgoing infrared falls to 68 while the other flows initially stay fixed, net storage is 70 − 68 = +2 units per step. This is an initial perturbation: as the system warms, its outgoing radiation changes. Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared; energy is not trapped forever. The toy 30/70 split is separate from a particular measured global energy-budget estimate.

Learn the skill

1. Track energy through connected spheres

Atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere exchange energy and matter. Sunlight can heat land and ocean, evaporation transfers energy with water, and radiation transfers energy to space. Carbon moves through photosynthesis, respiration, combustion and ocean exchange. Energy flow is not the same thing as a matter cycle, and ocean heat storage can delay temperature responses.

A model shows sunlight absorbed by ocean water, evaporation, cloud formation and precipitation, alongside carbon uptake by plants.
Try it · Identify an energy transfer and a matter transfer without confusing them.

Solar radiation transfers energy to the hydrosphere. Water moves between ocean and atmosphere through evaporation and return precipitation. Carbon uptake moves matter into the biosphere. Energy accompanies processes but is not itself a carbon atom or a water molecule.

2. Explain feedback and model boundaries

A warming that reduces reflective ice can increase absorbed sunlight and amplify the initial warming: positive feedback. A warmer system also radiates more energy, opposing continued imbalance. Cloud effects depend on cloud properties and conditions; “all clouds warm” is not a complete rule. Whole-Earth boundary flows must not be added indiscriminately to internal surface–atmosphere exchanges.

Toy change: absorbed sunlight stays 70, outgoing infrared changes from 68 to 69 units per step as temperature rises.
Try it · Has the imbalance disappeared?

No. Net storage is now +1 unit per step: smaller than +2 but still positive. A new balance would require equal absorbed and outgoing boundary flows under the relevant conditions.

1980 to 2020: CO₂ rises 75.45 ppm and anomaly rises 0.75 °C. From 1990 to 2000 CO₂ rises 15.26 ppm while the anomaly falls 0.06 °C. These sparse points illustrate variability and an endpoint pattern, not a complete causal proof.

Common mistakes

“Greenhouse gases stop all energy escaping.” Infrared still leaves; an imbalance changes storage and temperature until flows respond.

“Positive feedback is beneficial.” Positive means amplifying the initial change, not morally good.

“A cool year disproves long-term warming.” Separate short-term variability, measurement baselines and longer-term patterns.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Balance and connect the model

Draw the whole-Earth boundary and label its five initial quantities. Add a +2 storage annotation for the changed case. Then connect a warmer ocean to one water-cycle transfer and one consequence of heat storage, stating which sphere is involved.

Adjust the challenge Support: subtract outgoing from absorbed first. Core: distinguish internal exchange from boundary flow. Extend: explain why different model assumptions can produce different projections.

Use the supplied toy budget and dated data table. No heating experiment or personal weather observation is required to complete the task.

Three tricky questions

1. Does zero net storage mean no energy moves?

No. Large equal incoming and outgoing flows can coexist with zero net change.

2. Can the 30/70 toy split replace every published energy budget?

No. Published budgets have particular dates, rounding and boundaries; keep their component values together.

3. Does an anomaly of 1.01 °C mean the global temperature is 1.01 °C?

No. It means a difference from the stated reference average, here 1951–1980.

FAQ

Why include all four spheres?

Climate involves exchanges and feedbacks among air, water, land and living systems.

Why can warming continue while an imbalance shrinks?

A smaller positive net energy input still increases storage; the sign and the size are different questions.

Learning goals

Energy flows among Earth’s spheres; changes to the energy balance help explain long-term climate patterns.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Climate is driven by energy transfer among the atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere and geosphere.
  • Multiple long-term indicators show climate patterns and change.
  • Models support predictions but include uncertainty and assumptions.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Earth absorbs incoming solar energy and emits infrared energy.
  2. Increasing greenhouse-gas concentration reduces the rate at which some outgoing infrared energy escapes.
  3. An energy imbalance causes warming until flows move toward a new balance.
Common misconceptions
  • The greenhouse effect is a physical layer trapping heat: Trace absorption and re-emission of infrared energy by atmospheric gases.
  • One weather event proves or disproves climate change: Use long-term patterns across multiple indicators and data sets.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not confuse weather with climate. Do not omit natural drivers or misrepresent them as explaining the full recent trend.

Guided practice
  1. Students annotate an Earth energy-budget diagram with incoming, reflected, absorbed and emitted pathways.
  2. Change one arrow and predict two sphere interactions.

Work through the sequence Set the system boundary → Trace energy flows → Connect spheres → Test a changed balance, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Independent practice
  1. Trace the main path of energy from incoming solar radiation to outgoing infrared radiation from Earth.
  2. Explain how greenhouse gases alter the transfer of outgoing infrared energy without saying they simply “trap heat like a blanket”.
  3. Distinguish weather variability from long-term climate change.
  4. Identify one feedback or Earth-system interaction that can amplify or reduce climate change and explain the direction of its effect.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Separate the mechanism (energy balance and greenhouse gases) from evidence patterns and from policy choices. Students should be able to explain all three without conflating them.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to trace incoming solar energy and outgoing infrared energy, then explain what greenhouse gases change in that energy flow.
