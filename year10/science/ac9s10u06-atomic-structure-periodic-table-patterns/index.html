WORKED EXAMPLE

An isotope change and an ion change are different

Magnesium has atomic number 12, so each magnesium nucleus has 12 protons. Neutral magnesium-24 has 12 neutrons and 12 electrons; neutral magnesium-26 has 14 neutrons and 12 electrons. They are isotopes of the same element because their proton numbers match. A magnesium-24 ion Mg²⁺ has lost two electrons, leaving 10 electrons while retaining its 12 protons and 12 neutrons. Mass number counts protons plus neutrons, not the charge or the total of all particles.

Learn the skill

1. Use particle counts consistently

Atomic number Z identifies proton number; mass number A gives protons plus neutrons for a specified isotope. Neutral atoms have equal proton and electron counts. Ions gain or lose electrons without changing element identity. An isotope is not simply a charged atom.

An isotope has Z = 17 and A = 35. It gains one electron to form a 1− ion.
Try it · How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in the ion?

17 protons, 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. Gaining an electron changes charge, not the nucleus or the isotope mass number.

2. Connect shell models to position

For the first 20 elements in this simplified main-group model, occupied shells help identify period and outer electrons help explain group patterns. Magnesium is 2,8,2: period 3, group 2. Calcium is 2,8,8,2: period 4, group 2. Chlorine is 2,8,7: period 3, group 17. The third shell is not universally limited to eight electrons beyond this introductory scope.

Try it · Which pair shares an outer-electron pattern, and which pair shares the number of occupied shells?

Mg and Ca both have two outer electrons and belong to group 2. Mg and Cl both have three occupied shells and belong to period 3. Group numbers are not simply the number of outer electrons for every element.

3. Explain broad property and size patterns

Outer electrons help explain similar chemical behaviour within a main group, while nuclear charge, occupied shells and shielding help explain broad size patterns. Across a defined period, increasing attraction with similar shell structure often decreases atomic radius. Down a group, added occupied shells usually increase radius. Use consistent radius definitions; do not claim every property changes monotonically for every element.

Compare neutral sodium, 2,8,1, with neutral magnesium, 2,8,2, using the same atomic-radius convention.
Try it · Why can magnesium have a smaller radius despite having more electrons?

Both have the same number of occupied shells, while magnesium has a greater nuclear charge. The stronger attraction generally draws the electron distribution closer. “More electrons always means larger” ignores nuclear charge and shielding.

Common mistakes

“Isotopes have different proton numbers.” Different proton numbers identify different elements; isotopes differ in neutrons.

“A positive ion gained positive particles.” In these examples it lost electrons.

“Every group number equals outer electrons.” State the main-group convention and exceptions rather than applying that shortcut universally.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare three representations

Make a particle-count table for Mg-24, Mg-26 and Mg²⁺. Then use the shell table to sort pairs by same group and same period. Explain why a shell model is useful but is not a literal map of electron paths.

Adjust the challenge Support: calculate neutrons as A − Z first. Core: separate isotope from ion changes. Extend: explain why helium is placed with noble gases despite having only two electrons.

Use paper models and the supplied records. No chemical handling, flame test or electrical experiment is needed.

Three tricky questions

1. Does Mg²⁺ still count as magnesium?

Yes. Its nucleus still contains 12 protons.

2. Why is helium not group 2?

Its first shell is full with two electrons; its properties place it with the noble gases in group 18.

3. Do shell circles show exact electron trajectories?

No. They are a simplified arrangement model, not literal circular orbits.

FAQ

What keeps element identity fixed?

The proton number in the nucleus.

Why qualify periodic trends?

Trends depend on the property, measurement definition and comparison set; simplified main-group patterns have limits.

How do groups and periods work in the periodic table?

How do groups and periods work in the periodic table?

Elements are arranged by increasing atomic number, which is the number of protons in the nucleus. A period is a horizontal row; for the first 20 elements, the period number matches the number of occupied electron shells. A group is a vertical column. Elements in the same main-group column have similar outer-electron patterns and often similar chemical properties.

Year 10 example (AC9S10U06): Magnesium has atomic number 12 and, in a simple shell model, electrons arranged 2, 8, 2. It belongs to period 3 and group 2. Sodium has atomic number 11 and an arrangement of 2, 8, 1; it is also in period 3 but belongs to group 1.

Try a periodic-table project question: Compare sodium and magnesium. What is the same and what is different about their positions? Both occupy period 3 because their simple models show three occupied shells. They are in different groups because their outer-shell electron numbers differ. Use the full lesson below to explore why this helps explain element patterns; the simple shell model is a learning representation, not a complete modern atomic model.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Lithium (2, 1) and sodium (2, 8, 1) both have one outer electron.
  2. Both belong to group 1. Lithium has two occupied shells and sodium has three, so they are in periods 2 and 3 respectively.
  3. Elements in the same group share outer-electron patterns and therefore show related chemical behaviour.
Common misconceptions
  • Elements in a group are identical: Describe similar patterns alongside systematic changes down a group.
  • The periodic table is ordered by atomic mass: Use proton number as the modern organising sequence.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not teach orbital notation or quantum numbers as the target. Do not claim the simple Bohr model is a literal view of electrons.

Guided practice
  1. Give students Bohr models without element names.
  2. Infer period and group, then locate each element and predict one similarity.

Reasoning and problem-solving

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Use the Bohr model only for the curriculum purpose: linking electron shells and outer electrons to periodic organisation and broad property patterns. Do not turn the lesson into senior atomic theory.

For parents and carers

Ask your child why elements in the same column often behave similarly and have them connect the answer to outer electrons rather than memorising a table.
