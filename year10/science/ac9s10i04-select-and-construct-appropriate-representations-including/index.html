Represent Data Without Distortion

Tables, graphs, descriptive statistics, models and equations are tools for revealing patterns. The best representation matches the data and purpose, follows scientific conventions and makes uncertainty or limitations visible rather than exaggerating a result.

What students learn — Core concepts and success criteria

Tables, graphs, descriptive statistics, models and equations are tools for revealing patterns. The best representation matches the data and purpose, follows scientific conventions and makes uncertainty or limitations visible rather than exaggerating a result.

What students need to know

  • Tables, graphs, descriptive statistics, models and equations are tools for revealing patterns. The best representation matches the data and purpose, follows scientific conventions and makes uncertainty or limitations visible rather than exaggerating a result.

Success looks like: students can apply the inquiry skill to a new context, justify methodological choices and match the strength of a conclusion to the evidence.

Worked examples

Select a graph for the variables

Continuous independent and dependent variables are often suited to a scatter or line graph; categories are usually clearer in a bar chart.

Expose a misleading scale

A y-axis starting at 98 can make a change from 100 to 102 look dramatic. The scale may be legitimate, but the visual effect must not be mistaken for a large proportional change.

Use statistics with context

Two samples can have the same mean but very different spread. Report range, distribution or uncertainty when it changes interpretation.

Compare model and observation

A line of best fit summarises a relationship; points away from the line are not automatically wrong and may reveal variation, uncertainty or another factor.

Common misconceptions and fixes
  • The most complicated representation is best: Use the simplest form that answers the scientific question without hiding important variation.
  • Changing an axis only changes appearance: Scale changes perception and can influence the conclusion a viewer draws.
  • A mean tells the whole story: Spread, sample size, outliers and distribution can matter as much as the centre.
  • A model line is the data: The model is a summary or approximation; measured observations remain the evidence.
Step-by-step inquiry method

Define the message

Decide which comparison, trend, distribution or relationship must be communicated.

Select the representation

Choose table, graph, statistic, model or equation that fits the variables and purpose.

Apply conventions

Include meaningful titles, labels, units, scales, keys and uncertainty where relevant.

Interrogate the display

Check whether scale, statistic or model could distort, hide or overstate the evidence.

Apply and transfer

A graph of sea-level change uses a very narrow y-axis range. Explain one benefit and one risk of that choice, then state what additional representation would help a reader judge the magnitude fairly.

Student check: answer using specific variables, evidence, method choices or limitations rather than generic phrases such as human error.
