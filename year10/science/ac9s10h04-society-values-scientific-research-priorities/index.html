YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Explain why some research questions receive priority AC9S10H04

Learning intention: Examine how societal needs, values, funding, power and ethical responsibilities shape research agendas while findings remain accountable to evidence.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A transparent priority score is a decision tool

A fictional fund has 100 budget units and three indivisible proposals. A studies urban heat; B monitors water; C investigates youth health. The declared rubric weights scientific merit 40%, societal need 40% and access/equity 20%, each rated from 1 to 5. A scores 4.0, B 4.2 and C 4.4. B and C together cost exactly 100, whereas A+B costs 105 and A+C costs 115. These supplied ratings express a particular decision framework, not measured benefits or a universal best allocation. Funding a question does not decide its eventual scientific answer.

Learn the skill

1. Trace needs and values into questions

A need can direct attention toward a problem; values influence which benefits, burdens and methods matter. Governments, communities, researchers and funders can emphasise different questions. Australia’s 2024 national research-priority edition illustrates attention to health, environment, energy transition and knowledge systems. It is a dated agenda example, not a statement of current spending or proof that every voice was equally represented.

A community requests research on reliable water supplies. A funder proposes monitoring only easily reached city sites.
Try it · Which agenda choice should be examined?

Examine whether the sampling locations address the community’s water question and whose needs are excluded. Access and feasibility matter, but convenient data alone may leave the motivating need unanswered. Consultation should inform the question without dictating a preferred result.

2. Compare feasible choices and opportunity costs

Declare criteria before judging proposals, inspect their evidence and feasibility, then respect the budget. A score can make priorities transparent but cannot make uncertain benefits exact. Choosing B+C leaves A unfunded; that is an opportunity cost. Adding proposal scores does not automatically measure the combined benefit of different research projects.

Alternative declared weights: 70% merit, 20% need, 10% access. Use the same proposal ratings.
Try it · Which proposal now ranks first, and does that settle the allocation?

A scores 4.5, C 4.2 and B 4.1, so A ranks first under this rubric. Allocation still needs budget, feasibility, benefits and trade-off reasoning. Different defensible criteria can change priorities without changing the truth of future findings.

3. Protect ethical responsibilities and evidence

Urgency does not remove consent, privacy, environmental or cultural responsibilities. Identify who can authorise each use of data or knowledge, and respect local conditions. Public access to information does not automatically authorise recording, reproduction or commercial reuse. Funding interests should be transparent, and results must not be altered to satisfy a funder.

A hypothetical sponsor funds a community study but asks the team to remove inconvenient results and publish locally governed knowledge without checking agreed use.
Try it · How should the research plan and reporting respond?

Retain and evaluate the results honestly, disclose relevant funding interests and resolve permitted knowledge/data uses with the appropriate holders and school guidance. Redesign or omit an unauthorised component rather than inventing permission. An agenda can reflect social priorities while evidence remains open to scrutiny.

Common mistakes

“The most profitable question is always most valuable.” Public benefit, equity, scientific merit and feasibility can lead to different priorities.

“Any funding influence makes findings invalid.” Separate the choice of question from the quality of methods and evidence.

“A priority score predicts the research result.” It ranks proposals under declared judgements; it does not supply scientific findings.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Make and defend a funding choice

Choose a feasible allocation from the three proposals. Show the cost, name the values behind your criteria and explain one opportunity cost. Then change one weight and discuss whose needs might receive more or less attention.

Adjust the challenge Support: check feasible pairs first. Core: distinguish ratings from evidence about benefits. Extend: identify what further proposal information would make the decision better justified.

Use the fictional proposals and paper calculations. No real funding application, participant contact, cultural knowledge collection or permission request is part of this task.

Three tricky questions

1. Can all three proposals be funded?

No. Their combined cost is 160 units, above the 100-unit budget.

2. Does the highest score guarantee a successful study?

No. Scores reflect stated criteria and uncertain judgements; methods, feasibility and actual results still need evaluation.

3. Do consultation counts establish a representative public vote?

No. Examine who participated, how they were selected and whose views may be absent.

FAQ

What is an opportunity cost?

A worthwhile alternative or benefit that is forgone when limited resources are used elsewhere.

Can ethical requirements change a research question?

Yes. Appropriate permissions, participant welfare, privacy and knowledge governance can require a different question, method or form of reporting.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Needs, values, funding, ethics and power influence which scientific questions receive attention.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Societal needs and values influence research focus.
  • Funding and infrastructure enable some questions and constrain others.
  • Priority decisions have benefits, ethical dimensions and opportunity costs.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should know that research has limited time, funding and people, and that societies make choices about which problems receive attention.

Key concept

Learning sequence: Identify the need → Locate influence → Trace the funding choice → Evaluate consequences

Core teaching

  • Societal needs and values influence research focus.
  • Funding and infrastructure enable some questions and constrain others.
  • Priority decisions have benefits, ethical dimensions and opportunity costs.

Key vocabulary and ideas

  • societal need
  • value or concern
  • funding and capacity
  • research question
  • benefit and opportunity cost
Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Climate risk can direct funding toward modelling, mitigation materials and adaptation.
  2. Government, community and commercial values influence targets and timeframes.
  3. Research choices should be examined for benefit, equity and neglected needs.
Common misconceptions
  • Scientists freely choose research without constraints: Map funding, infrastructure, ethics, regulation and community need.
  • Funded research is automatically biased or invalid: Separate influence on topic choice from evaluation of method and evidence.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not imply all funding influence invalidates findings. Do not treat ‘society’ as one group with one value set.

Guided practice
  1. Compare two calls for research funding.
  2. Highlight whose needs are visible, whose are missing and what evidence guides priority.

Work through the sequence Identify the need → Locate influence → Trace the funding choice → Evaluate consequences, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Two fictional calls: Fund A seeks urban heat research using city monitoring sites, prioritising rapid deployment and local health benefit. Fund B seeks drought-related water monitoring in remote locations, prioritising access and community partnership. Both request evidence of feasible methods and honest reporting; neither supplies guaranteed results.

Check the supplied case

Compare visible needs, affected communities, feasibility and missing voices in each call. Ask what evidence supports the urgency and whether access/participation is appropriate. There is no unique winner from these brief calls; values shape the agenda without determining the scientific findings.

Independent practice
  1. Give two examples of societal needs that could legitimately influence research priorities.
  2. Explain how limited funding creates trade-offs between research programs.
  3. Distinguish a research priority from a scientific conclusion.
  4. Explain how equity can be relevant when deciding which research questions receive attention.
Check your answers
  1. Food security and affordable clean energy are examples of societal needs.
  2. Spending limited funds on one program can delay or reduce the scope of another.
  3. A priority decides what to investigate. A conclusion states what the evidence supports.
  4. Equity asks who benefits, whose needs are overlooked and who bears the costs.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A government can fund only one of three research programs. Build a transparent decision framework that considers scientific value, urgency, equity, cost and potential benefit.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Needs, values, funding, ethics and power influence which scientific questions receive attention.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Identify the need → Locate influence → Trace the funding choice → Evaluate consequences. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Scientists freely choose research without constraints: Map funding, infrastructure, ethics, regulation and community need.
Practice and review
  1. A funding body must choose between research on a rare disease, renewable-energy storage and drought resilience. Construct and justify a transparent decision framework.
  2. Analyse how urgency, expected benefit, cost, public values and equity could influence the focus of scientific research.
  3. Evaluate the claim: “If society influences research priorities, the science cannot be objective.” Distinguish priority-setting from evidence evaluation.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Do not frame social influence as automatically corrupting science. The curriculum target is to examine how legitimate needs, values and constraints shape research priorities.

For parents and carers

Ask your child why some diseases, technologies or environmental problems receive more research funding than others.
