Construct Evidence-Based Scientific Arguments

A scientific argument links a clear claim to relevant evidence and scientific reasoning, evaluates competing explanations and acknowledges limitations. Strong arguments can change when better evidence becomes available.

Core concepts and success criteria

A scientific argument links a clear claim to relevant evidence and scientific reasoning, evaluates competing explanations and acknowledges limitations. Strong arguments can change when better evidence becomes available.

What students need to know

  • Arguments synthesise multiple credible evidence lines.
  • Reasoning addresses alternatives and limitations.
  • Secondary evidence is accessed and cited ethically, including cultural protocols.

Success looks like: students can apply the inquiry skill to a new context, justify methodological choices and match the strength of a conclusion to the evidence.

Worked examples

Claim–evidence–reasoning

Claim: increasing temperature increased reaction rate. Evidence: mean reaction time fell across all temperature conditions. Reasoning: higher particle kinetic energy increases successful collision frequency.

Compare competing explanations

If two mechanisms could explain the same pattern, state what each predicts and identify evidence that would discriminate between them.

Use evidence proportionately

A small observational dataset may justify a tentative claim but not a universal conclusion.

Address counterevidence

Evidence that does not fit the preferred explanation should be examined, not ignored; it may reveal a limitation or a better model.

Common misconceptions and fixes
  • Argument means opinion: Scientific arguments are evidence-based and open to revision.
  • More evidence automatically means stronger evidence: Relevance, independence and quality matter more than quantity alone.
  • Counterevidence should be excluded: A strong argument addresses credible conflicting evidence explicitly.
  • Confidence means certainty: Use language that matches the strength and limitations of the evidence.
Step-by-step inquiry method

State the claim precisely

Make the scope of the conclusion clear and avoid broader claims than the evidence supports.

Select relevant evidence

Choose observations or data that directly bear on the claim and identify their quality.

Explain the reasoning

Use scientific concepts or models to show why the evidence supports the claim.

Evaluate alternatives and limitations

Address credible counterarguments, uncertainty and what new evidence could change the conclusion.

Boundary / do not overteach

  • Do not use culturally restricted information without permission.
  • Do not hide counter-evidence or exceed the evidence boundary.
Apply and transfer

Two explanations are proposed for declining frog numbers: habitat loss and disease. Outline an argument that uses evidence to compare them rather than assuming only one factor can operate.

Student check: answer using specific variables, evidence, method choices or limitations rather than generic phrases such as human error.

Australian Curriculum coverage

The content description is the required target. These elaborations are breadth examples and should be taught through authentic inquiry contexts rather than memorised as a checklist.

  • E1: constructing a scientific argument showing how a range of evidence supports a claim relating to the age of the universe
  • E2: engaging in evidence-based debates about the role of human activity in global climate change
  • E3: reasoning from a range of evidence to support or rebut claims made in news reports on scientific research
  • E4: examining secondary data to determine the credibility of the source and the validity and reproducibility of the data, and identifying the extent to which the data is consistent with data from other sources
  • E5: acknowledging the need to critically analyse scientific literature for potential cultural bias in relation to First Nations Australians
  • E6: considering the ethical issues of non-therapeutic genetic testing performed by commercial companies
  • E7: using primary or secondary scientific evidence to support or oppose a local action that may impact on global climate change
  • E8: preparing an argument for increased funding for a particular scientific research focus
