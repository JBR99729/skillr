Build a plan that can answer its question responsibly AC9S10I02

Learning intention: Plan valid, reproducible investigations with appropriate controls, sampling, error checks, risk decisions, ethics and Country/Place responsibilities.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A complete method specifies the comparison and the records

In a fictional cooling plan, each of three separately prepared pairs contains two matched vessels with 200 mL of water starting at 45 °C in a 21 °C room. A is unsleeved; B has the test sleeve. At eight minutes A readings are 35.0, 35.2 and 34.8 °C, while paired B readings are 38.0, 38.2 and 37.8 °C. Their means are 35.0 and 38.0 °C. Match vessel design, volume, initial temperature, exposure and timing; randomise positions or order, and retain pair identifiers, raw readings and calibration records. Learn the skill

1. Align variables, controls and error checks

The design must measure the intended outcome while addressing plausible alternatives. Name the sleeve condition as the changed variable and temperature after the specified interval as the measured outcome. Check sensor suitability and calibration; a common offset needs a reference check, whereas repeated independent setups help reveal variation. Detailed repetition of a confounded method remains confounded.

A draft gives A 200 mL at 45 °C but B 100 mL at 50 °C, then attributes the endpoint difference entirely to the sleeve.
Try it · Which design repair comes first?

Match starting volume and temperature, along with relevant vessel and exposure conditions, before attributing a difference to the sleeve. Define when timing starts and how the sensor is positioned and read. More repeats alone cannot separate changes that were bundled together.

2. Justify sampling and context-specific risk controls

Count separately prepared experimental units or pairs, not every sensor timestamp. Select sample size in relation to variation and the claim; three pairs are the givens here, not a universal adequate number. A risk assessment links a specific hazard and possible harm to controls, residual conditions and a stop/revise decision. Eliminating or substituting a hazard can be preferable to relying only on protective equipment.

A proposed real activity uses very hot water near a crowded bench edge and assumes gloves alone make it acceptable.
Try it · How should the plan change before any work starts?

Use school guidance to consider a safer temperature or supplied-data alternative, a stable uncluttered workspace and suitable handling/supervision. Assess remaining risks and stop or revise if controls are inadequate. The page does not provide apparatus-specific approval or a residual-risk certificate.

3. Make ethics and cultural protocols design requirements

Consent, privacy, environmental protection and relevant cultural authority can change or prevent a method. Before an investigation on Country/Place, identify the appropriate local guidance and land-manager requirements through the school. Access, handling, recording and publishing can have different conditions. Do not disturb heritage sites or artefacts or presume one person can authorise every use.

A hypothetical class has permission for an observation walk, but no agreement to collect artefacts or record and publish local knowledge.
Try it · What is the responsible next decision?

Stay within the authorised observation activity. Seek appropriate guidance about any proposed additional use, document conditions and redesign or omit it if not authorised. Do not collect, disturb or publish first and ask later. No real permission or local cultural knowledge is supplied by this example.

Common mistakes

“A long method is automatically valid.” Check whether its comparison isolates the intended relationship.

“Thousands of timestamps are thousands of independent samples.” Identify the experimental unit and independently prepared repeats.

“Ethics can be added after collection.” Responsibilities can determine whether and how collection occurs.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Audit and rewrite a plan

Use the cooling case to list the changed variable, measured endpoint, controls, pair identifiers and calibration/timing records. Identify one confound and give a specific repair. Add a context-specific risk decision and a permission question that must be settled before a relevant field activity.

Adjust the challenge Support: link each step to the question. Core: explain the effect of each proposed repair. Extend: distinguish a well-documented biased method from a valid, reproducible comparison.

Complete the planning audit on paper. A responsible teacher must separately assess any actual apparatus, hot-water work, field access or community-related activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Would repeating the unmatched-volume design 100 times remove its confound?

No. Volume and sleeve condition would still vary together.

2. Does reproducible mean identical numbers?

No. Describe the actual checking process and expect compatible results within relevant variation and uncertainty, not exact numerical identity.

3. Does permission to enter also permit publication?

Not automatically. Check the specific holders, activities, data and agreed uses through the appropriate school/local process.

FAQ

Why keep raw paired records?

They allow others to examine variation, matching, anomalies and processing rather than relying only on the final mean.

What should happen if conditions become unsafe or permission is refused?

Stop, revise the question or method, or use an authorised alternative; useful data do not override those responsibilities.

Learning goals

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Reproducibility requires transparent procedures and useful sample sizes.
  • Risk, ethics and Country/Place protocols can change or prevent a method.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. To test net force and acceleration, use a calibrated motion sensor and several controlled force levels.
  2. Keep total mass and track conditions constant, randomise run order where useful and collect repeated data.
  3. Plan calibration checks and quantify uncertainty before testing.
Common misconceptions
  • Many repeats fix systematic bias: Use calibration and design changes for bias; repeats mainly estimate random variation.
  • A detailed method is automatically valid: Check whether the evidence can actually answer the question.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not treat repetition as a cure for every design problem. Do not access heritage material, sites or knowledge without correct authority and protocols.

Guided practice
  1. Audit a motion investigation for confounding variables, calibration and sample size.
  2. Rewrite the three weakest steps.

Work through the sequence Align the design → Control confounding factors → Plan useful data → Complete responsibility checks, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Flawed fictional motion plan: push a 1 kg cart by hand; add mass while also pushing harder; read one unzeroed motion sensor once; write “it went faster” without times or units. The intended question is how mass affects acceleration at a fixed 2 N resultant.

Check the supplied case

Prioritise: keep the resultant fixed while changing total mass; reference-check suitable measurement equipment and define acceleration from measured velocity/time; collect independently reset runs at each mass with order/conditions and raw units recorded. Replace “faster” with the specified acceleration outcome. Appropriate apparatus-specific safety approval remains separate.

Independent practice
  1. A method changes temperature and concentration at the same time. Explain why this threatens validity and propose a correction.
  2. Identify two features that make a written method reproducible by another investigator.
  3. For a school investigation, distinguish a hazard from a risk-control measure and give one example of each.
  4. Explain why ethical requirements or cultural protocols may require a method to be changed even if it is scientifically workable.
Check your answers
  1. Changing two factors prevents attribution to either one. Change one and control the other.
  2. Specify quantities and describe exactly how and when to measure the outcome.
  3. A hot vessel is a hazard. Handling it with suitable heat-resistant equipment is a control measure.
  4. A design must respect informed consent and relevant community permissions or restrictions even when it could collect useful data.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Critique an investigation where two variables change at once, the sample is small and measurements are rounded heavily. Prioritise the design fixes that matter most for validity and reproducibility.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
A strong plan aligns variables, controls, sampling, risk, ethics and cultural responsibilities with the question.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Align the design → Control confounding factors → Plan useful data → Complete responsibility checks. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Many repeats fix systematic bias: Use calibration and design changes for bias; repeats mainly estimate random variation.
Practice and review
  1. Critique an investigation with one trial, an uncontrolled temperature, vague measurement instructions and no risk assessment. Prioritise four improvements and justify each one.
  2. Design a valid, reproducible investigation to test how surface area affects reaction rate. Include variables, controls, repeats, measurement, risk controls and a method for recording data.
  3. An investigation would involve access to a heritage site on Country/Place. Explain the scientific, ethical and cultural considerations that must be addressed before data collection begins.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Treat risk, ethics and cultural protocols as design requirements, not a form completed after the method. Students should justify controls and sampling choices.

For parents and carers

Ask your child what would make a test “fair” and whether another student could reproduce the same method from the written instructions.
