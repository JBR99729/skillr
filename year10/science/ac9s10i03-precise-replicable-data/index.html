Choose equipment and records that suit the question AC9S10I03

Learning intention: Select suitable equipment, record justified precision and calibration information, and distinguish useful independent samples from repeated digital readings.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A finer display does not guarantee a better measurement

Two fictional thermometers have the specifications below. Their stated accuracy limits apply from 20 to 50 °C after stabilisation. A slower response can hide rapid change even when display increments are small. In a separate toy reference check, a sensor reads 20.4 °C at a 20.0 °C reference and 40.4 °C at a 40.0 °C reference. Under a constant-offset model across that range, subtract 0.4 °C: an indication of 29.8 °C becomes 29.4 °C. This model correction does not remove every uncertainty or establish a real calibration certificate.

Learn the skill

1. Select by range, resolution and response

The instrument must cover expected values and resolve changes relevant to the question. Resolution is a displayed increment, not the complete accuracy or uncertainty. Response time matters when conditions change quickly. Consistent placement, immersion and reading procedure matter too; real equipment requires its actual instructions and specifications.

Task 1: distinguish a slow 0.3 °C change near 30 °C after readings stabilise. Task 2: record temperatures expected to reach 95 °C.
Try it · Which stated specification is especially important for each task?

For task 1, B has finer resolution and a smaller stated accuracy limit under these conditions, although its slower response must be allowed for. For task 2, B is outside its range; A covers 95 °C, but its quoted 20–50 °C accuracy condition does not establish accuracy at 95 °C. Check appropriate specifications before use.

2. Keep raw values and define corrections

Record raw indications, units, reference information, conditions, correction rule and processed values separately. A zero check addresses only part of a measurement process. Repeats reveal scatter but cannot alone remove a shared offset. State what the uncertainty statement means instead of assuming every ± value is a guaranteed bound or a confidence interval.

Independent toy check: reference 30.0 °C, indication 30.5 °C. Apply the proposed −0.4 °C correction.
Try it · What is the corrected value, and does it prove perfect accuracy?

30.1 °C. The remaining difference is 0.1 °C; reference uncertainty, repeatability, response and model assumptions still matter. Keep 30.5 °C as the raw record and 30.1 °C as the corrected result rather than overwriting the observation.

3. Count experimental units and digital records honestly

Digital logging can reduce transcription and timing work, but settings must suit the phenomenon and sensor response. Record sensor/setup IDs, time units, interval, start/stop rules, conditions and independently prepared trial IDs. Closely spaced readings from one cup can be strongly related; they are not new independent cups.

One probe in one cup stores times 0, 2, 4, …, 20 seconds, including both endpoints. Its response time is 30 seconds.
Try it · How many records and independent cups are described, and can it resolve subsecond changes?

There are 11 stored records from one cup. Faster storage does not make a 30-second-response probe resolve subsecond changes. More independent, suitably controlled setups address different questions from more timestamps in the same setup.

Common mistakes

“More decimal places mean more accuracy.” Compare specifications, reference checks and conditions, not display length alone.

“Averaging removes every bias.” A common offset can remain in every reading and its mean.

“Eleven records mean eleven independent samples.” Name the sampled or experimental unit before counting it.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Design an honest measurement record

Make columns for time, raw indication, unit, correction, processed value and trial ID. Enter the toy 30.5 °C reference check and its corrected result. Explain the purpose of each column and one additional metadata item a second investigator would need.

Adjust the challenge Support: retain raw and corrected values side by side. Core: justify equipment against the question. Extend: propose reference checks at different points without claiming they eliminate every error.

Use the fictional specifications and paper records. Do not adjust a real instrument or assume it has a user-calibration control without its instructions and teacher guidance.

Three tricky questions

1. Does A’s stated range prove its ±0.5 °C specification applies at 95 °C?

No. The supplied accuracy condition is only 20–50 °C; range and stated accuracy conditions are different.

2. Is an average calculated to six decimals automatically a six-decimal measurement?

No. Computation can produce extra digits without supplying corresponding measurement information.

3. Are more independent samples always useful if collected from the wrong population?

They can describe that sampled population better while leaving the intended population poorly represented. Check the sampling design.

FAQ

What is precision here?

The closeness of repeated values under stated conditions; distinguish it from closeness to a suitable reference.

Why specify endpoint conventions in logging?

The interval and duration alone do not always identify which endpoints are stored. Here both endpoints are explicitly included.

Learning goals

Calibration, resolution, sample size and consistent technique determine whether measurements can support a conclusion.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Calibration and instrument limits affect measurement quality.
  • Consistent procedures improve replicability.
  • Useful sample size depends on variation and independent evidence.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should read instrument scales, record units consistently and understand why repeated measurements and adequate sample size reduce the influence of random variation.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A motion sensor samples position many times per second.
  2. Calibration and consistent release reduce systematic differences between runs.
  3. Enough independent runs are needed to estimate variation, not merely thousands of points from one run.
Common misconceptions
  • A large data file means a large sample: Count independent experimental units or trials, not sensor rows.
  • Calibration and zeroing are the same for every instrument: Use the manufacturer or laboratory procedure appropriate to the instrument.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not confuse number of digital samples with independent repeats. Do not claim precise measurements are necessarily accurate.

Guided practice
  1. Compare three sampling plans with the same number of readings.
  2. Choose the most replicable and explain independence.

Work through the sequence Set the instrument → Measure consistently → Build the sample → Record uncertainty, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Purpose: compare cooling endpoints of independently prepared 200 mL cups starting at 45 °C under matched conditions. Each plan stores 30 values. A: 30 closely spaced sensor records from one cup. B: ten closely spaced records near the specified endpoint from each of three independently prepared matched cups, with actual times/setup/calibration recorded. C: one reading from each of 30 cups, but starting volumes differ and reading times are missing.

Check the supplied case

B supplies the clearest controlled, documented comparison among these plans, with three independent cup units—not 30 independent cups. Its serial records do not themselves establish that three cups are sufficient for every claim. A has only one cup; C has more units but loses the intended controlled endpoint comparison.

Independent practice
  1. A ruler has 1 mm divisions. Explain why recording a length as 12.34782 cm would imply unjustified precision.
  2. State two reasons for collecting repeated measurements rather than a single value.
  3. Compare the value of increasing sample size with increasing instrument resolution. Explain what problem each improvement addresses.
  4. Explain why units, instrument resolution and calibration information should be recorded with data.
Check your answers
  1. A ruler marked every 1 mm cannot support five decimal places in centimetres.
  2. Repeats show variation and help identify unusual results.
  3. More independent observations help estimate variability. Finer resolution helps distinguish smaller changes.
  4. Units make values interpretable, resolution shows the instrument’s smallest increments, and calibration records help others reproduce the setup.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Two groups measure the same event with instruments of different resolution and different numbers of repeats. Decide which dataset is more useful and explain the trade-off between precision and sample size.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Fictional purpose: distinguish slow endpoint differences near 30 °C. Group A uses four independently prepared cups, 0.01 °C display increments and stated ±0.2 °C accuracy after stabilisation. Group B takes 40 serial readings from one cup with 0.1 °C increments and stated ±0.5 °C accuracy. Both expected ranges fit the instruments.

Check the supplied case

A offers more independent cup units and finer stated performance for this particular slow comparison. B has more records but one independent cup. Check actual variation, reference checks, response, sampling design and claim scope; the counts alone cannot establish an adequate final sample size.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Calibration, resolution, sample size and consistent technique determine whether measurements can support a conclusion.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Set the instrument → Measure consistently → Build the sample → Record uncertainty. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
A large data file means a large sample: Count independent experimental units or trials, not sensor rows.
Practice and review
  1. Group A collects 4 measurements with a high-resolution sensor; Group B collects 40 measurements with a lower-resolution sensor. Evaluate which dataset is more useful for a stated investigation and what additional information is needed.
  2. Choose appropriate equipment for measuring a short reaction time and justify the required resolution, number of repeats and sample size.
  3. A dataset is recorded to different numbers of decimal places by different students. Explain how you would standardise the measurement and recording process to improve replicability.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Make students record instrument resolution and units, not just values. Precision should be appropriate to the instrument rather than invented with extra decimal places.

For parents and carers

Use a ruler or kitchen scale and ask your child what the smallest meaningful reading is and why writing extra digits would be misleading.
