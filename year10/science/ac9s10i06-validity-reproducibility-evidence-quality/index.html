YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Match the strength of a claim to the evidence AC9S10I06

Learning intention: Assess method validity and reproducibility, identify assumptions and uncertainty, and reconcile conflicting evidence through specific improvements.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A repeatable difference can still have a confounded explanation

A fictional plant report records seven-day growth of 15, 16 and 17 mm for A, and 18, 19 and 20 mm for B: means 16 and 19 mm. All three plants in A share one pot, and all three in B share another. B receives the test fertiliser and eight hours of light per day; A receives no fertiliser and four hours. Fertiliser, light and pot conditions are therefore not separated. Three plants per pot do not create three independently treated pots. The arithmetic is correct, but the claim “the fertiliser caused every plant to grow 3 mm more” is not established.

Learn the skill

1. Evaluate the method and the claim separately

Validity asks whether the design supports the intended inference. Reproducibility requires enough information for the relevant checking action; definitions vary, so describe whether you recalculate saved data, repeat collection or investigate a new context. Repeating a biased comparison can reproduce its apparent effect without identifying its cause.

A proposed repair repeats the same two-pot design many times without separating the light and fertiliser changes.
Try it · What must change to test a fertiliser effect more directly?

Hold light and other relevant conditions comparable, independently allocate suitable pots to conditions, define fertiliser treatment and measured growth, record initial conditions and repeat across independently prepared units. Repetition should address the design question, not simply reproduce the same confounding.

2. Identify assumptions and define uncertainty

Assumptions include comparable starting material, suitable measurement, independent units and applicable conditions. An uncertainty statement needs a definition. Instrument accuracy, repeat scatter and a statistical interval are different things; overlapping ranges are not a universal significance test. A precise calculation does not repair a weak causal design.

A second fictional laboratory controls light at six hours and uses four independently prepared pots per condition. A: 17, 18, 17, 18 mm. B: 17, 18, 18, 19 mm. Ruler accuracy is stated as ±0.5 mm under its conditions.
Try it · Compare the means and state an appropriate limit.

A averages 17.5 mm and B 18.0 mm, a 0.5 mm difference in this record. The instrument specification is not a confidence interval for that difference. Examine variation, sampling, measurement and further independent evidence; do not declare a universal effect or prove zero effect from overlap alone.

3. Reconcile conflict and target the next check

Different conditions, definitions or biases can explain apparent disagreement. Compare quality and relevance before counting studies as votes. The first and second plant records do not test exactly the same controlled comparison. A useful improvement names the uncertainty it addresses and the evidence needed to reduce it.

The first report claims a large fertiliser effect; the second has a much smaller difference under better-controlled light and independently prepared pots.
Try it · How should the conclusion and next investigation change?

Narrow the claim to what each design measures and explain the first report’s confounding. A further controlled, independently replicated comparison could test consistency and estimate variation under defined conditions. Specify measurement procedures, starting conditions and treatment allocation rather than saying only “do more research”.

Common mistakes

“Reproducible means valid.” A transparent method can consistently measure the wrong relationship.

“Uncertainty means no conclusion is possible.” State the supported scope and strength rather than treating all claims equally.

“The largest reported effect must be the best evidence.” Examine the design, units, assumptions and alternatives.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a method–effect–repair evaluation

Identify two weaknesses in the first report and explain how each affects the claim. Verify the second report’s means, compare the conditions and write a bounded conclusion. Propose one targeted improvement and the evidence it would provide.

Adjust the challenge Support: separate correct arithmetic from causal inference. Core: connect each weakness to its likely effect. Extend: describe a finding that would make you revise your current judgement.

Use the fictional records. Do not handle fertilisers or conduct plant trials for this paper task; no agricultural recommendation or performed experiment is supplied.

Three tricky questions

1. Is the first report’s 3 mm mean difference fabricated because its design is weak?

No. The supplied arithmetic is correct; its causal interpretation is unsupported by the confounded design.

2. Does the second report establish no possible fertiliser effect?

No. It gives a smaller observed difference under its stated conditions; absence of a large difference is not proof of zero effect in every setting.

3. Can an interval repair the wrong experimental-unit count?

No. Statistical processing based on unjustified independence cannot supply the missing independent treatment units or remove confounding.

FAQ

Why distinguish a limitation from an automatic rejection?

Some evidence supports a narrower conclusion even when a broader claim is not justified.

What is a targeted improvement?

A specific design, measurement or analysis change linked to a named limitation and the additional evidence it could supply.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Evaluation distinguishes error types, assumptions and uncertainty to decide how strongly evidence supports a conclusion.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Validity concerns whether the design supports the intended inference.
  • Reproducibility and error patterns affect confidence.
  • Conclusions must reflect assumptions, uncertainty and conflicting evidence.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should distinguish validity, reproducibility, precision and accuracy, and understand that every conclusion has a scope set by the method and evidence.

Key concept

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A media report claims one small observational study proves a food prevents disease.
  2. Confounding variables, sample selection and effect uncertainty weaken causal inference.
  3. The evidence may justify further study, not the headline claim.
Common misconceptions
  • Random error means a careless person made a mistake: Describe unpredictable measurement variation; separate systematic bias.
  • Reproducible results prove the explanation: A consistently biased method can reproduce the wrong value.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not call every difference ‘human error’. Do not use uncertainty as a reason to treat all claims as equally plausible.

Guided practice
  1. Evaluate a headline against a method and confidence interval.
  2. Rate the claim as supported, partly supported or unsupported and justify.

Work through the sequence Check alignment → Diagnose error → Quantify uncertainty → Constrain the claim, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Fictional headline: “Fertiliser makes every plant grow 3 mm more.” The report gives means 16 versus 19 mm, but three plants share one pot per condition; light is 4 versus 8 h/day. It asserts a “95% interval of 1–5 mm” without giving its construction and treats plants within each pot as independent treatment units. The interval is an unverified report claim, not a calculation endorsed by this guide.

Check the supplied case

The causal and universal headline is unsupported. The mean difference is a supplied observation, but light and pot conditions are confounded, and the stated independence basis is wrong. The reported interval cannot repair those defects or be assumed valid without its method/assumptions. Explain how controlled independent pots and transparent uncertainty analysis would improve the question.

Independent practice
  1. Distinguish validity, reproducibility, accuracy and precision in one sentence each.
  2. Identify one assumption that could limit a conclusion drawn from a school investigation.
  3. Explain why a conclusion can be consistent with collected data but still be too broad.
  4. Describe how conflicting evidence and uncertainty should affect the strength of a scientific claim.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A conclusion is consistent with the collected data but the method sampled only one location. Decide whether the conclusion is internally supported, broadly valid, both or neither.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Evaluation distinguishes error types, assumptions and uncertainty to decide how strongly evidence supports a conclusion.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Check alignment → Diagnose error → Quantify uncertainty → Constrain the claim. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Random error means a careless person made a mistake: Describe unpredictable measurement variation; separate systematic bias.
Practice and review
  1. A study samples one location and concludes that the result applies across Australia. Evaluate the method, the evidence and the scope of the conclusion separately.
  2. Two groups use the same method but obtain different results. Propose a systematic way to investigate reproducibility before deciding which conclusion is better supported.
  3. Evaluate a scientific claim that depends on an unstated assumption. Explain how the claim changes if the assumption is not justified.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Check understanding
  • I can explain and apply: Validity concerns whether the design supports the intended inference.
  • I can explain and apply: Reproducibility and error patterns affect confidence.
  • I can explain and apply: Conclusions must reflect assumptions, uncertainty and conflicting evidence.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Teach students to evaluate method and conclusion separately. A calculation can be correct while the claim is too broad for the sample or design.

For parents and carers

When you see a headline saying “study proves…”, ask your child what sample, method and uncertainty they would want to inspect before accepting the claim.
