YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Connect an expanding universe to testable evidence AC9S10U03

Learning intention: Explain the hot, dense expanding-universe model and how galaxy redshifts, the cosmic microwave background and light-element abundances support it.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

A shifted spectral pattern is evidence to interpret

A fictional distant galaxy has a recognisable spectral feature with rest wavelength 500 nm and observed wavelength 510 nm. The fractional shift z = (510 − 500)/500 = 0.02; the wavelength units cancel. In the cosmological interpretation, expansion stretches light during its journey. This small toy shift is not a new measurement of the Hubble constant, a precise galaxy distance or a complete history of the universe. The broader model is supported by several independent evidence lines.

Evidence linePrediction or explanation in the modelCareful qualification
Galaxy spectral redshiftsLarge-scale expansion stretches wavelengthsIndividual motions and measurement context also matter
Cosmic microwave background (CMB)A cooled thermal relic from the early hot universeNot photons created at time zero
Light-element abundancesEarly nuclear reactions produce characteristic light-element amountsCompare predictions with measurements and uncertainties

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Put events in a meaningful order

The model describes an early hot, dense universe expanding and cooling. Light nuclei formed during the first minutes. Much later, around 380,000 years after the early hot phase, neutral atoms could persist and radiation last scattered widely; that relic is observed today as the CMB. Stars and galaxies developed later. This is not an explosion from a central point into pre-existing empty space.

Cards: light nuclei; later stars and galaxies; hot dense expansion; neutral atoms and CMB last scattering.
Try it · Order the cards and explain why nuclei and neutral atoms are separate stages.

Hot dense expansion → light nuclei → neutral atoms and CMB last scattering → later stars and galaxies. A nucleus is not a neutral atom: neutral atoms also require bound electrons, which could persist when the universe cooled sufficiently.

2. Interpret expansion evidence

Identify the same spectral pattern before comparing wavelengths. A larger cosmological redshift generally corresponds to greater expansion during the light’s journey. A simple speed–distance proportionality is a low-redshift approximation with stated assumptions, not a universal linear calculator for all epochs.

Second fictional feature: rest 600 nm, observed 612 nm.
Try it · Does it give the same fractional shift as the 500 → 510 nm feature?

Yes: (612 − 600)/600 = 0.02. Consistent shifts of identifiable features support a common interpretation; a single unidentified red line is not enough to establish a reliable galaxy redshift.

3. Compare predictions and anomalies

The CMB has a near-thermal spectrum at about 2.73 K today, consistent with radiation cooling as the universe expands. Light-element predictions offer another test. A mismatch should be examined rather than hidden: an ESO report from 2014 described lithium in M54 at roughly one-third of a predicted amount. That dated discrepancy is a historical example of model testing, not a claim about its current research status.

A student says: “The CMB is 2.73 K, so every photon has exactly the same wavelength.”
Try it · What does the thermal spectrum actually mean?

It is a distribution of photon wavelengths and energies with a characteristic shape and peak. The temperature does not assign one identical wavelength to every photon. Multiple evidence lines and their limits matter more than one isolated slogan.

Common mistakes

“There must be a special explosion centre.” Large-scale expansion does not identify our location as a unique centre.

“The CMB came from the first instant.” The observed relic last scattered much later, when neutral atoms could persist.

“A model with a discrepancy is either perfect or worthless.” Investigate the mismatch and compare the full range of predictions and evidence.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Match evidence to a prediction

Make three columns for observation, model explanation and limitation. Place the three evidence lines from the table into them, then calculate the two toy shifts and explain why their agreement matters.

Adjust the challenge Support: start with the chronological cards. Core: connect each observation to a prediction. Extend: describe what a repeatable conflicting measurement would require scientists to check.

Use the supplied records and diagrams. Never observe the Sun through a telescope or optical instrument for this paper activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is the CMB light emitted by present-day stars?

No. It is a much older thermal relic, distinct from starlight.

2. Does z = 0.02 by itself give a precise age?

No. An age or distance inference requires a cosmological model and additional assumptions or measurements.

3. Does the expansion model settle every question about an ultimate beginning?

No. It explains tested aspects of the early hot universe and subsequent development without answering every origin question.

FAQ

Why use several evidence lines?

Their different measurement methods test different predictions, reducing reliance on one interpretation.

What does approximately 380,000 years describe?

The approximate epoch of neutral-atom formation and last scattering associated with the CMB, not the first formation of light nuclei.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

The Big Bang model explains an evolving universe and is supported by converging astronomical evidence.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • The universe has changed from an early hot, dense state.
  • Expansion, cosmic microwave background radiation and light-element abundance support the model.
  • Models and technologies connect observations to cosmic history.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should distinguish a scientific model from a direct observation, know that light carries information, and be comfortable with very large scales and scientific notation.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Light from many distant galaxies is redshifted.
  2. Greater distance is associated with greater recession speed in Hubble’s observations.
  3. This pattern supports expansion of space; it is not galaxies exploding from one central point into empty space.
Common misconceptions
  • The Big Bang was an explosion at one location: Use an expanding-space model with no privileged centre on the surface.
  • A scientific theory is an untested guess: Link the model to redshift, background radiation and element-abundance evidence.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not describe expansion as ordinary motion from a centre through pre-existing space. Do not present current unknowns such as dark matter as proof the whole theory fails.

Guided practice
  1. Mark points on an elastic strip and stretch it evenly.
  2. Measure how separation changes and identify what the model explains and distorts.

Work through the sequence Establish scale and time → Model expansion → Examine evidence → Evaluate the theory, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Independent practice
  1. Explain how galaxy redshift supports the conclusion that the universe is expanding.
  2. Describe what the cosmic microwave background is evidence for in the Big Bang model.
  3. Explain why the observed abundance of light elements is relevant to models of the early universe.
  4. Distinguish an observation made today from an inference about the early universe.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Three observations support the Big Bang model: galaxy redshift, cosmic microwave background radiation and light-element abundance. Explain why several independent evidence lines are stronger than one observation alone.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
The Big Bang model explains an evolving universe and is supported by converging astronomical evidence.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Establish scale and time → Model expansion → Examine evidence → Evaluate the theory. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
The Big Bang was an explosion at one location: Use an expanding-space model with no privileged centre on the surface.
Practice and review
  1. Explain how galaxy redshift, cosmic microwave background radiation and light-element abundance provide independent evidence for the Big Bang model.
  2. A student says, “The Big Bang is proven because galaxies are moving away.” Evaluate this claim using the language of scientific models and converging evidence.
  3. New astronomical observations do not match one prediction of a cosmological model. Explain how scientists should use the new evidence to test, refine or challenge the model.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Check understanding
  • I can explain and apply: The universe has changed from an early hot, dense state.
  • I can explain and apply: Expansion, cosmic microwave background radiation and light-element abundance support the model.
  • I can explain and apply: Models and technologies connect observations to cosmic history.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Teach evidence as evidence for a model, not as “proof”. Make students connect each observation to a prediction of an expanding, evolving universe.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to name one observation astronomers make today and explain how it supports a model about the early universe.
