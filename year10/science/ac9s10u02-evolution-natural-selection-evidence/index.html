Evolution by Natural Selection

Understand how heritable variation and environmental pressures change populations across generations—and how independent evidence supports the theory of evolution.

How populations evolve

  1. 01Heritable variation exists
  2. 02A selection pressure acts
  3. 03Reproductive success differs

Understand

Start with the mechanism

Natural selection is a sequence of population-level changes—not a decision made by individual organisms.

What you need to know

Natural selection requires variation, heritability, selection pressure and differential reproductive success. Mutations and recombination generate variation; selection is non-random with respect to fitness in a particular environment. Populations evolve—individual organisms do not evolve because they “need” to.

01

Variation

Individuals in a population have different characteristics.

02

Heritability

Some differences can be passed from parents to offspring.

03

Selection

Environmental conditions affect survival and reproduction.

04

Population change

Advantageous variants become more common across generations.

Variants already exist

Mutation and recombination produce heritable differences before the pressure acts.

Pressure acts

Predators, climate, disease or competition change which traits are advantageous.

Success differs

Some individuals survive or reproduce more successfully than others.

Frequencies shift

Across generations, advantageous alleles and traits become more common.

Worked model

Antibiotic resistance

Bacterial populations contain resistant variants before treatment. The antibiotic changes which bacteria reproduce; it does not cause bacteria to become resistant because they “try” to survive.

Before treatment

Most bacteria are susceptible, but resistant variants are already present.

Selection pressure

The antibiotic removes susceptible bacteria more often than resistant bacteria.

After reproduction

Resistant survivors reproduce, so resistance becomes more frequent in the population.

Evidence for evolution

Fossils

Ordered rock strata and transitional combinations of features provide a historical record of change through time.

Homologous anatomy

Shared underlying structures can indicate common ancestry even when the structures now perform different functions.

Molecular evidence

Similarities in DNA and proteins can reveal evolutionary relationships that appearance alone may hide.

Biogeography

Species distributions can match patterns expected from isolation, migration and divergence.

Observed change

Population changes such as antibiotic or pesticide resistance allow natural selection to be studied directly.

Converging evidence

The case is strongest when independent evidence lines support the same evolutionary relationship.

Apply

Use the idea in new contexts

Every strong explanation identifies the variation, the pressure, the reproductive difference and the population change.

10 important worked examples

1. Antibiotic resistance

Bacterial populations contain resistant variants before treatment. Antibiotics remove susceptible bacteria more often; resistant survivors reproduce, increasing resistance frequency.

2. Variation first

The antibiotic does not create resistance because bacteria “try” to survive; it changes which existing heritable variants reproduce.

3. Artificial selection

Breeders choose parents with desired traits. Selection is human-directed rather than environmental, but differential reproduction still changes trait frequencies.

4. Isolation

Separated populations experience reduced gene flow and different selection pressures; divergence can accumulate over generations.

5. Fossil evidence

Ordered strata and transitional combinations of features provide historical evidence of change through time; one fossil is not the whole case.

6. Homologous anatomy

Similar underlying limb bone patterns in different vertebrates support common ancestry despite different functions.

7. Molecular evidence

Greater DNA or protein similarity generally supports closer evolutionary relationship when interpreted with other evidence.

8. Biogeography

Species distributions on islands and continents can fit histories of isolation and divergence; geography provides an independent evidence line.

9. Biodiversity

Diversity reflects branching evolutionary histories, environmental change, extinction and selection—not a ladder from “simple” to “advanced”.

10. First Nations context

Curriculum exploration of structural and physiological adaptations of First Nations Australians should use current, authoritative science and culturally respectful framing, avoiding biological determinism or racial typologies.

Replace the misconception

Do not just memorise that an idea is wrong—replace it with the scientifically correct mechanism.

IncorrectIndividuals evolve during their lifetime.
CorrectPopulations change across generations as heritable variants differ in reproductive success.
IncorrectOrganisms acquire traits because they need them.
CorrectHeritable variation exists first; the environment changes which variants reproduce more successfully.
IncorrectNatural selection is completely random.
CorrectMutations can arise without regard to need, but selection is non-random with respect to fitness in a given environment.
Incorrect“Fittest” means physically strongest.
CorrectFitness means relative reproductive contribution in a particular environment.
IncorrectEvolution is a ladder toward perfection.

Learning sequence and worked model
  1. Name the target idea: use natural selection and evidence to explain biodiversity over time.
  2. Build the model: identify variation in a population, name the selection pressure, explain which heritable trait improves survival or reproduction, then describe how allele frequencies change over many generations. Fossils, comparative anatomy, DNA and observed selection should be used as converging evidence.
  3. Apply it: transfer the same idea to unfamiliar data, a diagram, a short scenario or a practical investigation.

Teacher prompt: What evidence or relationship are we using, and what would make this explanation stronger?

Solutions with explanations

01What four ingredients are required for natural selection?

Natural selection requires variation, heritability, a selection pressure and differential reproductive success. Without heritable differences that affect reproduction, trait frequencies cannot systematically change across generations.

02Why does antibiotic use not deliberately create resistant bacteria?

Resistance variants arise before exposure through mutation and genetic exchange. The antibiotic acts as a selection pressure: susceptible bacteria are removed more often, while resistant survivors reproduce and pass on resistance.

03How are artificial selection and natural selection similar and different?

Both change trait frequencies because some organisms reproduce more than others. In artificial selection, humans choose the breeding traits; in natural selection, environmental conditions influence reproductive success.

04What role can isolation play in evolution?

Isolation reduces gene flow between populations. Different mutations, genetic drift and selection pressures can then cause the separated populations to diverge over many generations.

05What evidence supports evolution?

Evidence includes fossils, homologous anatomy, biogeography, molecular similarities and observed population change. Independent evidence lines are most persuasive when they converge on the same evolutionary explanation.

06What are homologous structures?

Homologous structures share an underlying anatomical pattern inherited from a common ancestor, even if they now serve different functions. Vertebrate forelimbs are a familiar example.

07How can DNA similarity support common ancestry?

Related organisms inherit DNA sequences from common ancestors. Greater sequence similarity generally supports a closer evolutionary relationship when the comparison is interpreted alongside other evidence.

08Why is biogeography useful evidence?

The geographical distribution of species can match histories of continental movement, migration and isolation. Island species, for example, often resemble nearby mainland relatives while showing adaptations to local conditions.

09What does fitness mean in evolutionary biology?

Fitness is an organism’s relative reproductive success in a particular environment. It is not simply strength, speed or lifespan; a trait matters evolutionarily when it contributes to successful reproduction.

10Why must discussions of human adaptation avoid racial biological determinism?

Human genetic variation is complex, overlapping and largely continuous rather than divided into simple biological racial lineages. Adaptation claims should use current evidence, precise population context and culturally respectful framing without turning social categories into deterministic biology.

Curriculum and planning

Australian Curriculum and state alignment

Australian Curriculum v9.0 — AC9S10U02: use the theory of evolution by natural selection to explain past and present diversity and analyse scientific evidence supporting the theory.

  • E1: variation, isolation and selection.
  • E2: biodiversity as a function of evolution.
  • E3: fossil, chemical/molecular, anatomical and geographical evidence.
  • E4–E5: population change under selection pressure and the relationship between genetic characteristics and reproductive success.
  • E6: structural/physiological adaptations of First Nations Australians using careful contemporary science and cultural framing.

NSW: closest Stage 5 Science alignment is evolution, natural selection and evidence for biological change within Living World outcomes.

Victoria: closest Levels 9–10 Science alignment is evolution by natural selection, adaptation, diversity and evidence for common ancestry.

Other Australian jurisdictions: state and territory implementations substantially follow the Australian Curriculum v9.0 intent, with local sequencing and wording.

International curriculum alignment

United States: closest NGSS alignment includes HS-LS4-1 to HS-LS4-5.

England: closest GCSE Biology alignment covers evolution, natural selection, adaptation and evidence for evolution.

New Zealand: closest secondary Biology alignment includes evolutionary processes, variation and adaptation.

Canada: closest provincial secondary Biology outcomes cover evolution, selection and biodiversity; wording varies by province.

India: closest CBSE secondary Biology alignment includes heredity, evolution and natural selection concepts.

These are planning comparisons, not exact one-to-one equivalences.
