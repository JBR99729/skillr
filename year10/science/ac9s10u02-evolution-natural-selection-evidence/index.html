AC9S10U02: Evolution by Natural Selection

Understand how heritable variation and environmental pressures change populations across generations—and how independent evidence supports the theory of evolution.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Learning goals: What you will learn

Heritable variation, selection pressure and differential reproduction change populations over generations.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Populations contain heritable variation.
  • Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should understand inherited variation, genes and populations. AC9S10U01 is the strongest prerequisite because natural selection acts on heritable variation.

Concept teaching

Learning sequence: Identify variation → Apply selection pressure → Track reproduction → Evaluate evidence

Core teaching

  • Populations contain heritable variation.
  • Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.

Key vocabulary and ideas

  • heritable variation
  • selection pressure
  • survival and reproduction
  • population change
  • multiple evidence lines
Worked and modelled examples
1. Heritable variation already exists→2. Selection pressure differs→3. Some variants reproduce more→4. Trait frequencies shift across generations

1. Antibiotic resistance

Bacterial populations contain resistant variants before treatment. Antibiotics remove susceptible bacteria more often; resistant survivors reproduce, increasing resistance frequency.

2. Variation first

The antibiotic does not create resistance because bacteria “try” to survive; it changes which existing heritable variants reproduce.

3. Artificial selection

Breeders choose parents with desired traits. Selection is human-directed rather than environmental, but differential reproduction still changes trait frequencies.

4. Isolation

Separated populations experience reduced gene flow and different selection pressures; divergence can accumulate over generations.

5. Fossil evidence

Ordered strata and transitional combinations of features provide historical evidence of change through time; one fossil is not the whole case.

6. Homologous anatomy

Similar underlying limb bone patterns in different vertebrates support common ancestry despite different functions.

7. Molecular evidence

Greater DNA or protein similarity generally supports closer evolutionary relationship when interpreted with other evidence.

8. Biogeography

Species distributions on islands and continents can fit histories of isolation and divergence; geography provides an independent evidence line.

9. Biodiversity

Diversity reflects branching evolutionary histories, environmental change, extinction and selection—not a ladder from “simple” to “advanced”.

10. First Nations context

Curriculum exploration of structural and physiological adaptations of First Nations Australians should use current, authoritative science and culturally respectful framing, avoiding biological determinism or racial typologies.

Common misconceptions and corrections
  • Individuals evolve because they need to.: Track changes in trait frequency across a reproducing population.
  • Natural selection is random.: Separate random sources of variation from non-random survival and reproduction under a selection pressure.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not describe evolution as progress toward perfection. Do not claim organisms change traits deliberately in response to need.

Guided practice
  1. Use a coloured-token population and remove tokens according to one environmental rule.
  2. Reproduce survivors and graph trait frequency over three generations.

Retained teaching sequence

  1. Name the target idea: use natural selection and evidence to explain biodiversity over time.
  2. Build the model: identify variation in a population, name the selection pressure, explain which heritable trait improves survival or reproduction, then describe how allele frequencies change over many generations. Fossils, comparative anatomy, DNA and observed selection should be used as converging evidence.
  3. Apply it: transfer the same idea to unfamiliar data, a diagram, a short scenario or a practical investigation.

Teacher prompt: What evidence or relationship are we using, and what would make this explanation stronger?

Independent practice
  • Explain and apply: Populations contain heritable variation.
  • Explain and apply: Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • Explain and apply: Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A population becomes more resistant to an antibiotic over many generations. Evaluate the claim that individual bacteria changed because they needed to survive.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Assessment-style questions and review hints

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Exit ticket and mastery check
  • I can populations contain heritable variation.
  • I can selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • I can fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Insist on the sequence variation → selection pressure → differential survival/reproduction → population change. This prevents need-based explanations of evolution.

Differentiation retained from the legacy lesson

Support

Use a labelled diagram, sentence starter or partially completed table to identify the variation, pressure and outcome first.

Core

Explain the relationship independently using correct science vocabulary and one relevant example.

Extend

Evaluate a limitation, uncertainty, alternative explanation or real-world implication while keeping the claim evidence-based.

Boundary check

Stay inside Year 10 science: clear mechanisms, valid evidence and justified claims without unnecessary senior-specialist detail.

For parents and carers

Ask your child whether individuals evolve or populations evolve, and have them explain antibiotic resistance using variation that already existed before selection.
