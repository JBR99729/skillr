Evolution by Natural Selection

Natural selection changes populations across generations when heritable variants differ in survival and reproductive success under particular environmental conditions.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

Natural selection requires variation, heritability, selection pressure and differential reproductive success. Mutations and recombination generate variation; selection is non-random with respect to fitness in a particular environment. Populations evolve—individual organisms do not evolve because they “need” to.

Evolution cheat sheet

Sequence

Heritable variation → selection pressure → differential survival/reproduction → allele frequencies change.

Adaptation

A heritable feature that increases reproductive success in a particular environment.

Evidence

Fossils, anatomy, biogeography, DNA/protein similarities and observed population change.

Key caution

Evolution has no goal; “fittest” means greatest reproductive contribution in context.

10 important worked examples

1. Antibiotic resistance

Bacterial populations contain resistant variants before treatment. Antibiotics remove susceptible bacteria more often; resistant survivors reproduce, increasing resistance frequency.

2. Variation first

The antibiotic does not create resistance because bacteria “try” to survive; it changes which existing heritable variants reproduce.

3. Artificial selection

Breeders choose parents with desired traits. Selection is human-directed rather than environmental, but differential reproduction still changes trait frequencies.

4. Isolation

Separated populations experience reduced gene flow and different selection pressures; divergence can accumulate over generations.

5. Fossil evidence

Ordered strata and transitional combinations of features provide historical evidence of change through time; one fossil is not the whole case.

6. Homologous anatomy

Similar underlying limb bone patterns in different vertebrates support common ancestry despite different functions.

7. Molecular evidence

Greater DNA or protein similarity generally supports closer evolutionary relationship when interpreted with other evidence.

8. Biogeography

Species distributions on islands and continents can fit histories of isolation and divergence; geography provides an independent evidence line.

9. Biodiversity

Diversity reflects branching evolutionary histories, environmental change, extinction and selection—not a ladder from “simple” to “advanced”.

10. First Nations context

Curriculum exploration of structural and physiological adaptations of First Nations Australians should use current, authoritative science and culturally respectful framing, avoiding biological determinism or racial typologies.

Common misconceptions
  • Individuals evolve during their lifetime.
  • Organisms acquire traits because they need them.
  • Natural selection itself is random.
  • “Fittest” means strongest.
  • Evolution is a ladder toward perfection.
10 important questions to solve
  1. List four ingredients of natural selection.
  2. Why does antibiotic use not deliberately create resistant bacteria?
  3. How is artificial selection similar and different?
  4. What role can isolation play?
  5. Name four evidence lines for evolution.
  6. What are homologous structures?
  7. How can DNA similarity support common ancestry?
  8. Why is biogeography useful evidence?
  9. Define fitness in evolutionary context.
  10. Why must human-adaptation discussions avoid racial biological determinism?
Solutions with explanations
  1. Variation, heritability, selection pressure, differential reproduction.
  2. Selection changes frequencies of variants already generated by mutation/recombination.
  3. Both alter reproductive success; human choice directs artificial selection.
  4. Reduced gene flow permits populations to diverge.
  5. Fossils, anatomy, biogeography, molecular data and observed change.
  6. Structures sharing underlying ancestry despite differing functions.
  7. Shared sequence patterns can be inherited from common ancestors.
  8. Distribution patterns can match isolation/divergence history.
  9. Relative reproductive success in a given environment.
  10. Human variation is complex and continuous; social racial categories are not simple biological lineages.
Australian Curriculum and state alignment

Australian Curriculum v9.0 — AC9S10U02: use the theory of evolution by natural selection to explain past and present diversity and analyse scientific evidence supporting the theory.

  • E1: variation, isolation and selection.
  • E2: biodiversity as a function of evolution.
  • E3: fossil, chemical/molecular, anatomical and geographical evidence.
  • E4–E5: population change under selection pressure and the relationship between genetic characteristics and reproductive success.
  • E6: structural/physiological adaptations of First Nations Australians using careful contemporary science and cultural framing.

NSW: closest Stage 5 Science alignment is evolution, natural selection and evidence for biological change within Living World outcomes.

Victoria: closest Levels 9–10 Science alignment is evolution by natural selection, adaptation, diversity and evidence for common ancestry.

Other Australian jurisdictions: state and territory implementations substantially follow the Australian Curriculum v9.0 intent, with local sequencing and wording.

International curriculum alignment

United States: closest NGSS alignment includes HS-LS4-1 to HS-LS4-5.

England: closest GCSE Biology alignment covers evolution, natural selection, adaptation and evidence for evolution.

New Zealand: closest secondary Biology alignment includes evolutionary processes, variation and adaptation.

Canada: closest provincial secondary Biology outcomes cover evolution, selection and biodiversity; wording varies by province.

India: closest CBSE secondary Biology alignment includes heredity, evolution and natural selection concepts.

These are planning comparisons, not exact one-to-one equivalences.
