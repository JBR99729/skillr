Explain population change using natural selection AC9S10U02

Learning intention: Explain natural selection through inherited variation and reproductive success, and connect fossil, structural, molecular and geographical evidence for evolution.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

The proportion changes when inherited variants leave different numbers of descendants

In a fictional clonal microbe model, R and S are inherited variants already present before a selection event. Initially there are 50 R and 150 S individuals: 25% R. After the event, 40 R and 30 S survive. If each survivor produces four same-type offspring, there are 160 R and 120 S offspring: R is now 160/280 = 4/7, about 57.1%. R did not appear because individuals needed it. The model assumes faithful inheritance, equal reproduction per survivor and no migration or new mutation during this example.

Learn the skill

1. Build a causal explanation

Individuals vary, some differences are inherited, and conditions can cause different contributions to later generations. A variant can become more frequent if its bearers leave more descendants in that context. Selection acts on variation; mutation does not arise because an organism anticipates a need. Survival matters through its connection to reproduction.

In the supplied model, R survival is 40/50 = 80%; S survival is 30/150 = 20%.
Try it · Why does four offspring per survivor preserve the survivor proportion?

Multiplying both counts by four leaves the ratio 40:30 unchanged. The shift from 25% to 57.1% occurred through unequal survival followed by the specified reproduction, not through an individual S changing into R.

2. Interpret observed evidence within its limits

Compare measured counts and locations before drawing conclusions. In the saved HHMI rock-pocket-mouse classroom data, six sites grouped by substrate total 120 light and 48 dark mice on light substrate, versus 3 light and 54 dark on dark substrate. This is a spatial association; the table alone is not a time series showing the same population evolving.

Try it · What does the table support, and what further evidence would help explain selection?

Colour frequencies differ by substrate. Inherited colour variation, effects on predation or other reproductive success, and relevant environmental comparisons would help test selection. These spatial counts alone do not establish a generational change or rule out every alternative.

3. Connect independent evidence lines

Fossils give ordered historical evidence; homologous structures indicate inherited structural relationships; geographical patterns connect distribution and history; molecular similarities can test predicted relationships. Compare converging patterns rather than treating one resemblance as decisive. Similar function can evolve independently, so function alone is not homology.

Try it · Why combine a short DNA comparison with other evidence?

A small sequence may agree by chance or omit important differences. Broader comparable sequences and independent fossil, structural and geographical evidence can strengthen or challenge the proposed relationship.

Common mistakes

“Individuals evolve because they try.” Population frequencies change across generations; inherited variation exists before selection.

“Evolution aims for perfection.” Success depends on conditions and trade-offs; there is no universal ladder.

“A present-day association proves the whole history.” Distinguish observed pattern from the additional evidence needed for a causal history.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Explain and challenge the population model

Recalculate both survival percentages and the offspring proportion. Now keep the survivors fixed but let each R survivor leave two offspring and each S survivor leave four. Find the new R proportion and explain what changed.

Adjust the challenge Support: calculate counts before percentages. Core: connect inheritance to descendants. Extend: propose evidence that could distinguish selection from migration in a real population.

Use paper counts only. Do not culture microbes, expose organisms to harmful conditions or interpret R as instructions for antibiotic use.

Three tricky questions

1. What is R’s proportion in the changed reproduction model?

80/(80 + 120) = 40%. It remains above the initial 25%, but is lower than in the equal-reproduction model.

2. Must every useful trait have arisen by natural selection?

No. Mutation, genetic drift, migration and historical constraints can also affect patterns; test the relevant explanation.

3. Are humans descended from present-day chimpanzees?

No. Humans and chimpanzees share ancestral populations; evolution branches rather than forming a ladder of living species.

FAQ

Does a scientific theory mean a guess?

A scientific theory is a broad explanatory framework supported and tested by evidence; particular details remain open to investigation.

Why does inheritance matter?

A difference that is not passed to descendants cannot itself cause the inherited frequency change described by this simple selection model.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Heritable variation, selection pressure and differential reproduction change populations over generations.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Populations contain heritable variation.
  • Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should understand inherited variation, genes and populations. AC9S10U01 is the strongest prerequisite because natural selection acts on heritable variation.

Key concept

Learning sequence: Identify variation → Apply selection pressure → Track reproduction → Evaluate evidence

Core teaching

  • Populations contain heritable variation.
  • Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.

Worked examples
1. Heritable variation already exists→2. Selection pressure differs→3. Some variants reproduce more→4. Trait frequencies shift across generations

Worked reasoning model

  1. A bacterial population varies in antibiotic resistance.
  2. Antibiotic exposure does not create the needed mutation; resistant variants survive and reproduce more.
  3. The frequency of resistance increases across generations.
Common misconceptions
  • Individuals evolve because they need to: Track changes in trait frequency across a reproducing population.
  • Natural selection is random: Separate random sources of variation from non-random survival and reproduction under a selection pressure.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not describe evolution as progress toward perfection. Do not claim organisms change traits deliberately in response to need.

Guided practice
  1. Use a coloured-token population and remove tokens according to one environmental rule.
  2. Reproduce survivors and graph trait frequency over three generations.

Work through the sequence Identify variation → Apply selection pressure → Track reproduction → Evaluate evidence, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Paper-token rule: begin with 8 R and 8 S tokens. Types are inherited unchanged. In each generation keep all R but only half the S; each survivor then leaves two same-type offspring. Repeat for three generations, without migration or new mutation.

Check the three-generation record

Start R = 8, S = 8 (R = 1/2). Offspring after generation 1: 16 R, 8 S (2/3 R); generation 2: 32 R, 8 S (4/5 R); generation 3: 64 R, 8 S (8/9 R). Plot the R proportion against generation, explaining that inherited variants leave different descendant contributions in this stated model.

Independent practice
  1. Explain why heritable variation must exist before natural selection can change a population.
  2. Distinguish a selection pressure from an adaptation using one biological example.
  3. Explain antibiotic resistance without saying that individual bacteria changed because they needed to survive.
  4. Name two independent lines of evidence for evolution and explain what each contributes to the theory.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A population becomes more resistant to an antibiotic over many generations. Evaluate the claim that individual bacteria changed because they needed to survive.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Heritable variation, selection pressure and differential reproduction change populations over generations.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Identify variation → Apply selection pressure → Track reproduction → Evaluate evidence. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Individuals evolve because they need to: Track changes in trait frequency across a reproducing population.
Practice and review
  1. A drought leaves mainly hard seeds in a bird population’s habitat. Birds vary in beak depth and deeper-beaked birds reproduce more successfully. Explain how the population could change over several generations.
  2. Evaluate the statement, “Natural selection is random.” Separate the origin of variation from differential survival and reproduction.
  3. Use fossil, anatomical, biogeographical or molecular evidence to construct an evidence-based argument supporting evolutionary relatedness.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Check understanding
  • I can explain and apply: Populations contain heritable variation.
  • I can explain and apply: Selection pressures affect survival and reproductive success.
  • I can explain and apply: Fossils, anatomy, biogeography and molecular evidence support evolutionary theory.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Insist on the sequence variation → selection pressure → differential survival/reproduction → population change. This prevents need-based explanations of evolution.

For parents and carers

Ask your child whether individuals evolve or populations evolve, and have them explain antibiotic resistance using variation that already existed before selection.
