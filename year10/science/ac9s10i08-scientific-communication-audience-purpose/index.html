YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Adapt the explanation while preserving the science AC9S10I08

Learning intention: Create accurate scientific texts for named purposes and audiences, selecting useful language, evidence, representations and appropriate digital features.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two audiences need the same supported finding

Use this fictional cover-test packet: three independently prepared matched pairs start at 25 °C under a fixed lamp for ten minutes. Uncovered rises are 8, 9 and 10 °C; covered rises are 3, 4 and 5 °C. A peer report should retain the method, raw readings, sample structure, units, mean rises of 9 and 4 °C and limitations. A school-science-club committee brief can foreground a 5 °C smaller mean increase and propose further testing. Both must say this is a supplied fictional model case, not a demonstrated building, safety or cost-saving outcome.

Learn the skill

1. Start with an audience and a purpose

Choose whether the text reports, explains, argues or recommends, and identify readers’ knowledge and decisions. A technical term can be defined rather than removed. Simplifying language changes support and structure, not the result, units or essential limitation. Avoid presenting fictional teaching records as a study actually performed.

Draft for the committee: “This cover makes every building 56% colder, so install it everywhere.”
Try it · Write a scientifically bounded replacement.

For example: “In the supplied model test, covered samples had a mean temperature increase of 4 °C compared with 9 °C without the cover. This supports investigating the cover further; it does not establish performance or cost savings in buildings.”

2. Make representations and captions carry evidence

A useful visual names the measured quantity, units, groups, interval and source status. Show the distinction between initial temperature, final temperature and temperature increase. A labelled table may be clearer than a decorative graphic; a comparison chart needs honest scales and text labels as well as colour.

The initial temperature is 25 °C. Mean increases are 9 °C uncovered and 4 °C covered.
Try it · What are the mean final temperatures, and why must the caption distinguish them from the rises?

They are 34 and 29 °C. The 5 °C difference is the same here, but 55.6% describes the smaller rise relative to 9 °C, not the percentage change from 34 to 29 °C. Label exactly which quantity is represented.

3. Create, review and revise for accurate use

Write a complete text, then check whether its audience can understand the claim, evidence and uncertainty. An appropriate digital tool can help organise a table, chart or presentation, but verify formulas, scales and automated summaries. Use logical headings, readable labels, meaningful links, text alternatives and non-colour cues; obtain feedback without claiming accessibility or comprehension was automatically proved.

A draft chart has only red and green bars, no units, no caption and an image description saying “nice results”.
Try it · What specific revision should the student create?

Add labelled group names, the quantity and °C units, interval and a caption stating the bounded result and fictional source. Write a useful description, such as “Mean temperature rise after ten minutes: uncovered 9 °C; covered 4 °C; three matched pairs.” Check that colour is not the only way to tell groups apart.

Common mistakes

“Plain language permits changing the conclusion.” Preserve the supported scientific meaning and its scope.

“More jargon means more scientific.” Use precise terms that the audience can interpret.

“A polished chart proves its data and claims.” Check records, processing, units, scales and limitations.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Create two versions and one caption

Write a peer-report paragraph and a committee brief from the complete packet. Choose a labelled table or graph, create its caption and a text alternative, and explain two audience-based choices. Then edit both versions against the same data and limitations.

Adjust the challenge Support: begin with a shared list of facts that must stay unchanged. Core: justify vocabulary and text features. Extend: invite a reader to restate the claim and use that feedback to revise, without treating supported understanding as independent scientific mastery.

Use the supplied fictional records and paper or an appropriate digital tool. No lamp operation, building recommendation or audience-performance claim is required.

Three tricky questions

1. Can the committee version omit the fact that the data are fictional?

No. Source status is essential to interpreting the evidence and must remain clear.

2. Must every science text use the same formal laboratory structure?

No. Structure should suit its purpose and readers while preserving accurate evidence and reasoning.

3. What should happen if an automatic summary removes a key limitation?

Edit and verify it against the source record before using it. A digital tool does not take responsibility for scientific accuracy.

FAQ

What is a useful text alternative?

A concise explanation of the relevant information or relationship conveyed by the image, including necessary labels or values—not a decorative judgement.

How can communication quality be checked?

Review accuracy and source use, then seek relevant audience feedback about understanding and usefulness. Design features alone do not prove an accessibility or learning outcome.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A policy brief on school-zone speed uses force and stopping-distance evidence, a concise graph and a qualified recommendation.
  2. A technical appendix supplies method and uncertainty.
  3. Layered communication serves decision-makers without hiding the scientific basis.
Common misconceptions
  • Audience adaptation permits changing the conclusion: Change explanation depth and form, not the supported meaning.
  • More technical vocabulary is more scientific: Prefer precise language the audience can interpret.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not simplify by removing essential uncertainty or units. Do not use visual drama that exaggerates effects.

Guided practice
  1. Draft a concise policy brief and choose one data visual.
  2. Peers audit precision, accessibility and overclaiming.

Separate optical information gives reflected fractions 0.20/0.70 for opaque surfaces with negligible transmission. A windy/damp outdoor comparison instead reports +3/+3 °C. Audience A: a Year 10 science class evaluating the method. Audience B: a school-science-club committee choosing the next model investigation. Overclaim to repair: “The cover makes every building 56% colder.”

Check the supplied case

The lamp models support a smaller mean rise under those conditions, consistent with less absorption under the opaque assumption. The outdoor context limits a universal claim; compare methods and conditions before judging the disagreement. A 55.6% smaller rise is not a 55.6% reduction in absolute temperature, bills or every building outcome. The peer version should retain raw records, matched-pair structure and method; the committee version can foreground the bounded 5 °C difference and next test in plain language. Both retain fictional status, units, uncertainty/context, source use and meaningful captions/text alternatives.

Independent practice
  1. Explain how audience and purpose affect vocabulary, detail and text structure in scientific communication.
  2. Rewrite this for a general community audience: “The seedlings’ mean height increased from 4 cm to 10 cm over two weeks.”
  3. Choose an appropriate text or visual feature for communicating a trend in data and justify the choice.
  4. Identify two features that make a scientific argument clear and credible for a school assessment audience.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Content, language, structure and digital features are selected to communicate evidence accurately to an identified audience.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Define audience and purpose → Select the evidence → Design the text → Review accuracy and impact. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
Audience adaptation permits changing the conclusion: Change explanation depth and form, not the supported meaning.
Practice and review
  1. Communicate the same scientific finding to a Year 10 science class and to a general community audience. Explain and justify at least four differences between the two versions.
  2. Create a concise evidence-based explanation of an unfamiliar dataset for a specified audience, selecting appropriate language, content and representation.
  3. Evaluate a science communication product that is accurate but poorly matched to its audience. Recommend changes while identifying information that must remain scientifically unchanged.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Assess communication choices as well as correctness. Students should justify why a representation, level of detail and vocabulary suit the intended audience and purpose.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to explain one science idea twice: first to you in plain language, then as a formal school response. Compare what changes and what scientific meaning must stay the same.
