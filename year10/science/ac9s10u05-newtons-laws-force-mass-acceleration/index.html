Newton’s Laws of Motion: Force, Mass and Acceleration

Learn how forces change motion, how to calculate net force and acceleration, and how Newton’s three laws explain everyday motion, sport and vehicle safety.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
What you need to know

Newton’s first law describes inertia: an object remains at rest or continues at constant velocity unless a non-zero net force acts. Newton’s second law connects net force, mass and acceleration: Fnet = ma. Newton’s third law says forces arise in interaction pairs and act on different objects.

The key Year 10 skill is to identify forces on one object, calculate the net force, then use the evidence to explain or predict its acceleration.

Newton’s laws cheat sheet

First law

Net force = 0 → acceleration = 0. The object may be stationary or moving at constant velocity.

Second law

Fnet = ma, a = Fnet/m.

Third law

Interaction forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but act on different objects.

Problem-solving order

Choose object → identify forces → choose positive direction → calculate net force → use Fnet = ma → state direction and units.

Worked examples

Balanced forces

A book has 12 N weight downward and 12 N normal force upward. Fnet = 0 N, so acceleration is 0.

Find net force

A trolley is pushed 50 N right while friction is 18 N left. Fnet = 32 N right.

Calculate acceleration

A 1200 kg car experiences 3600 N net force: a = 3600/1200 = 3.0 m/s².

Same force, different mass

A 40 N force gives a 5 kg cart 8 m/s² but a 10 kg cart 4 m/s².

Constant velocity

If a cyclist travels at constant velocity with 90 N driving force, resistive forces total 90 N backward.

Third-law pair

A swimmer pushes water backward; the water pushes the swimmer forward with equal magnitude.

Seatbelts

Increasing stopping time reduces average acceleration magnitude and therefore average force on the passenger.

Motion data

Velocity changes from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s: a = 2 m/s². For a 4 kg cart, Fnet = 8 N.

Common mistakes
  • A moving object needs a forward force to keep moving.
  • Using one force instead of net force in F = ma.
  • Thinking action and reaction cancel on one object.
  • Confusing speed with acceleration.
  • Forgetting force and acceleration direction.
  • Using mass in grams instead of kilograms.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S10U05: investigate Newton’s laws of motion and quantitatively analyse the relationship between force, mass and acceleration of objects.

  • E1: motion relationships among distance, time, speed, force and acceleration.
  • E2: mathematical representations of force, speed, acceleration and mass.
  • E3: First Nations technologies such as spearthrowers and bows in applied force/motion contexts.
  • E4: vehicle safety, seatbelts, airbags and crumple zones.
  • E5: sport applications of Newton’s laws.
  • E6: evidence-based arguments using motion and force data.
  • E7: automated vehicles and braking relationships.
