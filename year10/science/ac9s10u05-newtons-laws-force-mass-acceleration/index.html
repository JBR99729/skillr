YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Use resultant force to explain acceleration AC9S10U05

Learning intention: Apply Newton’s three laws, choose an object or system, and calculate acceleration from resultant force and mass.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Calculate the force on the chosen cart

For a 6 kg cart, a horizontal pull of 30 N acts east and resistance of 6 N acts west. Choose east as positive: Fnet = +30 − 6 = +24 N. With constant mass, a = Fnet/m = 24/6 = 4 m/s² east. Assume the cart remains on a level track with no vertical acceleration, so vertical forces balance in this case. The 6 N resistance is not the third-law partner of the pull: the partner to the pull on the cart acts on the pulling object.

Learn the skill

1. Apply the first law to velocity

Zero resultant force means zero acceleration in an inertial frame. An object can remain at rest or continue at constant velocity. Acceleration is a change in velocity, so it may mean speeding up, slowing down or changing direction. A normal force is not universally equal to weight; its size follows the actual forces and acceleration in the chosen situation.

Case A: a cart moves east at constant velocity with balanced forces. Case B: a cart moves east while the resultant force points west.
Try it · How does the motion differ?

A has no change in velocity. B has westward acceleration; while still moving east it slows down. Force direction alone is not always the current direction of motion.

2. Use Fnet = ma and compare variables

Find the resultant before dividing by mass. With the same mass, doubling resultant force doubles acceleration. With the same resultant force, doubling mass halves acceleration. These comparisons depend on what is held constant, not just whether a larger number appears in a table.

Try it · At a resultant of 24 N, what mass gives acceleration 3 m/s²?

m = Fnet/a = 24/3 = 8 kg. The force must be the resultant on the same chosen object or system; a single applied force is not automatically the resultant.

3. Distinguish interaction pairs and fair tests

Third-law forces are equal and opposite forces on different interacting objects, so they do not cancel on a single-object force diagram. For a trolley–hanger investigation, define the moving system. Transferring masses from trolley to hanger can change the driving effect while keeping total moving mass fixed; include the hanger. Hanger weight is not identical to string tension during acceleration.

A paper plan transfers masses from the trolley to the hanger, measures repeated changes in velocity over time, and models pulley resistance.
Try it · What must be controlled or measured to test a force–acceleration relationship?

Keep the total moving mass and track conditions fixed; include the hanger and relevant pulley effects, estimate or account for resistance, and measure repeated accelerations using Δv/Δt. State how the system resultant is obtained rather than equating an individual force with it.

Common mistakes

“Motion requires an unbalanced forward force.” Constant velocity can occur with balanced forces.

“Action and reaction cancel on the cart.” The pair acts on different objects.

“Use any force number in F = ma.” Use the resultant on the selected object or system.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write two force accounts

Draw a cart-only horizontal force diagram and calculate its acceleration. Then describe the puller–cart interaction as a third-law pair, naming the object receiving each force. Finally predict what happens to acceleration if cart mass doubles at the same resultant.

Adjust the challenge Support: mark east positive and subtract. Core: name the system for every equation. Extend: explain a cart moving east with acceleration west.

Use the supplied paper records. Any real trolley or hanging-mass investigation requires teacher-approved apparatus and controls for falling masses and collisions.

Three tricky questions

1. What happens when the mass becomes 12 kg at the same 24 N resultant?

Acceleration becomes 2 m/s² east.

2. Are weight and normal force a third-law pair?

No. Both can act on the same cart. Their respective interaction partners act on Earth and the supporting surface.

3. Can a force change direction of motion without increasing speed?

Yes. A resultant perpendicular to velocity can change its direction; acceleration concerns velocity, not speed alone.

FAQ

Why use signs?

They make opposing directions explicit and keep the resultant and acceleration consistent.

Does Fnet = ma mean the mass changes when force changes?

No. It relates quantities for a chosen system; a fair comparison states which quantities remain fixed.

More teaching examples and curriculum coverage
Learning goals

Net force changes motion, and force, mass and acceleration are linked quantitatively by F = ma.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Newton’s laws relate force interactions to changes in motion.
  • Net force is the vector sum of forces on one object.
  • F = ma supports quantitative prediction and analysis.
Prerequisite knowledge

Students should know force as a vector, distinguish speed from velocity and acceleration, use SI units, and rearrange simple equations such as F = ma.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A 1200 kg car experiences 3600 N net force forward.
  2. Using a = F/m gives 3.0 m/s² forward.
  3. The acceleration direction follows net force; balanced vertical forces do not cancel the horizontal net force.
Common misconceptions
  • A moving object needs a forward net force to keep moving: Use the first law: zero net force means constant velocity, not necessarily rest.
  • Action and reaction forces cancel: Place each force on its different interacting object.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not mix forces acting on different objects in one net-force calculation. Do not treat speed and acceleration as interchangeable.

Guided practice
  1. Draw a free-body diagram for a moving trolley before using any formula.
  2. Students compare the acceleration when force doubles or mass doubles.

Work through the sequence Describe the motion → Identify all forces → Find net force → Quantify acceleration, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Independent practice
  1. Draw or describe a free-body diagram for a book resting on a table and explain why its acceleration is zero.
  2. A 4 kg object experiences a net force of 12 N. Calculate its acceleration and include the correct unit.
  3. Two objects experience the same net force. Explain how their accelerations compare if one has twice the mass of the other.
  4. Use Newton’s third law to explain the force pair when a swimmer pushes water backwards.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Two objects experience the same net force but have different masses. Predict and justify how their accelerations compare, then state what would change if the forces were balanced.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Practice and review
  1. A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s². Calculate the net force, then explain why the forward driving force from the road must be greater than this value if resistive forces act on the car.
  2. A student says, “If an object is moving, there must be a net force in the direction of motion.” Evaluate the statement using Newton’s first and second laws.
  3. A 5 kg trolley is pushed east with 20 N while friction acts west with 5 N. Find the net force and acceleration.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Make net force explicit before using F = ma. Free-body diagrams should represent forces on one chosen object, not forces the object exerts elsewhere.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to identify all forces acting on one everyday object and decide whether the forces are balanced before calculating anything.
