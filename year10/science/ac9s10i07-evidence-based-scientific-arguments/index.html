YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Build an argument that weighs evidence and its limits AC9S10I07

Learning intention: Construct scientific arguments from varied evidence, address counterevidence and respect the ethical and cultural conditions of secondary information use.

More examples ↓Help with gaps ↓10-minute activity ↓

WORKED EXAMPLE

Combine a controlled pattern with a mechanism and counterevidence

In a fictional reflective-cover investigation, three independently prepared matched model pairs start at 25 °C under the same test lamp for ten minutes. Uncovered temperature increases are 8, 9 and 10 °C; covered increases are 3, 4 and 5 °C. The mean rise is 5 °C smaller with the cover, or about 55.6% smaller relative to the 9 °C uncovered rise. A separate fictional optical record reports reflected fractions of 0.20 and 0.70. Both model surfaces are opaque with negligible transmission, so the corresponding absorbed fractions are 0.80 and 0.30. This is consistent with less absorbed radiation, without proving it is the sole thermal influence.

Learn the skill

1. Connect a claim to different kinds of evidence

A claim states what is supported; evidence supplies observations or information; reasoning explains the link through scientific ideas. Here the controlled thermal comparison and the separate optical record provide different information. Reflecting more of the specified illumination leaves less available for absorption under the opaque-surface assumption. Replotting the thermal record several times would not create new experiments.

Proposed claim: “In these matched models and conditions, the reflective cover reduced the mean temperature increase.”
Try it · Which evidence and reasoning support that bounded claim?

The mean increases are 9 and 4 °C, a 5 °C difference. The separate reflection record is consistent with reduced absorption under the stated conditions. Explain that connection while acknowledging other thermal processes and avoiding claims about every building, total energy use or long-term outcomes.

2. Represent counterevidence fairly

The windy/damp outdoor models both increase by 3 °C. Compare conditions, methods, uncertainty and independence before deciding whether records conflict. Wind, water, exposure and heat transfer can change the context. A contrary result can narrow a claim or reveal a new question rather than being hidden or treated as a vote that automatically cancels another record.

A draft argument deletes the outdoor record and says the cover always lowers absolute temperature by 55.6%.
Try it · How should the argument be repaired?

Retain and discuss the outdoor result. State that 55.6% concerns the smaller temperature increase in the particular lamp model, not a percentage reduction in absolute temperature. Narrow the scope and propose controlled comparisons that distinguish changed environmental conditions from other explanations.

3. Check provenance, permissions and cultural authority

For secondary evidence, record creator, source, date/version, methods, units, transformations and relevant access/use conditions. Citation helps verification but does not override privacy, consent or knowledge governance. Appropriate holders and purposes matter; a public webpage or one community’s permission is not blanket authorisation for every use.

Hypothetical source card: an archive permits viewing an entry but restricts reproducing its table. A separate locally governed knowledge record has no agreement for public sharing.
Try it · Does adding a citation authorise publishing either restricted record?

No. Check the specific permitted uses with the relevant holder and appropriate school/local guidance. Use an authorised alternative or omit the material if permission is absent. Do not invent consent or disclose restricted knowledge. These are hypothetical conditions, not an actual licence or community approval.

Common mistakes

“Three copies of one chart are three evidence sources.” Trace shared data and methods before claiming independence.

“Acknowledging contrary evidence weakens every argument.” Fair evaluation can make its scope and reasoning more defensible.

“Anything online is free to republish with a citation.” Respect the specific source conditions and relevant cultural or personal-data responsibilities.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a bounded argument and a rebuttal

Use the thermal, optical and outdoor records to write a claim, two evidence links, a mechanism-based explanation and a fair response to counterevidence. Add a source-use check and one specific next comparison.

Adjust the challenge Support: distinguish evidence from the explanation linking it to the claim. Core: explain why conditions matter. Extend: identify another thermal process that the current records do not isolate.

Use the fictional paper evidence. Do not operate a lamp/heating experiment, test building materials or access restricted information for this activity.

Three tricky questions

1. Is 55.6% a reduction in household bills?

No. It describes the relative decrease in the mean temperature rise of these fictional test models.

2. What fraction is absorbed by the covered surface under the stated optical assumption?

1 − 0.70 = 0.30, because transmission is negligible. Without that assumption, reflection alone would not determine absorption.

3. Does a funding conflict prove fraud?

No. It may require disclosure and closer scrutiny; evaluate methods and evidence rather than inventing misconduct.

FAQ

What makes evidence varied?

Different relevant datasets, methods or kinds of information with examined strengths and limitations—not merely a long list of links.

What should an argument do when important evidence cannot be used ethically?

Respect the conditions, acknowledge the evidential limit and use an authorised alternative or narrower claim instead of extracting or inventing the missing information.

Learning goals

Strong scientific arguments synthesise credible evidence, address alternatives and observe ethical and cultural protocols.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Arguments synthesise multiple credible evidence lines.
  • Reasoning addresses alternatives and limitations.
  • Secondary evidence is accessed and cited ethically, including cultural protocols.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Claim: The universe has expanded from an early hot, dense state.
  2. Galaxy redshift, cosmic microwave background radiation and light-element abundance provide independent support.
  3. Reasoning explains convergence and acknowledges questions the evidence does not settle.
Common misconceptions
  • A persuasive tone strengthens scientific evidence: Evaluate data quality and reasoning before rhetoric.
  • Acknowledging uncertainty weakens an argument: Use uncertainty to set an honest conclusion boundary.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not use culturally restricted information without permission. Do not hide counter-evidence or exceed the evidence boundary.

Guided practice
  1. Construct a three-evidence argument for a universe, evolution or climate claim.
  2. Add one rebuttal and one ethical citation check.

Work through the sequence Define the claim → Triangulate evidence → Address alternatives → State a bounded conclusion, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Three evidence cards from the accepted dated source records: (1) NOAA Mauna Loa annual CO₂ rises from 354.45 ppm in 1990 to 379.98 ppm in 2005. (2) NASA global temperature anomaly rises from +0.45 to +0.68 °C over those endpoints, relative to 1951–1980; 1991→1992 falls +0.41 to +0.22 while CO₂ rises 355.70→356.54 ppm. (3) The greenhouse explanation connects infrared absorption/emission to energy balance, while other influences affect individual years. Records use the November 2026 saved snapshot and may be revised.

Check the supplied case

Combine the overall increases with the greenhouse mechanism, but retain the annual counterexample and distinguish station CO₂ from global temperature. These cards provide different information, not three wholly independent experiments or full quantitative attribution. Preserve source/date/units/baseline, and do not invent temperature error bars. A justified rebuttal explains why a cooling year alone does not refute the greenhouse mechanism. Check the specific permitted source uses; citation is not blanket permission.

Independent practice
  1. Identify the claim, evidence and reasoning in a short scientific argument.
  2. Explain why three weak sources do not necessarily outweigh one high-quality source.
  3. List four features you would use to compare the quality of two conflicting evidence sources.
  4. Explain why ethical requirements and cultural protocols can affect whether and how secondary information may be used or cited.

Supplied short argument: “In the fictional matched lamp models, the reflective cover reduced the mean temperature rise from 9 to 4 °C. The separate opaque-surface optical record reports more reflection, consistent with less absorbed radiation. Different outdoor conditions need further testing.”

Check the supplied case

Claim: a smaller mean rise in the lamp models. Evidence: 9 versus 4 °C and the separate reflection record. Reasoning: greater reflection with negligible transmission is consistent with reduced absorption. Scope: the stated model conditions, not every outdoor or building result.

Reasoning and problem-solving

Two sources reach different conclusions. Build an argument that weighs sample quality, method, uncertainty, source expertise and any ethical or cultural constraints before deciding which claim is better supported.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Questions and answers
What is the central idea?
Strong scientific arguments synthesise credible evidence, address alternatives and observe ethical and cultural protocols.
What should a strong response do?
Use the sequence Define the claim → Triangulate evidence → Address alternatives → State a bounded conclusion. Connect the evidence, mechanism or data to the claim.
What common trap should I avoid?
A persuasive tone strengthens scientific evidence: Evaluate data quality and reasoning before rhetoric.
Practice and review
  1. Two scientific sources reach different conclusions. Construct an argument that weighs method, sample, uncertainty, expertise and conflicting evidence before deciding which claim is better supported.
  2. Evaluate an online scientific claim using at least three pieces of evidence and explicitly address one limitation or uncertainty.
  3. A dataset contains culturally sensitive knowledge supplied by a community. Explain how evidence quality, permission, cultural authority and citation protocols affect responsible use of the information.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Require students to weigh evidence rather than count sources. Cultural protocols are part of ethical evidence use, especially for knowledge connected to Country/Place and communities.

For parents and carers

Choose two conflicting online science claims and ask your child which evidence they would trust more and what further information they need.
