Chemical Reaction Patterns and Reaction Rates

Recognise recurring reaction patterns, represent reactions while conserving atoms, and explain rate changes using collision frequency and successful-collision conditions.

What you need to know

Synthesis: simpler reactants combine. Decomposition: one compound breaks into simpler products. Displacement: an element replaces another in a compound when chemically favourable. Balanced equations conserve each type of atom. Reaction rate changes with temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts because these alter collision frequency/effectiveness.

Chemistry cheat sheet

Patterns

A+B→AB; AB→A+B; A+BC→AC+B.

Balance

Change coefficients, never subscripts, to conserve atoms.

Faster rate

Higher temperature/concentration, larger surface area, suitable catalyst.

Catalyst

Provides lower-activation-energy pathway; changes rate, not overall amount predicted by limiting reactants.

10 important worked examples

1. Synthesis

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Two reactants form one compound type; coefficients conserve Mg and O atoms.

2. Decomposition

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. One compound produces simpler products.

3. Displacement

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu: zinc displaces copper under suitable reactivity conditions.

4. Predict type

Metal + oxygen commonly forms metal oxide: a synthesis pattern, though prediction still needs chemical knowledge.

5. Balance, don't rewrite formulas

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O becomes 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; changing H₂O to H₂O₂ would describe a different substance.

6. Surface area

Equal masses of powdered CaCO₃ and large chips react with acid; powder is faster because more particles are exposed for collisions.

7. Temperature

Higher temperature increases particle kinetic energy/collision frequency and the fraction of collisions energetic enough to react.

8. Concentration

More reacting particles per volume increases collision frequency, generally increasing reaction rate.

9. Catalyst

A catalyst can make a reaction reach completion sooner but does not create extra atoms or increase stoichiometric product yield by itself.

10. Cultural chemistry context

Curriculum examples include chemical processes used by First Nations Australians in producing substances or processing toxic plants into edible foods. Use a specific authoritative cultural/scientific source; explain the chemistry without publishing restricted or unsafe procedural detail.

Common misconceptions
  • Changing subscripts to balance equations.
  • Thinking atoms disappear in reactions.
  • Assuming a faster reaction makes more final product.
  • Thinking catalysts are consumed like reactants.
  • Saying hotter particles become larger rather than move with greater kinetic energy.

  2. Classify 2KClO₃→2KCl+3O₂.
  3. Classify Zn+CuSO₄→ZnSO₄+Cu.
  4. Balance H₂+Cl₂→HCl.
  5. Why not change subscripts when balancing?

  7. Explain temperature effect using collisions.
  8. Explain concentration effect.
  9. What does a catalyst change?

Solutions with explanations
  1. Synthesis.
  2. Decomposition.
  3. Displacement.
  4. H₂+Cl₂→2HCl.
  5. Subscripts define the substances; coefficients change amounts only.
  6. Greater exposed surface gives more collision opportunities.
  7. Particles move faster and more collisions exceed activation-energy requirement.
  8. More particles per volume → more collisions.
  9. Reaction pathway/activation energy and rate, not atom conservation or theoretical yield.
  10. Use authoritative respectful source/context and safe, appropriate detail.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S10U07: identify patterns in synthesis, decomposition and displacement reactions and investigate factors affecting reaction rates.

  • E1: define/represent reaction types with models, diagrams, word and balanced symbolic equations. Examples 1–5.
  • E2: identify reaction type and predict products. Examples 1–4.
  • E3: investigate synthesis/decomposition/displacement including metals/oxygen, water/NaCl, extraction, metal-acid, neutralisation/precipitation contexts. Examples 1–4.
  • E4: investigate temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts. Examples 6–9.
  • E5: investigate First Nations Australian chemical processes in production of substances such as acids/ethanol, using authoritative context.
  • E6: investigate chemical processes used to convert toxic plants to edible foods, using safe/culturally appropriate authoritative sources. Example 10.
  • E7: examine reactions used to produce useful products. Examples 1–4 can be extended to industrial contexts.
