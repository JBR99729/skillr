Earth’s Energy Balance and Global Climate Change

Climate emerges from energy transfer among atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere. Changes to incoming, reflected, absorbed and outgoing energy alter long-term climate patterns.

What you need to know

Solar radiation enters the Earth system; some is reflected and some absorbed. Earth emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb/re-emit some infrared, influencing the energy balance. Climate refers to long-term patterns; individual weather events do not prove/disprove climate trends.

Climate-system cheat sheet

Energy flow

Incoming solar → reflection/absorption → transfer among spheres → outgoing infrared.

Greenhouse effect

Atmospheric gases interact with outgoing infrared; it is not a solid “blanket layer”.

Indicators

Atmospheric/ocean temperature, sea level, ice/permafrost, species distribution/biodiversity.

Model rule

Predictions depend on assumptions, initial conditions and processes represented; uncertainty must be reported.

10 important worked examples

1. Incoming solar

Sun supplies most energy driving climate; land, oceans and atmosphere absorb different fractions and then exchange energy.

2. Albedo

Bright ice reflects more sunlight than dark ocean. Loss of ice can reduce reflection and increase absorption, a feedback.

3. Greenhouse mechanism

Increasing greenhouse-gas concentration changes absorption/re-emission of outgoing infrared and can shift the equilibrium temperature needed for outgoing energy to balance incoming energy.

4. Weather versus climate

A cold day is weather. A multi-decade temperature distribution/trend is climate evidence.

5. Multiple indicators

Rising ocean heat content, sea level and shifting species ranges provide evidence independent of a single air-temperature dataset.

6. Deep ocean currents

Density differences linked to temperature/salinity help drive deep circulation that redistributes heat and affects climate/marine systems.

7. Simulation

Change one forcing/parameter in a climate simulation and compare outputs; the model demonstrates conditional consequences, not certainty detached from assumptions.

8. Quantum-computing context

Advanced computing may improve parts of complex modelling/optimisation, but better hardware does not remove need for validated physical models and observations.

9. Mitigation versus adaptation

Reducing greenhouse-gas emissions targets causes/forcing; sea walls or heatwave plans reduce impacts. They solve different parts of the problem.

10. Evidence-based prediction

A prediction should specify model assumptions, evidence, uncertainty and time scale rather than state one deterministic future number.

Common misconceptions
  • Greenhouse effect is a physical lid.
  • Weather and climate are the same.
  • One data series is the entire climate evidence base.
  • Natural drivers mean human drivers cannot matter.
  • A model is worthless unless it predicts exactly.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Trace the main energy pathway through Earth system.
  2. How can lower ice cover alter albedo?
  3. Explain greenhouse effect without “trapping layer” language.
  4. Distinguish weather from climate.
  5. Name four climate-change indicators.
  6. What helps drive deep ocean circulation?
  7. Why change one parameter at a time in a model experiment?
  8. Why doesn't more computing power guarantee correct predictions?
  9. Distinguish mitigation and adaptation.
  10. What should accompany a climate prediction?
Solutions with explanations
  1. Solar input → reflect/absorb → transfers → infrared output.
  2. Less bright ice can reduce reflection/increase solar absorption.
  3. Greenhouse gases absorb/re-emit particular infrared wavelengths, changing energy flows.
  4. Weather is short-term conditions; climate is long-term statistical pattern.
  5. Examples: ocean/air temperature, sea level, ice/permafrost, species shifts.
  6. Temperature/salinity density differences plus winds/geometry.
  7. To isolate causal effect within model.
  8. Model structure/data/assumptions still determine validity.
  9. Mitigation reduces drivers; adaptation reduces impacts.
  10. Assumptions, evidence, uncertainty and time scale.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9S10U04: use models of energy flow between geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere to explain patterns of global climate change.

  • E1: examine solar radiation and interactions with atmosphere/ocean/land. Example 1.
  • E2: investigate climate indicators including temperature, sea level, biodiversity, species distribution, permafrost/ice. Examples 4–5.
  • E3: identify global climate changes over time and use visualisations/simulations to explore changing energy balances. Examples 7,10.
  • E4: examine energy factors driving deep-ocean currents and climate/marine effects. Example 6.
  • E5: investigate quantum-computing enhancement of complex weather/climate modelling. Example 8.
  • E6: predict Earth-system changes and identify mitigation/adaptation strategies. Examples 9–10.
