AC9S10U01: DNA, Cell Division and Mendelian Inheritance

Genes are DNA sequences on chromosomes. Mitosis preserves chromosome information for growth and repair, while meiosis and fertilisation generate variation and predictable inheritance probabilities.

LearnTeacher SlidesHomeworkPracticeTest
Learning goals: What you will learn

DNA, genes and chromosomes carry hereditary information; meiosis and fertilisation generate inheritance patterns and variation.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Genes are DNA sequences located on chromosomes.
  • Mitosis supports growth and repair; meiosis produces genetically varied gametes.
  • Mendelian models predict genotype and phenotype probabilities.
Prerequisite knowledge

Recall that cells contain genetic material and that chromosomes carry inherited information. Be comfortable with simple probability, ratios and reading a basic family tree or Punnett square.

Useful legacy quick-reference material

DNA → gene → chromosome

Genes are DNA sequences located on chromosomes.

Mitosis

1 parent cell → 2 genetically similar cells; chromosome number retained.

Meiosis

1 diploid cell → 4 genetically varied haploid gametes; chromosome number halved.

Mendelian terms

Genotype = allele combination; phenotype = observable outcome; dominant/recessive describe allele expression in a simple model.

Worked and modelled examples

1. Nest the structures

A cell nucleus contains chromosomes; chromosomes contain DNA; genes are defined DNA regions. Saying “a gene is a chromosome” collapses different levels of organisation.

2. Why mitosis matters

A skin cell divides by mitosis. DNA is copied, chromosomes are separated and two daughter cells receive the same chromosome set, supporting tissue growth/repair.

3. Why meiosis matters

Meiosis halves chromosome number so gametes can fuse at fertilisation without chromosome number doubling each generation.

4. Variation

Independent assortment and recombination during meiosis, plus random fertilisation, create new allele combinations in offspring.

5. Monohybrid cross Aa × Aa

Gametes from each parent: A or a. Punnett outcomes: AA, Aa, Aa, aa → genotype ratio 1:2:1; with A dominant, phenotype ratio 3:1.

6. Probability, not prediction of exact children

A 25% aa probability does not mean exactly one of every four children must be aa; each conception is a new probabilistic event.

7. Pedigree reasoning

If two unaffected parents have an affected child under a simple autosomal-recessive model, both parents must carry the recessive allele.

8. Mutation

UV radiation can damage DNA. A mutation in a body cell can contribute to cancer; a mutation must occur in a germ-line lineage to be inherited by offspring.

9. Genetic disorder

Cystic fibrosis can be modelled as autosomal recessive for introductory inheritance work, but real genetic conditions still involve molecular detail beyond a Punnett square.

10. Kinship and heredity context

First Nations Australian kinship/family structures are cultural knowledge systems with specific laws and responsibilities. When studying the curriculum elaboration, use authoritative community/context sources and do not reduce kinship to a Mendelian genetics analogy.

Common misconceptions and corrections
  • Gene = chromosome: a gene is a DNA region on a chromosome.
  • Mitosis creates variation: mitosis generally preserves genetic information; meiosis is the cell division central to gamete variation.
  • Dominant means common/stronger: dominance describes expression in heterozygotes, not population frequency or biological superiority.
  • Punnett square predicts exact family: it gives probabilities.
  • All mutations are inherited: inheritance depends on where the mutation occurs.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not imply one gene determines every characteristic. Do not extend to complex linkage or molecular gene regulation as the target.

Guided practice
  1. Build a chromosome–DNA–gene nesting model, then complete one monohybrid cross.
  2. Students explain where each allele in an offspring came from.

Retained teaching sequence

  1. Name the target idea: link chromosome behaviour in mitosis and meiosis to inheritance predictions.
  2. Build the model: Start with a diploid parent cell. Mitosis copies chromosomes and separates identical sister chromatids, so body cells keep the same genetic information. Meiosis separates matching chromosome pairs and then sister chromatids, so gametes receive one allele for each gene. Combine gametes in a Punnett square to predict genotype and phenotype ratios.
  3. Apply it: students transfer the same idea to unfamiliar data, a diagram, a short scenario or a practical investigation.

Teacher prompt: What evidence or relationship are we using, and what would make this explanation stronger?

Independent practice
  1. State relationship among DNA, genes and chromosomes.
  2. What is the main role of mitosis?
  3. Why must meiosis halve chromosome number?
  4. Name two sources of genetic variation associated with sexual reproduction.
  5. For Aa×Aa, give genotype ratio.
  6. For simple dominant A, give phenotype ratio.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A pedigree and a Punnett square appear to suggest different probabilities for a trait. Explain what each representation can and cannot tell you, then decide whether the evidence actually conflicts.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Assessment-style questions and review hints
  1. Why can 25% probability fail to appear in a family of four children?
  2. What can two unaffected parents with a recessive affected child imply?
  3. Distinguish somatic and heritable mutation.
  4. Why should First Nations kinship knowledge not be treated as a generic Punnett-square example?

Model answers to the retained legacy question set
  1. Genes are DNA sequences located on chromosomes.
  2. Growth/repair by producing genetically similar daughter cells.
  3. So fertilisation restores rather than doubles the species chromosome number.
  4. Meiotic recombination/assortment and random fertilisation.
  5. 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa.
  6. 3 dominant : 1 recessive.
  7. Probabilities describe repeated possibilities, not a fixed quota.
  8. Both parents are carriers under the simple autosomal-recessive model.
  9. Somatic mutations affect body-cell lineages; germ-line mutations can enter gametes and be inherited.
  10. Kinship is culturally specific knowledge/law and requires authoritative respectful context.
Exit ticket and mastery check

For Aa x Aa, students should show AA, Aa, Aa, aa and explain the 3:1 phenotype ratio when A is dominant.

Evidence of mastery: the response names the relevant science idea, applies it to the situation, uses precise vocabulary and avoids overclaiming beyond the evidence.

Exit ticket: Explain the core idea in 3–5 sentences and apply it to one new example without copying the worked model.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Keep chromosome behaviour, allele inheritance and probability connected. Require students to say where each allele came from rather than treating Punnett squares as a grid trick.

Differentiation retained from the legacy lesson

  • Support: provide a labelled diagram, sentence starter or partially completed table so students can identify the key relationship first.
  • Core: students explain the relationship independently using correct science vocabulary and one relevant example.
  • Extend: students evaluate a limitation, uncertainty, alternative explanation or real-world implication while keeping the claim evidence-based.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to explain the difference between DNA, a gene and a chromosome, then use one simple cross to show why probability does not guarantee an exact family outcome.
