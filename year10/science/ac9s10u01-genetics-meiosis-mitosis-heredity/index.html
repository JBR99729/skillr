WORKED EXAMPLE

A probability model does not promise a family ratio

In a fictional plant, P and p are alleles of one gene. Assume complete dominance: PP and Pp have purple flowers, and pp has white flowers. Each Pp parent produces equally likely P or p gametes. A Pp × Pp cross gives PP, Pp, Pp and pp as four equally likely combinations: genotype probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4, and phenotype probabilities 3/4 purple and 1/4 white. Four actual offspring need not include exactly three purple plants. The model excludes complications such as multiple genes affecting the characteristic.

Learn the skill

1. Locate the inherited information

Chromosomes contain long DNA molecules associated with proteins. A gene is a DNA region whose information contributes to a functional product, such as a protein or functional RNA. Alleles are versions of a gene. A characteristic can depend on many genes and environmental conditions; a gene is not a guaranteed destiny.

Two homologous chromosomes carry P and p at the same gene position. Before replication, each chromosome has one DNA molecule.
Try it · Are P and p different genes simply because their letters differ?

No. Here they are alleles of the same gene, at corresponding positions on homologous chromosomes. Homologues carry corresponding gene sets; after replication, sister chromatids are duplicated copies of one chromosome.

2. Compare the two divisions

Mitosis normally maintains chromosome number in the daughter nuclei; meiosis reduces the number of chromosome sets in gametes. DNA replicates before mitosis or meiosis I, but not between meiosis I and II. Homologues separate in meiosis I; sisters separate in meiosis II. Count chromosomes by centromeres rather than by DNA quantity.

Try it · Why does replication not immediately turn the toy diploid cell into a cell with 8 chromosomes?

Each replicated chromosome still has a joined sister pair counted as one chromosome at this stage, so there are 4 chromosomes but 8 DNA molecules. Meiosis halves the sets; fusion of two such gametes restores two sets.

3. Predict and qualify an inheritance result

Write parental genotypes, list possible gametes, combine them, and count genotype and phenotype probabilities separately. Independent assortment and crossing over can produce new combinations in meiosis, but not every product must be unique. Mendelian crosses require explicit assumptions about alleles, dominance and gamete probabilities.

A second fictional trait has R dominant to r. A parent known to be Rr is crossed with rr; each offspring independently has probability 1/2 of the dominant phenotype.
Try it · What is the probability that all three offspring show the dominant phenotype?

(1/2)³ = 1/8, conditional on the parent being Rr and the stated assumptions. This is not the probability that an unknown parent is Rr after observing three dominant offspring; that would require further information.

Common mistakes

“Dominant means common or stronger.” Dominance describes the phenotype of a heterozygote in this model, not population frequency or value.

“Replication doubles chromosome sets.” Distinguish duplicated DNA from the number of chromosome sets.

“A 3:1 prediction guarantees every group of four.” The ratio describes expected proportions over many independent offspring.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Compare two crosses

Draw the Pp × Pp grid, then a Pp × pp grid. Label gametes and compare the white-flower probabilities. Explain how a sample of four purple offspring remains compatible with the first cross.

Adjust the challenge Support: use one parent along each grid edge. Core: distinguish genotype from phenotype. Extend: calculate the probability of four purple offspring in the first cross.

Use the fictional plant and paper models; do not infer personal ancestry, health or family relationships.

Three tricky questions

1. What is the white-flower probability in Pp × pp?

1/2: two of the four equally likely grid combinations are pp.

2. Can mitosis occur in a haploid organism?

Yes. Mitosis maintains the starting chromosome number; it is not defined as always producing diploid cells.

3. What is the extension probability?

(3/4)⁴ = 81/256, about 31.6%, given independent offspring and the stated model.

FAQ

Why is meiosis important in sexual reproduction?

It reduces chromosome sets before gamete fusion and can reshuffle inherited combinations.

Does every characteristic follow one dominant/recessive gene?

No. This is a deliberately simple model; many characteristics involve multiple genes, environments or other inheritance patterns.

Learning goals

DNA, genes and chromosomes carry hereditary information; meiosis and fertilisation generate inheritance patterns and variation.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Genes are DNA sequences located on chromosomes.
  • Mitosis supports growth and repair; meiosis produces genetically varied gametes.
  • Mendelian models predict genotype and phenotype probabilities.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. Two heterozygous parents are represented as Aa × Aa.
  2. A Punnett model predicts AA, Aa, Aa and aa genotypes.
  3. The expected phenotype ratio is 3 dominant : 1 recessive across many offspring, not a guaranteed order in four births.
Common misconceptions
  • A gene and a chromosome are the same object: A gene is a segment of DNA at a particular location on a chromosome.
  • A Punnett square predicts exact family outcomes: Describe probabilities and expected ratios over many independent events.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not imply one gene determines every characteristic. Do not extend to complex linkage or molecular gene regulation as the target.

Guided practice
  1. Build a chromosome–DNA–gene nesting model, then complete one monohybrid cross.
  2. Students explain where each allele in an offspring came from.

Work through the sequence Nest the genetic structures → Compare cell divisions → Track alleles → Predict offspring, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Use this fictional one-gene cross: Pp × Pp, with complete dominance of P and equally likely P/p gametes. Draw four equally likely combinations.

Check the supplied cross

PP, Pp, Pp, pp: genotype probabilities 1/4, 1/2, 1/4; dominant phenotype 3/4. Each parent contributes one allele.

Independent practice
  1. Distinguish DNA, a gene and a chromosome, and explain how the three ideas are related.
  2. Compare mitosis and meiosis in terms of purpose, number of divisions and genetic similarity of the resulting cells.
  3. For a cross Aa × Aa, determine the expected genotype ratio and explain why this is a probability rather than a guaranteed family outcome.
  4. Explain how meiosis contributes to genetic variation in sexually reproducing populations.
Reasoning and problem-solving

A pedigree and a Punnett square appear to suggest different probabilities for a trait. Explain what each representation can and cannot tell you, then decide whether the evidence actually conflicts.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Supplied fictional family: two parents are known to be Pp under complete dominance. Their four observed offspring all have the dominant phenotype. The Punnett model predicts 3/4 dominant for each independent offspring.

Does the family conflict with the model?

No. All four dominant offspring have probability (3/4)⁴ = 81/256 under the model. A pedigree records a particular family and may not reveal every genotype; the Punnett square predicts probabilities under specified parental genotypes and assumptions.

What is the central idea?

What should a strong response do?

Practice and review
  1. A student says, “A child receives half of each gene from each parent.” Correct the statement using chromosomes, alleles and meiosis.
  2. Use a Punnett square and a short explanation to predict the possible offspring from two heterozygous parents, then state one limitation of the prediction.
  3. Explain how mitosis maintains genetic information for growth while meiosis and fertilisation contribute to inheritance and variation.
Check a worked answer

For Aa × Aa, what are the expected genotype probabilities?

Model answer: 25% AA, 50% Aa and 25% aa. These are probabilities for each offspring.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Keep chromosome behaviour, allele inheritance and probability connected. Require students to say where each allele came from rather than treating Punnett squares as a grid trick.

For parents and carers

Ask your child to explain the difference between DNA, a gene and a chromosome, then use one simple cross to show why probability does not guarantee an exact family outcome.
