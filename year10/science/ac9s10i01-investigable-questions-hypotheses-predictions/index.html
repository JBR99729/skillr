YEAR 10 SCIENCE

Connect a testable question to an explanatory model AC9S10I01

Learning intention: Develop feasible investigable questions, explanatory hypotheses and reasoned predictions, then use discriminating evidence to revise models.

WORKED EXAMPLE

One pattern can have more than one explanation

A fictional paper investigation supplies the same block, a force meter, mass labels and two dry surfaces. The outcome is the steady horizontal pulling force while the 2.0 kg block slides in a straight line at a controlled 0.10 m/s. Three repeated runs on surface A give 3.8, 4.0 and 4.2 N; B gives 5.8, 6.0 and 6.2 N. The means are 4.0 and 6.0 N. A surface-interaction hypothesis can explain the difference, but A was tested before B, so a changing meter offset is an alternative to investigate. These are constructed records, not measurements we performed.

SurfaceThree run readings (N)Mean (N)Design note
A3.8, 4.0, 4.24.0Tested first
B5.8, 6.0, 6.26.0Tested later

Use the supplied records and labelled models for exact values; do not measure contextual artwork.

Learn the skill

1. Make the question measurable and feasible

A useful question names the relationship, defines the measured outcome and fits the available materials, time and permissions. Here ask how the dry surface affects mean steady sliding force for the same block and conditions. Steady sliding force is different from the initial peak needed to start motion. At constant velocity, the pull balances resistance; the meter reading is not an unbalanced resultant force. An observational relationship question can also be investigable without manipulating its variables.

Available: block, force meter, two dry surfaces and mass labels. Proposed questions concern dry-surface force, wet-surface force and acceleration down a ramp.
Try it · Which proposal fits these supplied materials?

The dry-surface comparison fits. The others need additional materials or apparatus not supplied. Define the constant mass, motion, meter procedure and repeats rather than asking vaguely which surface is “better”.

2. Distinguish explanation from prediction

A hypothesis offers a tentative scientific explanation. A prediction states observable evidence expected if that explanation and its assumptions apply. The hypothesis that the surface interactions create greater sliding resistance on B predicts a larger mean steady force there under controlled conditions. Neither the hypothesis nor the prediction is a guaranteed outcome.

Proposed statement: “B will give a larger force reading.” No explanation is given.
Try it · What is missing, and how could you add it?

It is a prediction without an explanatory reason. Add a tentative explanation about the interacting materials producing different sliding resistance, then state the controlled conditions. Do not assume a simple roughness ranking applies to every pair of materials.

3. Choose evidence that distinguishes alternatives

A useful next test asks where competing explanations predict different observations. Interleave A and B runs, record order and check the meter against a suitable reference before and after. A stable reference with a repeated surface difference would weaken a simple drift-only explanation; a changing reference would require measurement correction and further investigation. More than one influence may operate.

Two proposed follow-ups: repeat all A then all B again; or interleave the surfaces and include reference checks.
Try it · Which better separates a surface effect from changing offset?

The interleaved, reference-checked plan better separates order/time from surface. Merely repeating the original order can repeat its confounding. Use the results to refine the model and its limits, not to declare an explanation proved forever.

Common mistakes

“A hypothesis is just any guess.” It should be a testable explanation grounded in relevant knowledge.

“A prediction supplies its own reason.” State the explanatory link and the assumptions.

“One successful prediction identifies the only cause.” Consider alternatives that could predict the same observation.

10-MINUTE ACTIVITY

Write a question-to-evidence chain

Write the dry-surface question, define the measured force, propose an explanatory hypothesis and state a conditional prediction. Add one reference-check outcome that would make you reconsider your explanation.

Adjust the challenge Support: separate question, explanation and expected observation. Core: name controls and a competing explanation. Extend: explain how both drift and a real surface effect could contribute.

Treat this as a paper investigation. Any actual force experiment requires teacher-approved equipment, controlled motion and appropriate collision or falling-object precautions.

Three tricky questions

1. Can an observational study test a relationship?

Yes. It can investigate associations when manipulation is impractical or inappropriate, but causal claims need additional justification.

2. Does a failed prediction automatically destroy every part of a model?

No. Check the method, assumptions and alternatives, then revise the explanation or its scope as the evidence warrants.

3. Why retain the order of trials?

Order can reveal changing conditions or drift that would be hidden by recording only group means.

FAQ

What makes a test discriminating?

Competing explanations make different expected observations under its stated conditions.

What does an explanatory model add?

It represents proposed components and relationships that account for observations and generate further testable predictions.

Questions, predictions and hypotheses connect measurable relationships to an explanatory model.

  • Hypotheses include scientific reasoning.

Worked examples

Worked reasoning model

  1. A trolley’s acceleration changes when added mass changes.
  2. Question: How does total mass affect acceleration under constant net force?
  3. Hypothesis: Acceleration decreases as mass increases because a = F/m.
Common misconceptions
  • A prediction and hypothesis are identical: Use prediction for expected outcome and hypothesis for the reasoned explanatory relationship.
  • A testable question must always be causal: Distinguish descriptive, relational and causal questions.

Keep the Year 10 boundary: Do not state causation from observational correlation alone. Do not force every inquiry into an ‘if–then–because’ sentence when another precise form is clearer.

Guided practice
  1. From the supplied motion record, write one question, one hypothesis and one quantitative prediction.
  2. Label which part comes from the model.

Work through the sequence Observe carefully → Define variables → Propose a mechanism → Predict a pattern, explaining the evidence or mechanism at each step before moving on.

Supplied fictional motion record: t = 0, 1, 2 s; velocity = 0, 1, 2 m/s in the same direction. The chosen cart has mass 2 kg and constant resultant force 2 N; track conditions are fixed.

Check the supplied case

Example question: how does total cart mass affect acceleration at fixed resultant force? The record gives acceleration 1 m/s². Hypothesis: increasing mass reduces acceleration because a = Fnet/m. Prediction: at 4 kg with the same 2 N resultant, acceleration is 0.5 m/s². The equation supplies the prediction; this record alone does not test every mass.

Independent practice
  1. Rewrite “Does light affect plants?” as a measurable investigable question that identifies an independent and dependent variable.
  2. Write a directional hypothesis for an investigation of temperature and reaction rate.
  3. In the trolley example above, identify the independent and dependent variables and two conditions to keep constant.
  4. Explain the difference between a prediction and a scientific reason that supports a hypothesis.
Check your answers
  1. Example: How does daily light exposure affect mean seedling height after two weeks?
  2. A higher starting temperature is expected to increase the initial reaction rate because a greater fraction of collisions have sufficient energy.
  3. In the trolley example, vary total mass, measure acceleration, and keep net force and track conditions constant.
  4. A prediction states the expected outcome. The scientific reason explains why the model predicts it.
Reasoning and problem-solving

Rewrite a vague question such as “Does temperature affect reactions?” into a testable question and hypothesis that name measurable variables and a directional prediction.

Reasoning standard: state the claim, use relevant evidence or a scientific mechanism, and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion without overclaiming.

Practice and review
  1. A student asks, “Which fertiliser is best?” Rewrite this as a testable Year 10 investigation question and hypothesis, defining what “best” will mean operationally.
  2. Evaluate the hypothesis “Plants grow better with music.” Identify why it is weak and rewrite it so the relationship can be tested.
  3. Develop an investigable question and reasoned prediction for a relationship between one physical variable and another, then justify your choice of variables.

Review hint: Use precise scientific vocabulary, show the relevant mechanism or evidence, and state any limitation or uncertainty when the evidence does not justify a stronger claim.

Teacher and parent guidance

For teachers

Make hypotheses relational and testable. A good hypothesis predicts how a measured dependent variable changes when the independent variable changes and gives a scientific reason.

For parents and carers

Take an everyday “I wonder…” question and ask your child what would need to be changed, measured and controlled to test it fairly.
