Technical and Academic Vocabulary

Precise academic vocabulary names concepts and relationships accurately; sophisticated writing uses the right term, not merely the longest or most unusual word.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA08: use an expanded technical and academic vocabulary for precision when writing academic texts.

Vocabulary toolkit

Technical vocabulary

Discipline-specific terms such as “iambic pentameter”, “modality” or “diegetic sound”.

Academic vocabulary

Cross-disciplinary words such as “evaluate”, “infer”, “contradict”, “significant”.

Precision

Choose the term that narrows meaning accurately.

Control

Define specialist terms for audiences who may not know them; avoid jargon used only for display.

10 important examples

1. Poetry

“Rhythm” is broad; “iambic pentameter” precisely names a recurring five-foot iambic line.

2. Argument

“Shows” may be replaced by “implies”, “demonstrates” or “suggests” depending on strength of evidence.

3. Visual analysis

“Camera thing” becomes “low-angle medium shot”.

4. Evaluation

“Bad” becomes “ineffective because the evidence is unrepresentative”.

5. Comparison

“Different” can become “contrasts”, “diverges from” or “qualifies” depending on relationship.

6. Caution

“Proves” may be too strong; “supports” better matches limited evidence.

7. Literary device

Naming “anaphora” is useful only if analysis explains the repeated openings and their effect.

8. Register

Technical language may suit an essay but require explanation in a public information text.

9. Word family

analyse → analysis → analytical; correct form supports grammatical precision.

10. Revision

Replace “a lot of things happen” with the actual process or features being analysed.

Common mistakes
  • Using a thesaurus synonym without checking connotation or grammatical fit.
  • Treating jargon density as sophistication.
  • Using a technical label with no explanation of effect.
  • Overstating certainty with “proves” when evidence only “suggests”.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Give a more precise alternative to “shows” when evidence is tentative.
  2. What does “iambic pentameter” name?
  3. Why is “utilise” not automatically better than “use”?
  4. Replace “the camera makes him look strong” with technical wording.
  5. Choose between “proves” and “supports” for a limited data set.
  6. What is the difference between technical and academic vocabulary?
  7. Why should specialist terms sometimes be defined?
  8. Use “qualifies” accurately in a sentence.
  9. Turn “the author uses repetition” into a precise analytical statement.
  10. Identify one sign vocabulary is becoming pretentious rather than precise.
Indicative responses
  1. “Suggests” or “indicates”.
  2. A metrical pattern of five iambic feet in a poetic line.
  3. Precision depends on meaning; “utilise” may add no useful distinction.
  4. “The low-angle medium shot positions the character as visually dominant.”
  5. “Supports” is safer because limited evidence does not remove alternatives.
  6. Technical vocabulary belongs to a field; academic vocabulary supports reasoning across subjects.
  7. To maintain accessibility and prevent jargon from excluding readers.
  8. “The second interpretation qualifies the first by accepting the symbolism but rejecting its wholly negative reading.”
  9. “The anaphora of ‘we remember’ creates a collective, cumulative rhythm.”
  10. Long or obscure words replace clearer terms without adding meaning.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA08: use an expanded technical and academic vocabulary for precision when writing academic texts.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying the meaning of an increasing range of technical vocabulary; for example, using specific terms about rhythm such as “iambic pentameter” when analysing poetry

In practice: Replace vague analytical wording with one precise discipline-specific term, then explain the term’s evidence and effect in the sentence.
