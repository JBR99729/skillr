Syntax, Nominalisation and Complex Ideas

Syntax shapes relationships between ideas: clause combinations show logic, nominalisation compresses processes into concepts, and noun groups can make writing more abstract.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA06: analyse how meaning and style are achieved through syntax.

Analysis and writing toolkit

Nominalisation

Turn a process into a noun: “decide” → “decision”; “pollute” → “pollution”.

Logical clauses

Cause, result, concession, condition and manner can be built through dependent clauses.

Abstraction

Expanded noun groups package complex ideas for academic discussion.

Analysis move

Identify syntax → unpack logical/abstract meaning → judge effect on style and clarity.

10 important examples

1. Nominalisation

“The company expanded rapidly” → “The company’s rapid expansion…” makes the process a noun-group topic.

2. Agency shift

“Authorities removed the trees” → “The removal of the trees…” can background who acted.

3. Cause

“Because rainfall declined, reservoir levels fell.”

4. Concession

“Although the sample was small, the pattern was consistent.”

5. Condition

“If emissions continue to rise, the model predicts further warming.”

6. Result

“The road flooded, so access was restricted.”

7. Abstraction

“The political, social and economic consequences of the decision” packages several dimensions into one noun group.

8. Dense style

Nominalisation can increase information density but too much can hide actors and reduce readability.

9. Pace

Short main clauses can make argument forceful; layered subordination can show nuance.

10. Revision

One controlled complex sentence can make the relationship between two vague simple sentences explicit.

Common mistakes
  • Using nominalisation simply to sound formal.
  • Creating long noun groups whose head noun becomes unclear.
  • Using “although” for cause or “because” for contrast.
  • Assuming complex sentences are automatically sophisticated.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Nominalise “the population declined rapidly”.
  2. What logical relation does “although” signal?
  3. Write a conditional sentence about a study result.
  4. How can nominalisation hide agency?
  5. Why might abstraction help academic writing?
  6. Identify the relation in “Because costs increased, demand fell”.
  7. Rewrite two simple sentences to show concession.
  8. What is one risk of overusing nominalisation?
  9. Explain how syntax can affect pace.
  10. Evaluate: “The implementation of the reduction of expenditure was undertaken.”
Indicative responses
  1. “The rapid decline in the population…”
  2. Concession.
  3. “If the second trial reproduces the pattern, confidence in the explanation will increase.”
  4. “The destruction of the habitat occurred” removes the actor named in “Developers destroyed the habitat”.
  5. It lets a writer treat a complex process or qualities as one concept for comparison or evaluation.
  6. Cause.
  7. “Although the sample was small, the result was consistent across all three sites.”
  8. It can make prose dense and obscure agency.
  9. Layered clauses slow reading and show relationships; short main clauses can accelerate or emphasise.
  10. It is over-nominalised and vague. Clearer: “The organisation cut spending.”
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA06: analyse how meaning and style are achieved through syntax.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: considering how nominalisation affects the way in which events are constructed and explained, making some information more explicit than other information

In practice: Transform a clause into a nominalised noun group, then check what happens to agency, abstraction and information density.

E2

Australian Curriculum elaboration: analysing how logical relations between ideas are built up by combining independent with dependent clauses indicating cause, result, manner, concession, condition, and so on; for example, “Although the poet was not generally well received by critics during her life (concession), her reputation grew substantially after her death.”

In practice: Combine two propositions using cause, concession and condition in turn; compare how the logical relationship changes.

E3

Australian Curriculum elaboration: considering how abstraction in a noun group allows for greater generalisation of complex ideas in a sentence; for example, “He focused on the political, religious, social and economic elements of the society in his thesis.”

In practice: Expand a noun group to package several dimensions of an idea, then edit it back if the head noun becomes hard to locate.
