AC9E10LE01 • Year 10 English

AC9E10LE01: analyse representations of individuals, groups and places and evaluate how they reflect their context in literary texts by First Nations Australian, and wide-ranging Australian and world authors

Analyse how literary texts represent people and places, using textual evidence and a clearly identified context.

Learning goals

We are learning to analyse how literary texts represent people, groups and places, then evaluate how those representations relate to a specific context.

  • Locate a representation in supplied words.
  • Separate the author, narrator or speaker, and represented people.
  • Connect an interpretation to supplied contextual evidence.
  • Compare different texts without treating a culture or period as having one voice.
Before this lesson

A representation is a selected construction, not the whole reality. A narrator or poetic speaker is not automatically the author. Context can include the circumstances of production, the setting of a story and the circumstances in which it is read; specify which you mean.

Key concept and three teaching focuses

Three teaching focuses

  1. Representation: examine the selected actions, labels, imagery and viewpoint that construct an individual, group or place.
  2. Context: use a supported circumstance to explain how a text reflects, questions or contests an idea. A date alone is not an explanation.
  3. Comparison and retelling: evaluate what changes across versions, audiences and media. An anti-hero, a community storyteller and a comic underdog do not offer interchangeable perspectives.

The examples below include a Noonuccal poet, a Kakadu Traditional Owner’s published storytelling, an Australian bush ballad and an English novel. Analyse each in its own terms. Do not treat one author as representative of all First Nations peoples or all Australians.

Worked examples with complete evidence

Worked example 1: belonging and a contested place

Oodgeroo Noonuccal, We Are Going (1964). The NFSA presents a transcript of the poem. It depicts people returning to a ceremonial place that has been altered by development. Brief quotation for analysis:

“We belong here, we are of the old ways.”

Read the full poem and its attribution at Australian Screen / NFSA. This page analyses the written text; no claim about the sound of the recording is required. The linked page contains the name and recording of a deceased First Nations person.

  1. Identify the claim: the collective speaker asserts belonging rather than accepting an outsider’s account of the place.
  2. Explain the representation: the place carries continuing cultural meaning, rather than being merely land available for development.
  3. Add supported context: NFSA dates the poem’s publication to 1964 during the author’s involvement in Aboriginal rights activism. The assertion of belonging can challenge dispossession in that context.
  4. Qualify the judgement: this is a reading of this poem and its collective speaker, not a factual description of every community or a claim that a culture has disappeared.

Worked example 2: a character’s rule is not the novel’s rule

Charles Dickens, Hard Times (1854), opening chapter. In this supplied excerpt, an adult instructs a teacher in a schoolroom:

“Now, what I want is, Facts. Teach these boys and girls nothing but Facts.”

Source: Dickens, Hard Times, Book I, Chapter I.

Model answer: the repeated capitalised noun and the command “Teach” represent the adult as controlling what counts as useful knowledge. The children are the recipients of his rule. In the context of a nineteenth-century novel opening in a schoolroom, the demand gives us a way to examine educational authority. It does not prove that Dickens endorses the speaker’s position or that every Victorian school worked this way. Reading the narrator’s surrounding description provides evidence for evaluating the treatment critically.

Worked example 3: a rescue story adapted across contexts

These paired SkillrHub microfictions are invented teaching texts, not historical records. They retain an unauthorised warning and a rescue, but change the technology and the presentation of reward.

A. A rescue-story version: an invented small town before mobile phones.

Mara rang the council bell without permission. The flood warning reached the river cottages. She stayed to carry supplies, refusing a reward.

B. A contemporary adaptation: an invented town where public emergency messages and saleable news footage are available.

Original SkillrHub microfiction. Context: an invented present-day town uses public emergency messaging during a flood.

Mara stole the council’s access code and sent the warning before approval arrived. She saved the caravan park, then sold the footage as an exclusive story. “Public service,” she called it, checking the payment.

Comparative model: A gives Mara an unauthorised action but foregrounds continued service and refusal of payment, encouraging a largely heroic reading. B retains the rescue pattern but makes the final reward commercial and self-interested. The access code and exclusive footage create a contemporary dilemma about public service and private profit. These contrasts belong to the stated fictional contexts; they do not prove that earlier people were selfless or that all contemporary rescuers seek profit.

Model answer for B: the rescue is admirable, but the theft and commercial self-interest complicate an uncomplicated heroic image. The final payment detail encourages a reading of Mara as an anti-hero: a central character with morally troubling qualities, not simply a villain. Emergency messaging and saleable footage locate the dilemma in this invented contemporary setting. Another reader might defend the breach of procedure while criticising the profiteering; both judgements need the supplied evidence.

Questions and answers
  1. How does context explain a representation? Connect a specific circumstance with a specific textual choice, rather than adding an unrelated historical fact.
  2. Does a text merely repeat its context? No. It can celebrate, question or contest the expectations surrounding it.
  3. Are First Nations narratives interchangeable? No. Attribute the particular storyteller, community, place and publication. Respect the boundaries of the publicly shared version.
  4. Can readers disagree? Yes, but a defensible judgement needs textual and contextual support; a personal reaction alone is insufficient.
Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • The narrator speaks for the author. Identify the voice and compare it with the text’s treatment.
  • Context means guessing an author’s private intention. Use attributed evidence and qualify uncertainty.
  • A contemporary retelling makes a cultural narrative obsolete. A changed audience or example can communicate continuing responsibilities. Do not infer that a shared narrative authorises outsiders to rewrite it.
  • A comic underdog represents every Australian. Analyse the text’s selected bush/city relationship and historical genre, not a universal national character.
Guided practice

A contemporary bridge in First Nations storytelling

In the ABC Education transcript of Garranga’rreli and the baby, Kakadu Traditional Owner Marcus Dempsey connects the narrative with an everyday comparison:

“Like today when we go to the shop and the little ones are crying for a candy”

Read Marcus Dempsey’s publicly shared account at ABC Education. Made with Parks Australia and Traditional Owners and staff at Kakadu National Park. This task considers the written analogy, not an unviewed performance or a medical claim.

Try: explain what locates the analogy in contemporary experience and how it could support a new audience without erasing the particular cultural context. Evaluate two possible contemporary audience responses; distinguish your predictions from observed reception.

Answer and explanation

The shop and candy connect the situation to a recognisable present-day interaction between children and adults. That bridge can help an unfamiliar reader understand a moment in Dempsey’s account. It does not turn the narrative into a generic story from all Aboriginal peoples or give the reader permission to invent a new version. One predicted response from an unfamiliar contemporary reader is recognition: the shop comparison can make the child–adult interaction easier to follow. Another reader might find that everyday comparison insufficient to understand Garranga’rreli’s particular cultural significance and seek the complete attributed account. The first helps initial access; the second guards against reducing the narrative to an everyday shopping dispute. These are reasoned possible responses, not reports of what audiences actually said.

Independent practice

Humour and the Australian underdog

In A. B. Paterson’s The Man from Ironbark, a city barber and his amused onlookers put a bush visitor at a disadvantage. The barber frightens him with a cruel trick. The visitor retaliates and later retells his supposed escape. The poem ends:

“And whether he’s believed or no, there’s one thing to remark,
That flowing beards are all the go way up in Ironbark.”

Read the poem in Paterson’s collection. The ballad’s exaggerated conflict uses a bush-visitor/city-barber contrast. The context supplied here is a late-nineteenth-century Australian comic ballad, not a report about every city or country person.

Task: explain how the ending makes the visitor both an underdog and a source of humour. Evaluate two possible audience responses.

Answer and explanation

The barber initially has local knowledge and a group of onlookers, giving the visitor a disadvantage. The final fashion for beards exaggerates the consequences of his frightening account, and uncertainty about whether he is believed makes the retelling comic. A reader might enjoy his refusal to remain an easy victim, or see that he is also being mocked for misunderstanding the trick. The bush/city contrast helps explain those competing responses, but neither response establishes what all Australians value.

Reasoning and interpretation

Compare these bounded excerpts and supplied contexts. Oodgeroo Noonuccal’s We Are Going (1964) depicts a collective claim to belonging at an altered ceremonial place; NFSA situates its publication during her Aboriginal-rights activism.

“We belong here, we are of the old ways.”

In Dickens’s Hard Times (1854), an adult gives a teacher this command in the opening schoolroom scene:

“Now, what I want is, Facts. Teach these boys and girls nothing but Facts.”

Explain why “both writers favour the powerful group” is not established by these words. The quotations and source links are also attributed in the worked examples; the first is a brief extract, not the full poem.

Answer and explanation

The claim collapses speaker and author and ignores the different contexts. Oodgeroo’s collective assertion can contest exclusion, while Dickens’s controlling adult is a character whose position must be compared with the narration. A useful comparison names whose authority is represented, how it is constructed and whether the text affirms or challenges it. Do not infer the whole work’s position from one character’s command alone.

Assessment-style practice

Original SkillrHub microfiction. Context: an invented regional town is celebrating the opening of a new library.

The mayor announced that he had given the town a voice. At the back, Nessa folded the petition she had carried from door to door for three years. When the applause stopped, she slid it under the microphone.

Task: analyse how the mayor and Nessa are represented. Explain how the supplied public-celebration context shapes the effect of the final action. Evaluate a different defensible reading.

Answer and explanation

The mayor’s claim makes him the giver of the town’s voice, while the petition and three years of work establish Nessa’s less publicly recognised contribution. Placing the petition at the microphone challenges whose work is heard during the celebration. A reader could see quiet resistance or a demand for shared credit rather than assume Nessa’s exact emotion. A strong response connects the contrast and final action to the supplied context; it does not invent the mayor’s biography or claim an entire novel has been read.

Exit ticket

Original closing line in an invented town-history exhibition: “The official photograph named the donor; the diary named the workers.”

Exit ticket: analyse the contrast in representation, relate it to the exhibition context, and state what further evidence is needed.

Answer and explanation

The contrast foregrounds different ways of distributing credit: the public photograph privileges the donor while the diary records workers. In a history exhibition it could encourage readers to question whose contributions enter an official account. The line alone cannot establish what the photograph or diary actually contains, why their authors made those choices, or whether either record is complete.

Support, core and extend
  • Support: mark who speaks, who acts and which detail represents them. Supply the stated context beside the excerpt.
  • Core: explain a representation and evaluate its relationship to the supplied context.
  • Extend: compare two defensible readings or how a retelling addresses a different audience without merging distinct cultural contexts.

For broader study, read complete authorised works by a range of First Nations Australian, other Australian and world authors. Brief extracts support bounded analysis, not claims about unread books. Do not ask learners to imitate a First Nations voice or invent restricted cultural stories.
