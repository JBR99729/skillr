Evaluative Language Reveals Values

Evaluation is never neutral: praise, criticism, modality and loaded description reveal what a speaker or writer treats as desirable, normal, risky or unacceptable.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA02: understand that language used to evaluate, implicitly or explicitly reveals an individual's values.

Evaluation can be direct or implied through labels, selection, comparison, metaphor, irony and modality.

Analysis and writing toolkit

Explicit evaluation

Direct judgement: “effective”, “irresponsible”, “beautiful”, “unfair”.

Implicit evaluation

Selection, comparison, metaphor, irony or detail can imply judgement without stating it.

Values

Ask: what does this language suggest the evaluator prizes, fears or rejects?

Analysis move

Quote the evaluative choice → identify judgement → infer value → test against context.

10 important examples

1. Explicit praise

“A courageous decision” evaluates the act positively and reveals that decisiveness or risk-taking is valued.

2. Explicit criticism

“A reckless proposal” evaluates negatively and suggests safety or caution is prioritised.

3. Implicit judgement

“She arrived only ten minutes late—again.” “Only” and “again” can imply irritation or scepticism.

4. Metaphor

Calling a policy “a lifeline” evaluates it as necessary and beneficial.

5. Selection

A profile listing only a leader’s failures creates negative evaluation through selection even without adjectives.

6. Modality

“We must act” presents action as obligatory and reveals a strong value commitment.

7. Irony

“Another brilliant delay” uses surface praise to communicate criticism.

8. Image + caption

A low-angle image labelled “the reformer” can reinforce admiration through multimodal evaluation.

9. Comparison

“Unlike the wasteful old system…” values efficiency by constructing a negative contrast.

10. Re-evaluation

“What seemed stubborn now appears principled” shows how new evidence changes the evaluator’s position.

Common mistakes
  • Listing positive or negative words without identifying the underlying value.
  • Assuming all evaluation is explicit adjective use.
  • Treating the inferred value as fact rather than a reasoned interpretation.
  • Ignoring multimodal evaluation in images, editing, sound or layout.
10 important questions to solve
  1. What value is implied by describing a decision as “responsible”?
  2. Explain the evaluation in “another convenient excuse”.
  3. How can omission create implicit evaluation?
  4. Analyse the metaphor “the proposal is a bridge to opportunity”.
  5. What does “we cannot tolerate this” reveal about the speaker’s stance?
  6. Explain how an image can evaluate without words.
  7. Compare “persistent” and “stubborn” as evaluations of the same behaviour.
  8. Why should inferred values be supported with textual evidence?
  9. Rewrite “a disastrous experiment” to sound more neutral.
  10. Explain how a writer can revise an evaluation after new evidence.
Indicative responses with explanations
  1. 1. It suggests the evaluator values duty, caution or accountability.
  2. 2. “Convenient” and “another” imply the explanation is self-serving and repetitive, revealing distrust.
  3. 3. Selecting only failures or excluding mitigating context can guide the audience toward a negative judgement without direct labels.
  4. 4. The metaphor frames the proposal as enabling movement toward a desirable future, valuing access and progress.
  5. 5. The high-modality “cannot tolerate” marks the behaviour as beyond acceptable limits.
  6. 6. Camera angle, lighting, cropping and facial expression can position a subject as powerful, vulnerable, threatening or trustworthy.
  7. 7. “Persistent” praises determination; “stubborn” criticises inflexibility.
  8. 8. Values are interpretations; evidence prevents unsupported mind-reading.
  9. 9. Possible neutral rewrite: “an experiment that produced several adverse results.”
  10. 10. A writer can acknowledge earlier judgement, introduce new evidence and use changed evaluative vocabulary to signal a revised stance.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA02: understand that language used to evaluate, implicitly or explicitly reveals an individual's values.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying explicit expressions of values when evaluating

In practice: Underline direct praise or criticism, then name the value behind the judgement rather than stopping at positive/negative tone.

E2

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying subtle or implied values communicated through language; for example, using a term such as “teenager” to refer to an individual rather than using a specific name

In practice: Compare a neutral label with the chosen label and explain what extra judgement or stereotype the choice introduces.

International curriculum alignment

Closest alignments only: Victorian Year 10 English, NSW Stage 5 English, Common Core ELA Grades 9–10 language/reading, UK GCSE English, provincial Canadian Grade 10 English, NZ secondary English and CBSE Class 10 English.
