Sentence Structures for Meaning and Effect

Sentence structure controls focus, pace and emphasis: writers can shift agents, omit verbs, vary clause patterns and break conventions for deliberate effect.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA05: analyse and evaluate the effectiveness of particular sentence structures to express and craft ideas.

Analysis and writing toolkit

Active / passive

Active foregrounds the actor; passive can foreground the affected participant or omit the actor.

Clause type

Independent, dependent and verbless clauses alter completeness, rhythm and focus.

Sentence opening

Fronting a phrase or conjunction changes emphasis and flow.

Evaluation move

Name the structure → explain what it foregrounds → connect to tone/purpose.

10 important examples

1. Active

“The committee rejected the proposal.” The actor is explicit and responsibility is foregrounded.

2. Passive

“The proposal was rejected.” Attention shifts to the proposal; the actor can be backgrounded or omitted.

3. Actor omission

“Errors were discovered.” The passive form can avoid naming who caused them.

4. Verbless fragment

“No lights. No voices. Nothing.” Verbless units slow the scene and intensify absence.

5. Question fragment

“And the cost?” isolates the concern and mimics abrupt spoken challenge.

6. Conjunction opening

“But the evidence changed.” Beginning with “But” creates a direct pivot.

7. Periodic sentence

Holding the main clause until the end can build suspense or qualification.

8. Cumulative sentence

A clear main clause followed by added detail can create flowing elaboration.

9. Parallel syntax

“We asked, we waited, we returned.” Repeated structure creates rhythm and persistence.

10. Context

Passive voice is not inherently weak; in a report it can appropriately foreground process rather than researcher.

Common mistakes
  • Calling every passive sentence vague or incorrect.
  • Assuming fragments are errors even when deliberately crafted.
  • Naming sentence types without explaining focus or effect.
  • Treating an unusual sentence opening as effective merely because it is unusual.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Compare “The storm destroyed the bridge” with “The bridge was destroyed by the storm”.
  2. Why might a report use “Samples were heated to 80°C”?
  3. What effect is created by “No warning. No time.”?
  4. How can passive voice conceal responsibility?
  5. Write a sentence beginning with “And” for deliberate continuation.
  6. Explain parallel syntax in “We listened, we learned, we changed”.
  7. When is a sentence fragment inappropriate?
  8. Rewrite “The council closed the pool” to foreground the pool.
  9. Why is passive voice not automatically weaker?
  10. Evaluate a short sentence after a long, complex one.
Indicative responses
  1. The active foregrounds the storm; passive foregrounds the damaged bridge.
  2. Scientific procedure often foregrounds process/material rather than the predictable actor.
  3. The verbless fragments create abrupt rhythm and urgency.
  4. Omitting the agent can remove explicit responsibility.
  5. Example: “And still the river kept rising.”
  6. The repeated grammatical pattern creates rhythm and presents a deliberate sequence.
  7. When accidental fragmentation makes the relationship between ideas unclear.
  8. “The pool was closed by the council.”
  9. It can be precise when process or affected participant matters more than actor.
  10. The contrast can deliver emphasis or finality.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA05: analyse and evaluate the effectiveness of particular sentence structures to express and craft ideas.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: recognising how the focus of a sentence can be changed through using the passive voice; for example, compare active “The police had caught the thief.” with passive “The thief had been caught.”

In practice: Rewrite one active sentence in the passive and compare what becomes foregrounded, backgrounded or omitted.

E2

Australian Curriculum elaboration: recognising how authors sometimes use verbless clauses for effect; for example, “And what about the other woman? With her dark glasses and briefcase.”

In practice: Turn a complete descriptive sentence into a controlled verbless fragment and explain how rhythm and focus change.

E3

Australian Curriculum elaboration: recognising that a sentence can begin with a coordinating conjunction for stylistic effect; for example, “And she went on planning how she would manage it.”

In practice: Compare a conventional opening with one beginning “And” or “But”, then decide whether the continuation or pivot is justified.
