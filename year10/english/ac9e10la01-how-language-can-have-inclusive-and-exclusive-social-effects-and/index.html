AC9E10LA01 • Year 10 English

AC9E10LA01: understand how language can have inclusive and exclusive social effects, and can empower or disempower people

Compare how labels, pronouns and assumptions can include people, exclude them or give them a voice.

Learning goals

We are learning to explain how wording can include people, exclude them or give them more or less control in a particular situation.

  • Identify the exact label, pronoun or assumption.
  • Explain who is invited to belong, speak or decide.
  • Compare an alternative and justify its likely social effect.
  • Support a reading with context; allow a different response when the evidence supports it.
Before this lesson

Know the difference between a speaker and an audience. A pronoun stands for a person or group; a shared assumption is something a speaker treats as already agreed. You do not need to disclose personal experiences to complete this lesson.

Key concept and three teaching focuses

Three teaching focuses

  1. Belonging: labels and pronouns draw a boundary around a group. Ask who is included in “we” and who is described as “they”.
  2. Authority: wording can allow someone to make choices or treat them as a passive recipient. Examine who speaks, acts and decides.
  3. Assumptions and alternatives: phrases such as “obviously” can make disagreement sound unreasonable. Check the audience and compare a clearer, more inclusive version.

An effect is not automatic: “we” can welcome a whole class or exclude newcomers. A polite sentence can still deny a person a choice. Explain the mechanism in the stated context instead of labelling individual words good or bad.

Worked examples with complete evidence

Worked example 1: who belongs in “we”?

A school is inviting students to a science club. These are original SkillrHub examples.

A: We serious science students know the laboratory is where we belong. New people can watch.
B: Everyone interested in science can join. New members can choose a partner and take part in the first experiment.

  1. Locate the boundary: “we serious science students” presents an established group as the proper members.
  2. Connect wording to participation: “can watch” gives newcomers a spectator role, while “take part” gives them an active role.
  3. Evaluate the alternative: B makes interest the entry condition and gives new members a choice. That is more inclusive for this open club, provided the practical arrangements also allow participation.

Model answer: A uses “we” to align established members while separating newcomers from the group. Its permission to watch offers access without equal participation. B directly welcomes interested students and offers a partner choice, which can help new members enter as participants. The wording supports inclusion, but cannot itself guarantee that the club is accessible.

Worked example 2: an apparently shared value

At a school consultation, a speaker says: “Of course, every caring family can attend our 3 pm meeting. You agree that real supporters will be there.”

  1. “Of course” treats agreement as obvious. “Every caring family” links attendance with caring.
  2. The context matters: 3 pm can conflict with work, travel or caring responsibilities. Absence would not show that a family does not care.
  3. “You agree” recruits the listener into the assumption; “real supporters” marginalises families who cannot attend.

Justified revision: “We value every family’s views. Join at 3 pm, send comments beforehand or request another way to contribute.” This retains the aim of consultation while separating a family’s commitment from its availability.

Questions and answers
  1. Does “we” always include everyone? No. Identify its referent. In example 1 it names an established group; in “we value every family’s views” it names the organisers.
  2. Can “you” empower and pressure? Yes. “You can choose your partner” grants choice; “you agree” presumes consent.
  3. How do cultural values enter the argument? A speaker may appeal to belonging, loyalty or care, but must not assume every audience member shares the same experiences or expresses care in the same way.
Common mistakes and quick fixes
  • Inclusive wording fixes every barrier. It helps, but access, timing and decision-making also matter.
  • One label is universally preferred. Respect a person’s stated preference and the context; do not infer preferences from a group label.
  • Intent proves effect. A welcoming intention does not establish how every reader responds. Use a qualified, evidenced explanation.
  • Disagreement means the reader is wrong. Compare plausible readings against the actual wording and situation.
Guided practice

A student council invites all year levels to propose a playground improvement.

“Obviously, sensible students want a quiet playground. We will hear from the older students first; the younger ones can agree afterwards.”

Try: identify one presumed value, one unequal role and one revision that retains a fair consultation.

Answer and explanation

“Sensible” links quietness with good judgement, leaving active-play preferences looking unreasonable. Older students get the first speaking role, while younger students are offered agreement rather than proposals. A defensible revision is: “Students may prefer quiet, active or mixed spaces. Each year group can propose an option, and we will compare the benefits and constraints.” This offers a voice without promising every request can be adopted.

Independent practice

A library wants to recruit volunteers. Analyse this original notice, then revise it for the stated purpose.

“Our real locals already know the rules. Outsiders should ask a local before suggesting changes.”

Task: explain how two wording choices allocate belonging or authority. Write an alternative that makes the rules clear and allows newcomers to contribute.

Answer and explanation

“Real locals” treats long-standing members as more authentic. “Outsiders” labels newcomers as separate, and requiring permission before suggesting changes gives established members control. One alternative is: “All volunteers can read our shared guidelines and suggest improvements. Ask the coordinator if a rule needs explaining.” It retains a clear contact and rules without using local identity as a condition for having a voice. Other revisions can pass if their effects are explained.

Reasoning and interpretation

“You may choose which role you take, but only existing members may lead a group.”

Evaluate: does this sentence both empower and restrict?

Answer and explanation

Yes. It offers a choice of role but reserves leadership for existing members. Whether that restriction is justified needs context, such as relevant training or a genuine safety requirement. Inclusion does not mean that all restrictions are automatically unfair; assess their reason and practical effect.

Assessment-style practice

New task: a community garden wants everyone affected by opening hours to have input.

“As everyone knows, committed gardeners arrive before breakfast. We need your support for a 6 am opening; latecomers can read the decision later.”

Write a paragraph analysing the appeal to shared knowledge, the label for non-attenders and the difference between supporting and deciding. Propose one revision.

Answer and explanation

“As everyone knows” presents an early start as common knowledge. “Committed” associates dedication with a schedule, while “latecomers” can diminish people unable to attend. Asking for support and offering only a later decision notice limits their influence. A stronger invitation would ask which opening hours work and accept written suggestions before deciding. A complete answer uses exact evidence, explains the effect in this context and justifies the revision.

Exit ticket

A coach says to a new player: “Tell me which position you would like to practise; we will discuss what support you need.”

Exit ticket: identify two choices that give the player agency and one thing the sentence cannot establish.

Answer and explanation

“Tell me which position” invites the player’s preference, and “discuss” makes support a conversation rather than a decision made without them. “We” can include player and coach here. The sentence alone cannot establish that the requested support will actually be available.

Support, core and extend
  • Support: underline the group labels first; draw a two-column list of who can speak and decide.
  • Core: connect two exact choices to a supported social effect, then justify a revision.
  • Extend: defend a reasonable participation restriction while improving the invitation. Evaluate a plausible alternative reading.

Teacher check: accept more than one reading when it names the audience, explains a mechanism and uses evidence. Discuss fictional situations rather than requiring personal accounts of exclusion.
