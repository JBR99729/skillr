Inclusive, Exclusive and Empowering Language

Language choices can invite belonging, mark outsiders, distribute authority and shape how people or groups are valued.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA01: understand how language can have inclusive and exclusive social effects, and can empower or disempower people.

Language choices can invite belonging, mark outsiders, distribute authority and shape how people or groups are valued.

Analysis and writing toolkit

Inclusion

Pronouns such as “we” and “our”, shared labels and respectful naming can build belonging.

Exclusion

Labels, stereotypes, gatekeeping terms and insider language can position people outside a group.

Power

Commands, institutional vocabulary, naming rights and who gets quoted can empower or silence.

Analysis move

Identify the choice → name who it positions → explain the social effect → judge the effect in context.

10 important examples

1. Inclusive pronoun

“We can redesign the space together.” The inclusive “we” constructs shared responsibility rather than separating decision-makers from users.

2. Exclusive label

“Real fans understand this.” “Real” creates an in-group and implies that disagreement makes someone less legitimate.

3. Euphemism

Calling job losses “workforce optimisation” softens human impact and can protect institutional authority.

4. Derogatory label

A dismissive nickname reduces a person to a stereotype; the effect is not just description but social positioning.

5. Humour

A joke that depends on a disability stereotype may bond one group while marginalising the targeted group.

6. Gatekeeping jargon

“Only accredited insiders can comment” uses status language to restrict who is treated as credible.

7. Empowering naming

Using a community’s preferred name respects self-identification and returns authority over representation.

8. Passive exclusion

“Mistakes were made” removes the actor and can reduce accountability.

9. Appeal to belonging

“As members of this school community…” frames cooperation as part of a shared identity.

10. Context matters

The same slang can signal solidarity among friends but exclusion when used to mock an unfamiliar speaker.

Common mistakes
  • Treating inclusive language as automatically positive; inclusion can also pressure people to conform.
  • Naming a device without explaining who is empowered, excluded or positioned.
  • Assuming intention and effect are identical; a speaker may intend humour but produce marginalisation.
  • Discussing a single word without considering speaker, audience, setting and power relationship.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Explain how “We all know what sensible people choose” can both include and exclude.
  2. Rewrite “the disabled” in a more respectful form and explain the change.
  3. How can specialist jargon create power in a workplace meeting?
  4. Explain one way humour can build belonging and one way it can marginalise.
  5. Why might “Mistakes were made” reduce accountability?
  6. Analyse the social effect of calling dissenters “outsiders”.
  7. Give an example of language that empowers a previously marginalised speaker.
  8. Why does context matter when evaluating slang?
  9. Compare “You must comply” with “Could we agree on…” in terms of power.
  10. Write a two-sentence judgement on whether inclusive pronouns are always genuinely inclusive.
Indicative responses with explanations

English analysis can support more than one interpretation. These responses model the quality of reasoning expected; students should support alternatives with evidence.

  1. 1. The sentence includes listeners inside “we all” only if they accept the speaker’s judgement; “sensible people” excludes dissenters by implying they are irrational.
  2. 2. Use a context-appropriate form such as “people with disability” or the terminology preferred by the relevant community; the rewrite foregrounds people rather than reducing them to a category.
  3. 3. Jargon can establish expertise and control access to the discussion; people unfamiliar with it may be treated as less credible even when they have relevant knowledge.
  4. 4. Shared humour can mark common experience, but humour based on stereotypes can create solidarity by making another group the target.
  5. 5. The passive construction omits the person responsible, shifting focus from the actor to the event.
  6. 6. “Outsiders” constructs dissent as a problem of belonging rather than evidence, weakening dissenters’ social legitimacy.
  7. 7. Example: “The residents will present their own findings first.” The wording gives the affected group authority to frame the issue.
  8. 8. Slang can be affectionate within an in-group but hostile or patronising across unequal power relationships.
  9. 9. “You must comply” is direct and hierarchical; “Could we agree on…” presents the decision as collaborative, though the actual power relationship still matters.
  10. 10. No. Inclusive pronouns can create genuine solidarity, but they can also manufacture consensus by implying that disagreement falls outside the group.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA01: understand how language can have inclusive and exclusive social effects, and can empower or disempower people.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying language that seeks to align the listener or reader; for example, “of course”, “obviously”, “as you can imagine”

In practice: Mark the words that presume agreement or shared knowledge, then identify who is invited into that assumed community and who may be left outside it.

E2

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying the use of first person (“I”, “we”) and second person (“you”) pronouns to distance or involve the audience

In practice: Compare first- and second-person choices in two short versions of the same message and explain how audience distance changes.

E3

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying language used to reference or indicate shared assumptions

In practice: Mark the words that presume agreement or shared knowledge, then identify who is invited into that assumed community and who may be left outside it.

E4

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying language that appeals to shared cultural knowledge, values and beliefs

In practice: Mark the words that presume agreement or shared knowledge, then identify who is invited into that assumed community and who may be left outside it.

E5

Australian Curriculum elaboration: identifying examples of language that are inclusive or marginalising

In practice: Test the wording against speaker, audience and power: identify whose identity or authority is strengthened and whose is reduced.
