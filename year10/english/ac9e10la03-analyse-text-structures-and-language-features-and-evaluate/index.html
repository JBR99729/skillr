Text Structure, Language Features and Purpose

Effective texts organise information and choose language features to guide an audience toward a specific purpose; evaluation asks how well those choices work.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesWorksheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

AC9E10LA03: analyse text structures and language features and evaluate their effectiveness in achieving their purpose.

Analysis toolkit

Structure

Opening, sequencing, paragraphing, headings, evidence placement and ending.

Language

Register, sentence pattern, modality, evaluation, figurative language and cohesion.

Purpose

Inform, explain, persuade, argue, reflect, entertain or combine purposes.

Evaluation move

Feature → effect → audience/purpose → judgement using specific evidence.

10 important examples

1. Persuasive opening

Starting with a concrete consequence before statistics gives the issue human urgency, then evidence supports the claim.

2. Explainer structure

Definition → mechanism → example → limitation helps a novice move from concept to application.

3. Debate language

“The evidence suggests” is more measured than “everyone knows”, strengthening credibility in formal argument.

4. Subjective register

“An outrageous waste” creates strong alignment but may weaken perceived objectivity.

5. Print to online

An online version can add navigation, hyperlinks and embedded media; effectiveness depends on whether these features support rather than fragment the purpose.

6. Sentence variation

A long cumulative sentence can build complexity; a short final sentence can deliver emphasis.

7. Review structure

A review that states criteria, gives evidence and then judges is easier to trust than one built only from unsupported praise.

8. Counterargument

Placing a counterargument before rebuttal can make persuasion appear fairer and more reasoned.

9. Multimodal combination

Graphics show spatial relationships, narration explains causation and sound establishes tone; each mode should add rather than duplicate.

10. Comparative evaluation

Two texts may share a purpose, but the one matching register, evidence and navigation to its audience can be judged more effective.

Common mistakes
  • Describing structure without connecting it to purpose.
  • Calling any persuasive device “effective” without judging audience/context.
  • Treating sentence-level features as isolated from paragraph and whole-text organisation.
  • Comparing two texts by listing differences instead of evaluating consequences.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Why might a persuasive article open with an anecdote before data?
  2. Explain how headings support an informational purpose.
  3. Compare “must” and “may” in an argument.
  4. How can paragraph order shape persuasion?
  5. Why can a short sentence be effective after a long sentence?
  6. Give one reason a counterargument may increase credibility.
  7. How can moving a print text online improve or weaken it?
  8. What makes a judgement evaluative rather than descriptive?
  9. Compare objective and emotional register for a public-health explainer.
  10. Explain how graphics, text and sound can work together without duplication.
Indicative responses
  1. The anecdote creates immediate human relevance; later data broadens the claim.
  2. Headings chunk information, preview content and help readers locate answers.
  3. “Must” presents obligation; “may” leaves possibility. Fit depends on evidence and purpose.
  4. Claim/evidence order can create logical momentum; counterargument/rebuttal can signal fairness.
  5. Contrast in length makes the short statement feel decisive.
  6. It demonstrates awareness of competing views before rebuttal.
  7. Hyperlinks and media can improve navigation and explanation, but excessive choices can fragment reading.
  8. It makes a supported judgement about how the feature serves audience and purpose.
  9. Objective register may build trust; emotional register may motivate but can appear manipulative if evidence is weak.
  10. Each mode should contribute distinct information: a graph shows pattern, narration explains, sound shapes tone.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9E10LA03: analyse text structures and language features and evaluate their effectiveness in achieving their purpose.

E1

Australian Curriculum elaboration: comparing the text structure and language features of a print text transposed to an online environment and evaluating the effectiveness of the online text

In practice: Compare the print and online versions feature by feature—navigation, sequencing, headings, hyperlinks, visuals—and judge which better serves the audience and purpose.

E2

Australian Curriculum elaboration: analysing the text structures and language features of reviews from different sources, and evaluating their effectiveness

In practice: Compare two reviews of the same invented product or performance, focusing on structure, evidence, evaluative vocabulary and target audience.

E3

Australian Curriculum elaboration: analysing and experimenting with combinations of graphics, text and sound in the production of multimodal texts such as documentaries, media reports, online magazines and digital books to influence audience responses

In practice: Plan a 30-second multimodal explanation and justify what each mode contributes instead of duplicating the same information.
