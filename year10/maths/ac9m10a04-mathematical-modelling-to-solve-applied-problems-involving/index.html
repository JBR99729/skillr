Growth, Decay and Financial Modelling

Choose a model from the pattern of change, calculate and interpret its predictions, then test whether its assumptions remain sensible in the real situation.

What you need to know

Linear models have constant first differences, quadratic models have constant second differences, and exponential models have a constant ratio. Modelling also requires assumptions, interpretation, checking and revision; calculating a number is only one part of the task.

Modelling cheat sheet

Choose a model

Constant difference → linear.

Constant second difference → quadratic.

Constant ratio/percentage → exponential.

Growth/decay

A=P(1+r)ᵗ for growth.

A=P(1−r)ᵗ for decay.

Model cycle

Formulate → choose variables/model → calculate → interpret → evaluate assumptions → modify/report.

Validity

Ask: What range is realistic? Which quantities are assumed constant? What data could challenge the model?

10 important worked examples

1. Choose linear

Outputs 12,17,22,27 have constant difference +5, so a linear model is appropriate.

2. Choose quadratic

Outputs 2,7,16,29 have first differences 5,9,13 and constant second difference 4, indicating a quadratic model.

3. Choose exponential

Outputs 80,96,115.2,138.24 multiply by 1.2 each step, so the model is exponential growth at 20%.

4. Compound interest

$5000 at 4% p.a. compounded annually for 3 years: A=5000(1.04)³≈$5624.32.

5. Exponential decay

A $24,000 asset loses 15% each year. After 2 years: 24000(0.85)²=$17,340.

6. Doubling time by table

P=300(1.12)ᵗ. At t=6, P≈592; at t=7, P≈663. The population first doubles past 600 between 6 and 7 years.

7. Half-life model

If a quantity halves every 10 years, Q=Q₀(1/2)^(t/10). After 30 years, Q=Q₀/8.

8. Compare linear and exponential

Start at 100. Linear +20 per year gives 200 after 5 years. Exponential +20% gives 100(1.2)⁵≈248.83. Same initial change, very different long-term behaviour.

9. Carbon dating context

Radioactive decay is modelled exponentially because a constant proportion decays over equal time intervals. The model can estimate age from the remaining proportion, subject to scientific assumptions and measurement uncertainty.

10. Critique population growth

P=P₀(1.08)ᵗ assumes 8% growth indefinitely. Food, habitat, disease and management actions can change the rate, so extrapolating far beyond observed data is risky.

Common mistakes
  • Choosing exponential just because a graph curves.
  • Using 6% as multiplier 0.06 instead of 1.06 for growth.
  • Ignoring units/time step.
  • Giving a prediction without interpreting it in context.
  • Assuming a model remains valid indefinitely.

  4. Find $4000 at 5% compound interest for 2 years.

  6. Find when 200(1.15)ᵗ first exceeds 400 using a table/calculator.

  9. State one assumption in a compound-interest model.
  10. State one reason an animal-population exponential model may fail long-term.

  2. Quadratic. First differences 9,15,21; second differences 6.
  3. Exponential. Ratio 1.2.
  4. $4410.
  5. 800(0.88)³≈$545.18 in the relevant units.

Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9M10A04: use mathematical modelling to solve applied problems involving growth and decay, including financial contexts; formulate problems, choose linear, quadratic or exponential models, interpret solutions, evaluate/modify models and report assumptions, methods and findings.

  • E1: choose between linear, quadratic and exponential models from first differences, second differences and constant ratios. Examples 1–3.
  • E2: contrast exponential growth/decay with linear change; determine doubling time, half-life and ranges. Examples 5–8.
  • E3: use authentic financial information and interest rates for compound-interest problems. Example 4.
  • E4: investigate exponential equations in carbon dating in relation to First Nations Australians’ artefacts or material culture. Example 9 states the mathematical mechanism; classroom use should be culturally respectful and source-specific.
  • E5: formulate population-growth models for native animals on Country/Place and critique applicability. Example 10.
