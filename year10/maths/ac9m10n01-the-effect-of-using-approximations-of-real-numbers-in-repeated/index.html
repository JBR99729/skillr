Exact Surds and Decimal Approximations

Exact surds preserve value through repeated calculations; simplify and combine them before making a final decimal approximation.

Success criteria

  • Explain keep surds exact and apply valid root rules using the approved model.

Concept boundary and prerequisites

Must teach

  • recognise the effect of using approximations of real numbers in repeated calculations and compare the results when using exact representations

Prerequisites

May support informally

  • Digital tools may test, visualise and verify after the mathematical structure is explicit.

Do not overteach

  • Do not introduce later-year formalism that is unnecessary for this content description.
Teaching progression

This progression makes the Year 10 number relationship visible before independent application.

  1. 1. Keep surds exact and apply valid root rules
    Compare an exact-surds calculation with one using √2 ≈ 1.414 and √3 ≈ 1.732, then test the false addition rule using 9 and 16.
    Students: Compares exact and approximate pathways, identifies the first rounded step and explains the counterexample.
  2. 2. Simplify first, collect like surds and keep coefficients exact
    Model √45 = 3√5, √20 = 2√5 and √80 = 4√5, then align coefficients. Repeat with √12, √27 and √75 using a common denominator of 30.
    Students: Rewrites entire surds, collects only matching radicands and retains fractional coefficients exactly.

Australian Curriculum elaborations

E1: Keep surds exact and apply valid root rules

Curriculum: comparing and contrasting the effect of truncation or rounding on the final result of calculations when using approximations of real numbers rather than exact representations

Exact surd notation preserves value through repeated calculations; early decimal rounding introduces error that later operations can compound.

Teacher: Compare an exact-surds calculation with one using √2 ≈ 1.414 and √3 ≈ 1.732, then test the false addition rule using 9 and 16.

Ask: Where does rounding error enter, and what counterexample disproves distribution over addition?

Look for: States that exact notation avoids premature rounding and shows √(9 + 16) = 5 whereas √9 + √16 = 7.

E2: Simplify first, collect like surds and keep coefficients exact

Curriculum: investigating the impact of approximation on multiple calculations in contexts that involve the area of compound shapes involving circles, the surface area and volume of compound objects, and repeated calculations of simple interest where the solutions are not exact cents

Use perfect-square factors to rewrite entire surds as like surds, then apply the distributive law to their coefficients.

Teacher: Model √45 = 3√5, √20 = 2√5 and √80 = 4√5, then align coefficients. Repeat with √12, √27 and √75 using a common denominator of 30.

Ask: Which perfect-square factor should be extracted, which terms are now like surds, and what coefficient remains?

Look for: Obtains 5√5 in the first example and b = 11/30 in the fractional example with each transformation shown.

Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Exact and approximate values are treated as interchangeable — Keep exact notation until the requested approximation step.
  • The exact surds and decimal approximations model is copied without interpretation — Connect every label in ‘Keep √2 and √3 exact until the final requested approximation; √(9 + 16) = 5, but √9 + √16 = 7.’ to its mathematical role.
  • The result is not verified — Use a second representation, boundary case, inverse operation or simulation check.
Support, core and extend

Support: Reduce numerical or representational load while retaining the same decision and explanation.

Core: Connect at least two representations, justify the method and verify independently.

Extend: Test a boundary case, counterexample or model limitation without introducing later-year procedures.

Quick check and mastery evidence

Prompt: Why is √(a + b) = √a + √b not a valid general rule, and when should a decimal approximation be introduced?

Expected idea: Square roots do not distribute over addition: one counterexample is √25 = 5 while √9 + √16 = 7. Retain exact surds during working and approximate only when the context requests a decimal result.

Students should explain the relationship in their own words, apply it in a new case and verify the result using another representation, estimate or check.
