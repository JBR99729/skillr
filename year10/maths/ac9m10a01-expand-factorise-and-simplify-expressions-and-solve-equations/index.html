AC9M10A01 • Year 10 Maths • Algebra

Expanding, Factorising, Exponent Laws and Algebraic Equations

Equivalent algebraic forms look different but have the same value. Learn to move accurately between expanded, factorised and completed-square forms, simplify powers, and solve equations while preserving equivalence.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What this Year 10 algebra skill means

Algebraic manipulation is about equivalence: changing the form of an expression or equation without changing its mathematical value. Expansion removes grouping symbols by distributing multiplication. Factorisation reverses expansion by exposing common factors or factors of a quadratic. Completing the square rewrites a quadratic to reveal its structure. Exponent laws simplify repeated multiplication and division of the same base.

Success criteria

  • Expand and factorise algebraic expressions accurately, including quadratics.
  • Explain how expansion and factorisation are inverse processes and recognise equivalent forms.
  • Rewrite a quadratic in completed-square form where appropriate.
  • Apply product, quotient and power exponent laws, including zero and negative integer exponents.
  • Solve algebraic equations by performing equivalent operations and check solutions by substitution.
Prerequisites and concept boundary

Prerequisites: collecting like terms, distributive law, simple factorisation, integer arithmetic, substitution and basic exponent notation.

Core Year 10 idea: every manipulation must preserve equivalence. For equations, an operation applied to one side must be applied consistently so the solution set is unchanged.

Do not confuse: simplifying an expression with solving an equation. An expression has no value to “solve for” until values or an equation are supplied.

Teaching progression
  1. Connect expansion and factorisation. Start with (x+3)(x+5), expand to x²+8x+15, then reverse the process. Verify equivalence by substituting a value such as x=2 into both forms.
  2. Factorise quadratics deliberately. For x²+7x+12, find two numbers whose product is 12 and sum is 7: 3 and 4, so x²+7x+12=(x+3)(x+4).
  3. Introduce completed-square form as another equivalent representation. Rewrite x²+6x+5 as (x+3)²−4 and expand it back to verify.
  4. Apply exponent laws to algebraic terms. Keep the same-base condition visible and distinguish multiplication, division and powers of powers.
  5. Solve and verify equations. Simplify first where useful, isolate the variable using equivalent operations, then substitute the solution into the original equation.
Worked examples

1. Expand and simplify

Question: Expand and simplify 3(2x−5)−2(x+4).

Distribute each multiplier: 6x−15−2x−8.

Collect like terms: 4x−23.

Check: if x=5, the original expression gives 3(5)−2(9)=−3 and 4(5)−23=−3.

2. Factorise a quadratic

Question: Factorise x²−x−12.

We need two numbers with product −12 and sum −1. They are −4 and 3.

Therefore x²−x−12=(x−4)(x+3).

Expanding the factors gives x²+3x−4x−12=x²−x−12, confirming the factorisation.

3. Complete the square

Question: Rewrite x²+8x+7 in completed-square form.

Half the coefficient of x: 8÷2=4. Add and subtract 4²=16:

x²+8x+7 = x²+8x+16−16+7 = (x+4)²−9.

This form makes the square structure visible while remaining exactly equivalent to the original quadratic.

4. Apply exponent laws and solve

Simplify: (6x⁵y⁻²)/(3x²y) = 2x³y⁻³ = 2x³/y³, for y≠0.

Solve: 2(x−3)+5=3x−4 → 2x−6+5=3x−4 → 2x−1=3x−4 → 3=x.

Check: x=3 gives 5 on both sides of the original equation.

Why the methods work
  • Distributive law: a(b+c)=ab+ac, so every term inside a bracket must be multiplied.
  • Factorisation: reverses distribution; expanding the factors is a reliable check.
  • Exponent laws: follow from counting repeated factors. For the same non-zero base a, aᵐ/aⁿ=aᵐ⁻ⁿ; a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ.
  • Equation solving: equivalent operations preserve equality. Substitution into the original equation verifies the proposed solution.
Common misconceptions and quick fixes
  • Distributing to only the first term: 3(x+4) is 3x+12, not 3x+4. Mark both multiplication links before simplifying.
  • Combining unlike terms: x² and x are not like terms. Terms combine only when their variable parts, including exponents, match.
  • Adding exponents during addition: x²+x²=2x², not x⁴. Exponents add when multiplying the same base: x²·x²=x⁴.
  • Negative exponent means negative value: x⁻³ means 1/x³, not −x³.
  • Factor pair has the right product but wrong sum: always expand the proposed factors to check the middle term.
Choose a strategy

Expand when you need to collect terms or compare coefficients. Factorise when a product form exposes zeros or common structure. Complete the square when the square structure of a quadratic is useful. Simplify with exponent laws before solving when powers obscure the equation. Whatever form you choose, verify that it remains equivalent.

Australian Curriculum coverage

Content description: expand, factorise and simplify expressions and solve equations algebraically, applying exponent laws involving products, quotients and powers of variables, and the distributive property.

  • E1: Connect factorisation and expansion, including completed-square form for quadratic expressions.
  • E2: Apply exponent laws to algebraic terms, including positive and negative integral exponents, when simplifying expressions and solving equations.
Exit ticket: check for mastery
  1. Expand and simplify 4(2x−3)−(x+5).
  2. Factorise x²+2x−15.
  3. Rewrite x²−10x+21 in completed-square form.
  4. Simplify (12a⁶b⁻¹)/(3a²b²) using positive exponents.

Answers: 1. 7x−17. 2. (x+5)(x−3). 3. (x−5)²−4. 4. 4a⁴/b³, b≠0.
