Simultaneous Linear Equations and Inequalities

Solve two linear conditions at once, interpret intersections in context, and represent inequalities as intervals or regions with justified boundaries.

Topic GuideTeacher SlidesPractice SheetPracticeTest
What you need to know

A solution to a pair of simultaneous equations is an ordered pair that satisfies both equations. Graphically it is the intersection point. Algebraically it can be found by substitution or elimination. An inequality describes a set of values rather than one value; in 2 variables its solution is usually a region.

Cheat sheet

Simultaneous equations

Substitution: make one variable the subject and substitute.

Elimination: add/subtract equations to remove one variable.

Graph: intersection = common solution.

Inequalities

< or > → boundary not included.

≤ or ≥ → boundary included.

Multiply/divide by a negative → reverse the inequality sign.

Region test

Draw the boundary line, choose a test point not on it, substitute, then shade the side that satisfies the inequality.

Context check

Ask whether negative, fractional or non-integer values make sense in the situation.

10 important worked examples

1. Elimination

x+y=11 and x−y=3. Add equations: 2x=14, so x=7. Then y=4.

Solution: (7,4).

2. Substitution

y=2x+1 and x+y=10.

x+(2x+1)=10 → 3x=9 → x=3; y=7. Solution: (3,7).

3. Break-even point

Plan A costs C=20+4n; Plan B costs C=50+2n.

20+4n=50+2n → 2n=30 → n=15. Both cost $80. The intersection means break-even at 15 uses.

4. Solve a linear inequality

3x−5≤16 → 3x≤21 → x≤7.

5. Reverse the sign

−2x>8. Divide by −2 and reverse the inequality: x<−4.

6. Shade a region

For 2x+3y<24, draw 2x+3y=24 as a dashed line. Test (0,0): 0<24 is true, so shade the side containing the origin.

7. Budget integer solutions

Movies cost $12 and skating $21 with budget ≤$150: 12m+21s≤150.

If s=4, then 12m+84≤150 → m≤5.5, so integer m values are 0–5.

8. Test a point

Does (3,5) satisfy 2y<x²? Left side=10; right side=9. Since 10<9 is false, the point is not a solution.

9. Circle-area inequality

Find r when πr²>100. Divide by π: r²>100/π. For positive radius, r>√(100/π)≈5.64.

10. Interpret the ordered pair

If x=adults and y=students, a solution (24,18) must be stated as “24 adult tickets and 18 student tickets”, not merely “(24,18)”.

Common mistakes
  • Solving only one of the two equations and not checking the other.
  • Forgetting to reverse an inequality when dividing by a negative.
  • Using a solid boundary for < or > instead of a dashed boundary.
  • Shading a region without testing a point.
  • Reporting an ordered pair without interpreting what each coordinate means.
10 important questions to solve
  1. Solve x+y=9 and x−y=1.
  2. Solve y=3x−2 and x+y=14.
  3. Find the break-even point for C=30+5n and C=70+3n.
  4. Solve 4x+7>31.
  5. Solve −3x≤12.
  6. Describe the boundary and test point for x+2y<8.
  7. Does (2,4) satisfy 3x+y≤10?
  8. For 15a+20b≤120, find the maximum integer a when b=3.
  9. Find positive r satisfying πr²≥64.
  10. Explain what a graph intersection represents for two competing price plans.
Solutions with explanations
  1. (5,4). Add equations to get 2x=10.
  2. (4,10). x+3x−2=14.
  3. n=20, cost $130.
  4. x>6.
  5. x≥−4. Reverse the sign when dividing by −3.
  6. Dashed line x+2y=8; (0,0) satisfies the inequality, so shade the origin side.
  7. Yes. 3(2)+4=10≤10.
  8. 45+15a≤120 → a≤5, so 5.
  9. r≥8/√π≈4.51.
  10. The intersection is the input where both plans have the same output/cost.
Australian Curriculum v9.0 and elaborations

AC9M10A02: solve linear inequalities and simultaneous linear equations in 2 variables; interpret solutions graphically and communicate solutions in terms of the situation.

  • E1: investigate linear equations in context, including quotes or profit/loss; solve graphically and express the intersection as a break-even or change-provider point. Example 3.
  • E2: describe simultaneous-equation solutions in context. Example 10.
  • E3: graph regions for inequalities such as 2x+3y<24 and verify with a test point. Example 6.
  • E4: identify integer combinations under a budget such as 12m+21s≤150. Example 7.
  • E5: test whether points satisfy inequalities and solve applied inequalities such as circle-area thresholds. Examples 8–9.
  • E6: investigate strategies in First Nations Australian instructive games such as Weme from Warlpiri Peoples and connections to reasoning with two-variable simultaneous conditions. This context should be taught respectfully and with an appropriate source rather than reduced to a token example.
